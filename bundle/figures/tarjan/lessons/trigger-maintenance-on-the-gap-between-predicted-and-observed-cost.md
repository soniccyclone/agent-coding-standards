# Trigger maintenance on the gap between predicted and observed cost, and reject any tuning rule that needs the answer up front

**Lesson:** Restructuring on every single access is the obvious cost of a self-adjusting structure, and the obvious way to reduce it is to restructure less often. The interesting question is what the condition should be. The paper's answer is to compare what an operation actually cost against what the cost analysis says it should have cost, and to restructure only when the actual exceeds the predicted by more than a chosen margin. The consequence is sharp: total restructuring work becomes independent of how many operations you perform. It is bounded by the initial disorder alone, because each triggered restructuring is provably paying for itself out of a fixed budget that only the starting configuration funds. So a maintenance policy gated on a cost overrun is self-limiting in a way that a policy gated on a count or a timer never is — nothing accumulates, because work is only done when a measurement proves work is owed.

The generalizable shape is to look for a quantity your analysis already predicts, measure the same quantity at runtime, and let the discrepancy drive the maintenance. Almost any system with a performance model has such a pair available and does not use it: an index that rebuilds on a row-count threshold could instead rebuild when observed selectivity diverges from the estimate; a rebalancing pass on a schedule could instead run when an operation's measured cost exceeds its budget; a cache that re-sorts periodically could re-sort when hit cost drifts above what the current ordering should deliver. The tell that you have picked the wrong trigger is that your maintenance work grows with traffic rather than with disorder. The tell that you have picked the right one is that you can state the total maintenance work as a function of the initial state.

Then the paper turns on its own construction, and this is the more valuable half. Making the comparison requires knowing the predicted cost, which requires the weights the analysis uses — and those must be supplied in advance from assumed properties of the workload. The authors call the method antithetical to the philosophy of self-adjustment, and they are right: a mechanism whose whole purpose is to discover the access pattern without being told it has been made to depend on being told it. There is also a mechanical cost, since deciding whether to restructure means traversing the path once to measure and again to act. Both objections generalize into a test worth applying to any adaptive component: does its tuning rule require a parameter that the component was supposed to infer? If yes, the adaptivity has been relocated onto whoever configures it rather than eliminated, and you have built a manually tuned system with extra steps — no worse than an honest one, but you should stop calling it self-adjusting and start documenting the parameter.

**Source:** [Self-Adjusting Binary Search Trees](../works/self-adjusting-binary-search-trees.md) — the semi-adjusting section's first form of conditional restructuring, which classifies an access path as long or short by comparing its depth against a constant multiple of the amortized bound predicted by the access lemma and restructures only on long paths, the theorem showing that the total restructuring time is then proportional to the cost of accessing each item once and therefore independent of the number of accesses, and the two stated drawbacks that the method requires two passes over each long path and that the decision requires knowing item weights predetermined from assumed properties of the access sequence, which the authors call antithetical to the philosophy of self-adjustment.
