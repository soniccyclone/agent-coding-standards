# Decide a property by trying to build the thing, not by searching for the forbidden shape

**Lesson:** The property this paper decides has a famous mathematical characterization: an object has it exactly when it contains no substructure of two specific forbidden kinds. That characterization is a complete answer to the mathematical question and, the authors say plainly, useless as a procedure — searching for an embedded copy of a forbidden pattern is a subgraph-matching problem whose cost is superlinear at best and probably far worse. So they abandon it. The approach that works is to attempt to build the thing whose existence the property asserts, incrementally, and let the attempt itself decide: if the construction completes, the property holds by exhibition; if the construction gets stuck, the property fails. The theorems that license the second half — that getting stuck at a particular step really means no completion exists rather than merely that this attempt was unlucky — are where the paper's mathematical work goes.

The distinction is between a characterization and a decision procedure, and it comes up constantly outside graph theory. A negative characterization tells you the property is equivalent to the absence of some pattern, which invites you to go looking for the pattern; the space of places the pattern could hide is usually enormous, and every one must be ruled out before you may answer yes. A constructive procedure inverts the quantifier: instead of checking that no bad configuration exists anywhere, it builds one good configuration step by step, and each step is a local decision over a small set of options. The cost changes from a search over an exponential or high-degree space to a walk over the object's own size. The obligation you take on is proving that local failure implies global impossibility, which usually means proving that at each step the choices you did not take could not have helped — and that obligation is what the paper's central lemma discharges by showing a blocked step corresponds to a genuine topological conflict.

The habit worth building is to notice when you are checking for the absence of something and ask whether you could be constructing its presence instead. Type checking that hunts for inconsistencies versus inference that builds a substitution; a scheduler that verifies no deadline is violated versus one that constructs a schedule; a linker that scans for conflicts versus one that assigns addresses and reports the first that cannot be satisfied. In each case the constructive form is not merely faster but produces a witness for free, which the absence-checking form never does. And when the absence-checking form is all you have, the reason is worth stating: either nobody has found the right incremental order in which choices become forced, or the local-failure-implies-global-failure theorem is genuinely false. Those are very different situations and it is worth knowing which one you are in.

**Source:** [Efficient Planarity Testing](../works/efficient-planarity-testing.md) — the previous-research section, which presents the forbidden-subgraph characterization of planar graphs, states that despite its elegance it is useless as a practical test because directly testing for such subgraphs may take time far worse than quadratic, and concludes that the best approach appears to be attempting to construct a representation of an embedding, with planarity established if the construction completes and non-planarity if it cannot; together with the lemma giving a necessary and sufficient condition for adding a path to a partial embedding, whose necessity half is what turns a stuck construction into a proof of impossibility.
