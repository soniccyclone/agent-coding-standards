# A heuristic cannot be simplified by inspection: two nearly identical rules can differ by an order of magnitude

**Lesson:** Three restructuring rules in this paper all do the same thing at a glance: after touching an item, rotate it upward until it reaches the top. Two of them were already known and both are catastrophic — sequences exist on which they cost time proportional to the whole structure per operation, indefinitely. The rule the paper introduces differs only in that the rotations are performed two at a time, with the pair's order chosen by whether the two steps went the same direction or opposite directions. That distinction, which reads as a fussy detail, is the entire difference between a rule that degrades linearly and one that is within a constant factor of the best possible. The mechanism behind the gap is that the paired rule roughly halves the depth of every item along the path it touched, not just the one item requested, and the authors note that getting this halving effect appears to require treating the two direction cases differently. Nothing about the code's shape reveals this. Only the accounting does.

The immediate practical consequence is that a heuristic's variants must be re-analyzed individually, and the paper demonstrates the discipline on itself. A colleague suggests dropping one of the two rotations in the opposite-direction case, which makes the code visibly shorter; the analysis is redone and the bound survives with its constant degraded by about five percent. A different variant that moves the touched item only partway up rather than all the way is analyzed separately and turns out to *improve* the constant while, the authors observe, adapting more slowly to changes in usage. So among three edits of comparable apparent size, one is fatal, one is nearly free, and one is an improvement on one axis and a regression on another. There is no way to sort them without doing the work, and the intuition that "smaller code implies similar behavior" has no purchase here at all.

The habit to carry is a reflex about which parts of a design are load-bearing in a way that inspection cannot detect. Anywhere behavior is governed by a rule tuned against an accounting argument — a cache eviction policy, a backoff schedule, a scheduler's preemption choice, a rebalancing trigger, a retry heuristic — the argument depends on details of the rule that look arbitrary, and simplifying the rule silently discards the argument. Two responses are appropriate. First, record next to such a rule what its guarantee is and what argument establishes it, so a later reader knows a proof rather than a preference is being edited. Second, when you do want to simplify, redo the argument rather than benchmarking the change: the workloads that separate a good rule from a fatal one are adversarial sequences, and they are exactly what your benchmark will not contain.

**Source:** [Self-Adjusting Binary Search Trees](../works/self-adjusting-binary-search-trees.md) — the discussion of the single-rotation and move-to-root heuristics with the cited result that both admit arbitrarily long access sequences costing time proportional to the structure size per access, the definition of the three-case splaying step and the observation that producing the depth-halving effect seems to require handling the same-direction and opposite-direction cases differently, the analysis of the suggested simplification that omits the second rotation in the opposite-direction case and holds the same lemma with the constant raised from three to a little over three, and the semi-adjusting section's variant that moves the accessed item only partway up, holds the lemma with the constant reduced to two, and is noted to adapt more slowly to changes in usage.
