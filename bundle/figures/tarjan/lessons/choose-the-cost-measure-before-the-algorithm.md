# Choose the cost measure before the algorithm, because the measure decides the answer

**Lesson:** Two heuristics for keeping a frequently-searched list in a useful order had been studied for years under average-case analysis, which assumes requests are drawn independently from a fixed probability distribution and asks for the expected cost per request. Under that measure, the cautious rule that nudges an accessed item one position forward comes out ahead of the aggressive rule that yanks it to the front. Practitioners consistently found the opposite. The resolution in Sleator and Tarjan's paper is not a better algorithm but a better yardstick: total cost over an arbitrary worst-case request sequence, averaged across the sequence rather than across a hypothesized distribution. Under that measure the aggressive rule is provably near-optimal and the cautious one is provably not, matching what the field had observed. When a well-formed theory ranks options in the reverse order from practice, the first suspect is the input model the theory assumed, not the practitioners' judgment.

What the sequence-level measure buys is stated precisely enough to be worth internalizing as a category. Average-case analysis over an assumed distribution is unrealistic because real request streams are correlated: consecutive operations on a data structure influence each other, which is exactly the regularity a distributional model discards. Pure worst-case-per-operation analysis is unrealistically pessimistic because it charges every operation as though the expensive case could recur without limit, when the structure's own state forbids that. Averaging over the sequence keeps the honesty of worst-case reasoning about adversarial inputs while letting cheap operations pay for expensive ones. The reason the aggressive rule wins under it is that the measure is sensitive to locality of reference, a property real workloads have and the distributional model cannot express.

The same theorem then supplies something more subtle: once you have an algorithm known to be within a constant of the best possible strategy, its cost stops being a fact about the algorithm and becomes a definition of how hard the workload was. Difficulty of a request can be read off as the amount of other traffic since that item was last touched, and the difficulty of the whole stream is the sum. This inverts the usual direction of evaluation. Rather than measuring an algorithm against a benchmark, you use the provably-near-optimal algorithm as the instrument that measures the benchmark, which is what makes it possible to say a workload has high locality without hand-waving. A programmer who absorbs this treats the choice of cost measure as the load-bearing design decision it is — asking what correlations the measure can see, whether it rewards the structure real inputs actually have, and whether an unflattering result is telling them about their code or about their assumptions.

**Source:** [Amortized Efficiency of List Update and Paging Rules](../works/amortized-efficiency-of-list-update-and-paging-rules.md) — the introduction's framing of amortization as more robust than average-case and more realistic than worst-case, the survey of prior distributional results that favored the transpose rule, the discussion following the first theorem where the algorithm's cost is turned into a complexity measure for the request sequence, and the closing remarks reaching the same conclusion about which measure is the realistic one.
