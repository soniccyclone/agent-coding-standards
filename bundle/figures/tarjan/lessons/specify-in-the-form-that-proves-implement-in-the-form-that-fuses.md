# Specify in the form that proves easily, implement in the form that fuses the passes, and keep the equivalence explicit

**Lesson:** The restructuring rule at the heart of this paper is defined as a second pass: walk down to find the item, then walk back up transforming as you go. That definition is what makes the accounting tractable, because each step of the upward walk is a local transformation whose effect on the cost function can be computed in isolation and the whole then summed. It is also a bad implementation. Walking back up requires the downward path to be available, which means either parent pointers in every node, an auxiliary stack, or destructively encoding the path in the structure as you descend. So the authors give a second formulation that performs the same transformation during the descent, maintaining three partial results that are stitched together at the end, and note that the second pass and its storage disappear entirely. The same move recurs in the paper's most elaborate structure, where the operation is defined as three separate upward passes for the purpose of analysis and then observed to be implementable as one pass with a small fixed lookahead.

The general principle is that the form in which you *state* a transformation and the form in which you *run* it are different artifacts optimized for different readers, and conflating them costs you one or the other. Stated as a sequence of independent phases, a transformation is easy to reason about, easy to prove things about, and easy to explain, because each phase has a precondition and a postcondition and nothing is entangled. Fused into a single traversal, it is faster and needs less memory, because the intermediate results that the phase boundary would have materialized are consumed as they are produced and can live in control state rather than in a data structure. The mistake is to pick one form and pay the other's cost — either shipping the phased version and eating the extra traversal and its storage, or writing only the fused version and thereby having no artifact anyone can reason about, including yourself in six months.

What makes the two-form discipline safe rather than dangerous is the obligation that comes with it: the equivalence has to be stated and argued, not assumed. The paper does this each time, asserting that the descending formulation transforms the structure exactly as the ascending one does and that the bound carries over with its constant unchanged, and doing the same for the fused version of the three-pass operation. That assertion is the seam where the proof about the clean form attaches to the behavior of the fast form, and if it is missing the proof is about a program nobody runs. The same paper also shows where the boundary of the technique lies: it notes that encapsulating each local transformation in a reusable primitive keeps auxiliary-field maintenance in one place but performs assignments the fused version can skip, so the two forms genuinely differ in cost and the honest move is to keep both and say which one is deployed.

**Source:** [Self-Adjusting Binary Search Trees](../works/self-adjusting-binary-search-trees.md) — the definition of splaying as a bottom-up second pass over the access path with its enumerated options for recovering that path, the top-down reformulation that performs the restructuring during the descent by maintaining left, middle, and right partial trees and assembling them at the end, the statement that the lemma holds for the top-down methods with the constant unchanged, the discussion of encapsulating restructuring in rotation procedures versus expanding them inline and keeping extra information in the control state, the corresponding top-down version of the partial-restructuring variant with its claim of equivalence to the bottom-up version, and the virtual-tree operation defined as three bottom-up passes with the remark that it can be carried out in a single pass using four-node lookahead.
