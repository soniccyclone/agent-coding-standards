# Get the bound first, then spend a separate revision buying the simplicity back

**Lesson:** The paper traces its own lineage without embarrassment. An earlier attempt by the same authors hit a bound with an extra logarithmic factor and is described by them as a complicated program. A subsequent attempt reached the target bound and appeared in a dissertation. What this paper presents is neither a new bound nor a new idea but a considerable simplification of that second attempt — same result, much less machinery — and the authors note separately that the version they actually implemented and measured was the complicated earlier one, and that implementing the simpler presentation would reduce both time and space further. So three rounds: get it working, get the bound, then get it simple, with the third round producing no improvement in the headline claim and being worth publishing anyway.

The sequencing is the lesson. Simplicity is not something you can usually pursue at the same time as a hard performance target, because you do not yet know which of your mechanisms are essential — the first version that hits a bound is complicated precisely because it was assembled while the question of what was necessary was still open. Once the bound exists, that question has an answer, and a deliberate simplification pass can remove the scaffolding, at which point the same result is teachable, provable, and cheaper to run. Treating that pass as optional polish is the mistake; it is where the reusable artifact is produced. Every later structure in this line of work builds on the simplified version, not on the dissertation program that first achieved the bound.

The paper also shows what goes wrong when a method's control structure is left informal, and it is a sharper failure than the usual inefficiency. The original published version of the underlying method was *wrong* — the recursion it described could fail to terminate — and the correction consisted of reformulating it iteratively with the processing order made explicit. The idea survived; the way it sequenced its own work did not. That is worth carrying as a specific suspicion: when a method is presented as "recurse on each piece," the part most likely to be broken is the argument that the pieces are strictly smaller in some order, and making the sequencing explicit is how you either fix or expose it. Two practical rules come out of this: schedule a simplification revision as its own piece of work with its own justification, and when adopting a published or inherited algorithm, check the ordering and termination argument before checking anything else.

**Source:** [Efficient Planarity Testing](../works/efficient-planarity-testing.md) — the previous-research section's account of the authors' own earlier variant achieving a bound with an extra logarithmic factor by means of a complicated program, their subsequent improved algorithm appearing in a dissertation, and the statement that the algorithm presented here is a considerable simplification of that one; the introduction's remark that the algorithm in the paper is much simpler than the one originally programmed; the experimental section's note that implementing the simpler presented algorithm would cut space and time requirements considerably; and the account of the original method containing an error whereby the proposed procedure could loop indefinitely, corrected by a later formulation that used iteration instead of recursion.
