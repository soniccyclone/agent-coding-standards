# State the claim you cannot prove precisely enough for someone else to attack it

**Lesson:** The paper proves that its structure matches several specific alternatives, and then says plainly that it believes something much stronger which it cannot prove: that no way of maintaining such a structure, including one designed with full knowledge of the exact sequence of requests in advance, can beat it by more than a constant factor. What matters is how the belief is written down. The competitor is given a precise cost model — it may traverse from the top at a cost equal to the depth reached, and may perform any number of local transformations anywhere between requests at unit cost each — so that "beat" has an unambiguous meaning and a counterexample would be recognizable as such. Two weaker beliefs are stated the same way, shown to follow from the strong one, and one narrow special case of one of them is proven outright. The authors also say where their confidence comes from: the analogous statement is known to be true for a much simpler self-organizing structure.

The discipline here is worth separating from the mathematics. An unproven belief about a system is not noise to be suppressed until it becomes a theorem; it is the most valuable thing you know that you cannot yet demonstrate, and its value depends entirely on being stated sharply. A vague version — "we think this is basically optimal" — cannot be attacked, cannot be refuted by a counterexample, and cannot be used by anyone else, so it decays into folklore and eventually into an assumption nobody remembers making. A sharp version, complete with the model under which it would be measured, is a target: someone can disprove it, someone can prove a special case, someone can derive consequences from it. The paper does all three of those things to its own conjectures within the same section.

For ordinary engineering the analogue is direct and mostly ignored. Systems accumulate beliefs of exactly this kind — this cache policy is near-optimal for our traffic, this queue never exceeds that depth, this retry schedule cannot amplify load, this component's cost is dominated by that one. Each is worth writing down with the same care: what precisely is being claimed, against what alternative, measured how, and what makes you believe it. Doing so has three payoffs. It separates what you have established from what you are assuming, which is the distinction that later failures always turn out to have blurred. It makes the assumption falsifiable, so evidence against it can actually register rather than being explained away. And it names the reasoning your confidence rests on, so a reader can evaluate whether that reasoning transfers — in this paper's case, whether an argument that worked for a linear structure should be expected to survive in a branching one, which is a question a reader can now ask because the authors said out loud that this was the basis of their faith.

**Source:** [Self-Adjusting Binary Search Trees](../works/self-adjusting-binary-search-trees.md) — the closing section's three formally stated conjectures, in particular the dynamic optimality conjecture with its explicit cost model for the competing algorithm covering both the traversal cost and a unit charge per local restructuring step performed between accesses, the statement that this conjecture implies the other two by way of an argument about the power of local restructuring that the authors say they are separately investigating, the theorem establishing a special case of one of the weaker conjectures, and the remark that the authors' confidence rests on the corresponding result being known for the move-to-front list update rule.
