# To bound how often a step fires, find a quantity it increments and then audit everything that can decrement it

**Lesson:** The whole cost of this structure hinges on one question: how many times does the inner extension step run? The step is inside a loop with no obvious trip count, and no argument about the loop itself will answer it. The technique used instead is indirect and mechanical. Find a quantity in the system that the step is guaranteed to increase by one whenever it fires. Then, since the quantity is bounded above and cannot go negative, the number of firings is bounded by the number of decrements that other operations can supply. So the proof becomes an audit: go through every operation in the interface, one at a time, and establish the most it can lower the quantity. Each entry in that audit is a small, local, checkable claim, and the loop bound falls out of their sum without ever reasoning about the loop.

What makes the technique work here rather than merely sound plausible is a second move: the first quantity does not cover every firing. A firing that leaves the quantity unchanged escapes the argument entirely. So a second classification of the same objects, on an axis unrelated to the first — a structural property of each edge rather than a state of the representation — is superimposed on it. Crossing the two classifications yields four populations, one of which supports a counting argument of exactly the same shape, and the residual population is bounded by a static fact about the structural axis alone. The habit worth extracting is that when a counting argument almost works, the productive response is not to strengthen the quantity but to look for an independent property to cross it with, and to argue about the product classes separately. Two crude classifications overlaid frequently outperform one clever one, because each of the resulting cases is easier than the whole.

The audit step is where the real intellectual work sits, and it is where the technique earns its reliability. Each operation must be examined for its worst effect on the quantity, which forces the analyst to walk the whole interface rather than the paths that came to mind — and in this paper that walk is what surfaces the awkward cases, since re-rooting a tree and severing an edge each disturb the structural property along an entire path and so pay a logarithmic decrement, while joining trees turns out to disturb nothing because the funnel it calls has already normalized the affected path. None of those three facts is guessable; each comes from asking the same narrow question of a specific operation. That is the transferable procedure for any bounded-resource argument in real systems — retries, cache evictions, rebalance triggers, reconnection attempts. Name the resource the event consumes, prove the event consumes it, then enumerate every producer. If you cannot complete the enumeration, you do not have a bound, and the operation you cannot account for is exactly the one that will produce the unbounded behavior in production.

**Source:** [A Data Structure for Dynamic Trees](../works/a-data-structure-for-dynamic-trees.md) — the two theorems bounding the number of splicing steps, the first identifying the count of solid edges as a quantity that each special splice increases and then tallying the decrement each of the exposure, linking, and cutting operations can contribute, and the second superimposing the heavy/light edge classification on the solid/dashed one to produce four edge classes, bounding the light splices per exposure by the static fact that a root path crosses at most logarithmically many light edges, and bounding the heavy splices by the same increment-and-audit argument applied to the count of heavy solid edges, including the separate examinations of how linking, cutting, and re-rooting each affect that count.
