# A proof covers your reasoning, not the substrate underneath it, so keep a cheap check at run time

**Lesson:** Establishing that a piece of software does what it should is a claim about your reasoning, made relative to assumptions about everything underneath: that the translator implemented the language you thought it did, that the machine performs the arithmetic it advertises, that the libraries beneath you behave as documented. Those assumptions are not part of what you checked, and each of them has been wrong in practice. So the strongest possible verification of your own work leaves a real residue of ways the running system can misbehave, and that residue is precisely the part no amount of additional care at your level can reach.

The proportionate response is a cheap check performed while the system runs. Associate with a component a condition relating what it was given to what it produced, evaluate it after the component finishes, and act on failure. Two properties make this practical and distinguish it from an assertion. It may be deliberately incomplete: for something that reorders a collection, confirming the result is in order and that a simple total over the elements is unchanged catches an enormous fraction of realistic failures for almost no cost, without amounting to a full check. And it is genuinely dynamic, so it observes what actually happened rather than what your model said would happen — which is exactly why it can catch a failure below your level of description.

Failure then wants a response, and the useful pattern is an alternative implementation of the same promise, tried after the state has been put back the way it was. Notice what this requires and why it is the hard part: you must be able to restore the starting state, which is a constraint on how the component interacts with everything around it, and it is far easier to arrange deliberately than to retrofit. Where no alternative remains, the honest move is to report failure upward rather than continue, and the whole arrangement nests, so an outer component's own check and alternative can cover a failure an inner one could not handle.

The point worth keeping is the layering. Careful reasoning and runtime checking are not competing approaches to reliability where one makes the other unnecessary; they cover disjoint failure classes. Reasoning eliminates the errors you can express in your model. Checking catches the ones your model could not see because they live below it. Where the consequences of failure are serious enough to justify the cost, use both, and be clear which one is buying you what.

**Source:** [Software Development: A Rigorous Approach](../works/software-development-a-rigorous-approach.md) — chapter 22's "Environment Independence" section on recovery blocks: the framing question of whether a program needs more insulation from its input data or from the supporting software it runs with, with the recommendation to check both; the description of a test condition as like a post-condition in relating starting and final states but differing in being tested at run time and in possibly being incomplete, illustrated by a sort whose test checks ordering and a simple checksum of input and output values; the response to failure by switching to an alternative implementation after restoring the initial values, and by signalling failure to the calling level when no further alternative exists, with the scheme extending naturally to nested recovery blocks; the observation that for any formally defined piece of code a candidate test condition is already available; and the argument that even with the relative certainty of machine-checked proofs the test conditions could trap hardware or compiler errors, so that for critical systems the two approaches should be employed together.
