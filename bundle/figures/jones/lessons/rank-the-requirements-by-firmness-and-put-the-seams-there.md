# Rank the requirements by how firm they are, and put your seams where the soft ones are

**Lesson:** When a description of what is wanted first arrives, you can nearly always tell which parts of it are settled and which are still moving. Somebody has thought hard about one area for years; another was written last week and everyone involved knows it will change. That judgement is available for free at the start, it is rarely written down, and it is the single most useful input to where you draw your internal boundaries.

Use it directly. Structure the description and the design so that the parts you expect to move are isolated behind something, and concentrate your effort on the parts that will not. Building a tool for a language still under design, work on the part that is not the language. This is not the same instruction as "make everything modular," which is unactionable because everything cannot be equally insulated from everything. It is a ranking exercise: firmness is the criterion that tells you which of the many possible decompositions to choose, out of the several that are otherwise equally defensible.

Two mechanisms make this more than an intention. A description can be split so that what a component promises is written as several separate claims, with the unsettled ones deliberately left incomplete — which records both that they are unsettled and exactly where the eventual answer will attach. And where the same information will be used by more than one consumer, the criterion for choosing how to hold it changes: optimizing for the access pattern of the one program you are writing today is the wrong objective when three more will arrive later, and flexibility should win over performance in that particular case. The general form is that the more consumers a decision has, the more it should be judged on how many futures it permits rather than on how well it serves the present.

Two honest limits. Deferring what is not essential does not eliminate change; some changes will land on decisions you did commit to, and that is true of every method. What a well-structured development buys you there is not immunity but estimation — you can trace what a proposed change touches and say what it costs, instead of discovering it. And the fact that a problem cannot be fully specified in advance is not a reason to abandon precision. Even where the eventual analysis depends on early results and genuinely cannot be settled up front, the input structure, the internal structures, and one worked example of the analysis can all be pinned down; what remains open is a smaller and clearly bounded part, which is exactly what you wanted to know.

**Source:** [Software Development: A Rigorous Approach](../works/software-development-a-rigorous-approach.md) — chapter 22's "Anticipating Change" section: the observation that abstraction itself insulates against certain changes of detail and that deferring interface representation decisions reduces the incidence of change; the recommendation that on first receiving a specification one forms a judgement of the relative firmness of its parts and documents the specification and design so as to isolate the less firm parts, with the interactive-compiler example of concentrating on the interactive portion because the language will continue to evolve; the note that the split can be made formal by expressing post-conditions as conjunctions of predicates some of which are left incomplete, which may also yield a more general program; the database observation that since the data will be accessed by more than one program the representation should be chosen more for flexibility than for performance in one particular case; the acknowledgement that changes will still land on decisions that influenced the design, as with any method, with the compensation that a detailed estimate of a change's impact becomes possible; and the closing account of the student whose data-gathering problem could not be specified in advance, who nonetheless documented the input structure at abstract and concrete levels, then the internal table structure and the input routine at an abstract level, and finally one analysis as an example.
