# Let the checks tell you which decision you owe next, and never expect them to tell you whether it was a good one

**Lesson:** The obligations a rigorous discipline generates are usually presented as things you discharge afterwards, which undersells them. They are also forms to fill in, and their shape names the decision you owe next. The obligation for putting two things one after the other contains a slot for the condition holding between them, and filling that slot simply is the interface decision — so the obligation has told you what to decide, and told you to decide it before writing either side. Designing a repetition, what you must produce is a pair of claims, and producing them is the design; the body then follows almost mechanically, and sometimes one of the pair does all the work while the other contributes nothing at all, which is itself informative. Read that way, the checking apparatus becomes a source of questions rather than a tax, and the order it imposes — commit to the joint, then to the parts — is the order you wanted anyway.

The limit has to be stated as plainly as the benefit. The apparatus records designs and checks them; it does not produce them. Which representation to choose, which algorithmic idea to use, where to break the problem — these remain invention, and attempts to automate them have succeeded only in very narrow territory. Worse, what the checks certify is correctness and nothing else: a step can be fully justified and still be a dead end, because its consequences for performance are unacceptable, and then there is no recourse but to go back and revisit the decision that led there. So the discipline supplies a frame into which commitments are placed and against which they are checked. Mistaking it for a design procedure produces verified bad designs, which are harder to argue with than unverified ones and no better.

There is a reframing that puts the detailed work in proportion. Instead of reading a finished development as the claim that some program is correct, read the description as the claim that an implementation exists, and the whole development as the argument for that claim. Then the choice of steps is the decomposition of the argument, and that is where nearly all the intellectual effort actually goes — a much more honest estimate than any count of obligations suggests. It also puts the obligation-discharging in its place: shallow, repetitive, cross-checking work, of the kind a careful mathematician does by differentiating to confirm an integration, and correspondingly the part most worth handing to a machine. The joints are what you are paid for. The verification is how you find out you cut in the wrong place.

**Source:** [Systematic Software Development Using VDM](../works/systematic-software-development-using-vdm.md) — the opening of the data-reification chapter, which states that the method is not intended to help make representation choices, that design relies on invention which has been automated only in very narrow areas, and that what is provided is a notation for recording designs together with the obligations establishing their correctness rather than their optimality; the decomposition-in-design section, which shows the sequencing rule's intermediate assertion fixing an interface between two sub-operations and uses the invariant-and-relation pair to drive loop construction — including a development whose loop is controlled entirely by the relation with the invariant offering no constraint — while cautioning that design requires intuition, cannot in general be automated, and that even a verified decision can lead to a blind alley with no choice but to reconsider it; and the opening of the case-study chapter, which replaces the program-is-correct reading with the specification as a claim that an implementation exists, treats the development as the proof and the choice of steps as the major decompositions of the argument, and compares creating a loop with its assertions to integration checked by differentiation.
