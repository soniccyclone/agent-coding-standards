# Name the restricted case, because generality is what costs you the ability to say anything about it

**Lesson:** The most general version of a concept is usually the one you can say least about. A rule for computing a result from an argument is enormously powerful and correspondingly opaque: you cannot in general even decide whether it produces anything for a given input. Restrict it to a finite explicit association of arguments with results and questions that were undecidable become trivial — what is it defined on, what does it produce, what happens if I combine two of them — and a whole vocabulary of operations becomes definable that had no meaning before. Restrict further, to associations whose argument set is an initial run of positions, and joining two end to end suddenly makes sense. Each restriction you accept buys back operations and laws. That trade is the reason to introduce the narrower thing as a named concept in its own right rather than treating it as the general concept with a side condition attached, and the reason not to reach for the most general formulation available just because it subsumes what you need.

The same move pays off at much smaller scale, where it looks like pedantry until you use it. Two operations can agree wherever both are defined and still be worth distinguishing: combining two associations by letting the second win where they overlap, and combining two whose arguments do not overlap at all, compute the same answer in the case both apply. Giving the non-overlapping case its own name costs one definition and buys commutativity in every argument that uses it — and buys something else, too. The restricted operation is undefined outside its case, so writing it is an assertion that you believe the case holds, checkable at the point of use. Naming a special case is a way of putting an assumption where a reader and a checker will both trip over it, instead of in a comment nobody reads. The same logic explains why an association that maps distinct arguments to distinct results deserves its own name: only for those does reversal make sense, and you want the fact that you are relying on reversibility to be visible in the type rather than discovered later.

There is a real cost, and it should be stated rather than waved past: every named special case is another concept a reader has to learn, and a vocabulary that grows without discipline is its own kind of opacity. The test for whether a restriction has earned its name is whether it unlocks a law or an operation you will actually use in argument — commutativity, invertibility, a decidable question, a total operation where the general one was partial. A restriction that only makes something shorter has not earned it. What you are buying is not brevity; it is the ability to reason at all, and that is worth paying an extra name for.
