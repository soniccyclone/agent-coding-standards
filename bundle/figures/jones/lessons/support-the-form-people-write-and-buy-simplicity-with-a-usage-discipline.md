# Support the form people actually write, and buy back the simplicity with a discipline on how it is used

**Lesson:** Build any analysis over a language and you meet a fork. The language has several constructs that overlap in what they can express; your machinery only needs one of them, and the others can be rewritten into it. So you can support one form and translate everything else into it, or you can supply separate machinery for each form people actually use. Translation is cheaper to build and it is the wrong choice. It means nobody can reason about the text in front of them — they must first transform it into something else, then reason, then carry the conclusion back, and every one of those crossings is an opportunity to be wrong about a program that was perfectly clear before you started. Worse, the natural form was usually chosen because it fits the problem; the construct that steps an index over a collection is the one people get right when working with collections, and forcing it into a general repetition form throws away exactly the structure that made it reliable.

The reason the translation route looks attractive is that supporting every form seems to require machinery general enough to cover every abuse of every form, which is genuinely awful to use. But that trade is avoidable, and the move that avoids it is the useful part of this. Do not build for the unconstrained construct. Constrain how the construct may be used, then build simple machinery for the constrained case. If the loop's control variable may be read but never assigned inside the body, the reasoning becomes short and there is nothing to prove about termination at all — where a fully general treatment would need both. The restriction is not a cost being paid for tractability; it is independently good practice, and it is the same discipline you would want on the code anyway.

The general shape is worth carrying around: use a large, messy, real language in a deliberately constrained way rather than either replacing it or handling all of it. You get to keep the parts that make people productive, your analysis stays small enough to actually apply, and the constraint you imposed is a rule you can state in one sentence and check by inspection. When a tool is unusable because the underlying language is too permissive, the first thing to try is not a more powerful tool but a narrower agreement about how the language will be used — and to notice which restrictions buy the most simplification, because those are the ones worth arguing for.

**Source:** [Software Development: A Rigorous Approach](../works/software-development-a-rigorous-approach.md) — chapter 7's treatment of the indexed iteration construct: the observation that although an array-scanning loop could be translated into the general repetition form already covered, it should not be necessary to translate programs in order to discuss their correctness, and the argument that the indexed form is a natural way of manipulating arrays and thus more likely to be used correctly; the resulting decision to supply a separate set of rules for it; the stated assumption that the control variable is a locally bound constant that may be read but not assigned within the loop, with the explicit parenthetical that general rules not requiring this restriction could be given but would be more difficult to use, and that preventing assignment makes programs clearer and thus more likely to be correct; the accompanying note that under these rules there are no termination conditions to prove; and, in chapter 1's "Structure of the Book" section, the stated intention to take a large language and use it in a constrained way, considering its features as required.
