# Narrow the boundary to a system you do not control, even when it costs you capability

**Lesson:** Whatever standard of description you hold your own work to, the systems you must talk to were not built that way, and their looseness is contagious. Reach into an undocumented system from many places, with rich argument lists, and its imprecision has now become a property of yours: nobody can say what your system assumes, because the assumptions are scattered across every call site and half of them are undocumented on the other side too. The defence is a boundary — every interaction with the foreign system routed through a small set of components, and the arguments crossing that boundary kept as few as you can manage.

The part that is easy to state and hard to do is the price. A narrow boundary will not expose everything the underlying system can do, and it will sometimes cost performance that a direct call could have had. Take the trade anyway, as the default. The reason is not fastidiousness: it is that the foreign system is the thing most likely to change out from under you — a new version, a different vendor, a move to another platform — and the size of that eventual bill is set precisely by how many places touched it and how much of its surface they used. A capability you did not expose is a capability you do not have to re-provide. Where you genuinely need something the narrow boundary excludes, widen it deliberately, in one place, and know that you have taken on the cost.

There is a second move that pairs with the first and is less obvious. Even when the thing on the far side already exists and its representation is fixed and inconvenient, write down an idealized version of what it holds anyway, and record how the real thing maps onto it. You did not get to choose the representation, but you still get to choose the terms you think in, and complexity does not become manageable any other way. The mapping you are forced to write is not overhead: in practice it turns out to be the specification of the adapting code you were going to have to write regardless — describing precisely how the foreign layout is to be read is most of the work of reading it.

**Source:** [Software Development: A Rigorous Approach](../works/software-development-a-rigorous-approach.md) — chapter 17's "On Interfaces" section: the warning that a new system interfacing with existing systems documented in a less rigorous style risks having that looseness subvert the new work; the recommendations that such interfaces be kept as narrow as possible, that interaction with existing systems be localized within the new one, and that argument lists to the interface modules be as short as possible, with the explicit instruction to follow these even where they prevent access to all the function or performance of the underlying software, on the grounds that localization pays off in migration to other systems or other versions; and the further proposal that even where the representation already exists it is worth documenting the abstraction, since complexity can only be controlled by abstraction, illustrated by the compiler project in which a translator specified over abstract programs had to consume the linear output of an existing parser, where the abstract documentation brought the interface under control and the necessary retrieve functions led to the scanning techniques required in the final program.
