# Describing a type by laws instead of a model does not escape the model, it just stops you choosing it

**Lesson:** The appeal of defining a type purely by equations between its operations is that you appear to avoid committing to any concrete structure, and with it the risk of accidentally describing one particular implementation. The appearance is false. Such a description does have an underlying structure: the space of all expressions you can build from the chosen constructors, carved into classes by the equations. So the commitment was made anyway, only sight unseen and dictated by whichever constructors you happened to pick. Sometimes that inherited structure is the one you would have chosen — for a stack it fits perfectly. Sometimes it is not, and then the equations you must write to compensate come out looking exactly like the thing you were avoiding: for a queue, the natural law splits into two cases with a recursive one, which is an algorithm in disguise. A structure you chose deliberately can be the convenient one. A structure your notation imposed cannot be, and you have given up the ability to change it.

A second, sharper point: a collection of laws does not by itself say what is equal. The same equations support two readings — things are distinct unless the laws prove them identical, or identical unless the laws prove them distinct — and they denote different types. Under the first reading you have to keep adding laws to stop the space being too finely divided; under the second you have to add an operation landing in some type you already trust, or everything collapses into a single value. Which extra operation you add is what pins the type down: an is-a-member test yields collections without duplicates or order, a counting operation yields collections that remember multiplicity, a first-element operation yields ordered ones — from the identical constructors each time. The general form of this is worth carrying around: rewriting laws never fix identity on their own. Identity comes from the reading plus at least one observation that escapes into a domain whose equality you already understand.

Where that leaves the two styles is a division by scale, not a contest. The equational style rewards mathematical care and repays it on the small reusable kit of base types, where the same description gets used by everything downstream and where it is what parameterizing over element types actually needs — but its axioms need checking for consistency and completeness by someone competent to do it, and some types cannot be characterized by finitely many laws at all. For an application you intend to build, the structure-based style is safer and more informative: the held structure conveys insight on its own and lets you consider one operation at a time. The productive middle is to derive properties from your structure routinely as a check against intent, and to stop well short of grinding them into a complete axiomatization.
