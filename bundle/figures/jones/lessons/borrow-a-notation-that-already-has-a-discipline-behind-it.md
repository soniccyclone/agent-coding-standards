# Borrow a notation that already has a discipline behind it, and treat it as shorthand rather than as ceremony

**Lesson:** When you need a language to describe something precisely, there are two moves available: invent one fitted to your problem, or take one from a field that has already been arguing about it for a century. The second is almost always better, and for a reason that is not about familiarity. A notation lifted from an established discipline arrives with its internal consistency already established by that discipline — the operators mean compatible things, the laws relating them are known, and nothing you write can quietly contradict something else you wrote. Invent your own and you own that consistency problem yourself, usually without noticing you have taken it on until two parts of a description turn out to disagree.

The second half of the argument is about what notation is actually for, and it cuts against the way formal notation is usually resented. It is a shorthand. Somebody writing a number in Roman numerals is not failing to be rigorous; long division on those numerals is nonetheless close to impossible, and the difficulty is entirely in the writing system rather than in the arithmetic. The payoff of a good notation is not that it proves you were careful. It is that it frees attention for the part of the problem that is genuinely hard, and — decisively — that it makes the description something you can *manipulate*: derive consequences from, transform, check an implementation against. A description you can only read is worth much less than one you can compute over, and which of those you get is settled by the notation you picked, before you write the first line.

There is a corollary that resolves the usual objection that a formal description will be unreadable by the people who most need to read it. Adopting a formal notation does not force every leaf of the description to be formal. What it forces is a *structure* — a skeleton of obligations and slots, where each slot is a claim that has to be made about something. Individual slots can be filled with ordinary prose when the concept is genuinely understood by everyone involved and formalizing it would only be practice. The structure is what makes the informal parts safe, because it fixes exactly what each one is claiming and leaves a visible hole where a claim is missing. Later, any slot can be replaced by a formal version without disturbing anything around it. Note the asymmetry with the alternative: a well-structured description in ordinary prose can be derived from a precise one, but an ill-structured prose description is no basis for deriving anything.

**Source:** [Software Development: A Rigorous Approach](../works/software-development-a-rigorous-approach.md) — chapter 1's "Merits of a Rigorous Approach" section, on notation as shorthand rather than as an appeal to complex mathematics, the Roman-versus-Arabic numeral comparison and the quoted claim that a good notation frees the brain for more advanced problems, and the argument that apposite notation is what makes specifications manipulable; together with chapter 2's reasoning for taking notation from established branches of mathematics rather than building a specification language from scratch (familiarity, and consistency assured by the soundness of the underlying disciplines), its point that formal notation forces a structure onto a definition whose remaining parts may be given in natural language and later replaced without disrupting the skeleton, and the observation that a well-structured English document can be derived from a precise specification while the reverse does not hold.
