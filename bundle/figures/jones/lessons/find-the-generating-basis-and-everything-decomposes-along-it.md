# Find the smallest set of things that build your type, because definitions and proofs both decompose along it

**Lesson:** For any type you intend to reason about, the highest-leverage thing to establish first is the small collection of constructors from which every value can be built — an empty one and a way to extend, or the analogous pair for whatever you are describing. Once you have it, three things follow almost mechanically. Every other operation gets defined by cases over the constructors. The reasoning principle for the type falls straight out of the same list, with base obligations coming from the non-recursive constructors and step obligations from the recursive ones, so you do not have to invent it. And proofs then decompose in exactly the shape the definitions did, which is why they come out short. It is worth being explicit about what this beats, because the alternative is tempting and available: you can prove things about finite collections by inducting on how many elements they have, reducing everything to counting, and it works. It just produces worse arguments, because they are now about numbers rather than about the thing you care about, and the correspondence has to be maintained by hand.

Two properties of a basis deserve as much attention as its existence. First, whether distinct expressions over it denote distinct values. For ordered collections they do, and nothing further need be said. For unordered ones they do not, and you must state precisely which rearrangements are identifications — that two extensions with different elements may be swapped, that extending twice with the same element is the same as once. Second, and this is the part that repays the effort: those identification laws are the entire source of the algebraic properties of every operation you later define over the basis. Commutativity of a combining operation is inherited from commutativity of the constructor; idempotence from the absorption law. Which means the properties you cannot state about the basis are exactly the ones you do not get downstream — the reason a joining operation on ordered collections is associative but not commutative is visible at the constructors and nowhere else. Deciding what your constructors identify is therefore deciding what you will be able to prove for the rest of the type's life.

Two smaller notes. Where the type carries a legality condition, put it into the step obligation of the reasoning principle as an available hypothesis, so that every proof over the type gets the condition for free rather than dragging it along separately. And notice what a recursive definition over a basis is silently relying on: that each individual value is finite, even though there are unboundedly many of them. That is a genuine restriction on the type, usually worth imposing deliberately, and the alternative — admitting infinite values — quietly removes your reasoning principle along with the termination of every recursive definition you wrote.
