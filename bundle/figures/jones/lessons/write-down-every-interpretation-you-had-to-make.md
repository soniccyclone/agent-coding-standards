# Write down every interpretation you had to make, because each one is a question you are answering for someone else

**Lesson:** Take any requirement written in ordinary prose and try to restate it precisely, and you will discover that you cannot do it without deciding things the prose left open. Is an oversized item still counted? Is it truncated in the output, or passed through? Does the terminator appear in the result? Is a boolean enough, or was a count wanted? None of these were withheld deliberately. They are what prose does: it reads as complete because the reader supplies the missing pieces from context, and every reader supplies different pieces.

The valuable thing here is not the observation that prose is ambiguous, which everybody already accepts and then proceeds to ignore. It is what the attempted precision does to the timing. Each decision you are forced to make is a question the customer could have answered in a sentence, and you are encountering it at the cheapest possible moment — before any structure has been built on it. The alternative is not that these questions go away; it is that they get answered silently, by whoever writes the code, at whatever moment the code first needs an answer, with the decision recorded nowhere. It will surface later as a defect, and the person who eventually finds it will have no way to tell whether it was a misunderstanding or a deliberate choice.

So the discipline is to keep the list. Every place you resolved an ambiguity, write down what you decided and, crucially, that a decision was made at all. The list is the deliverable to take back to whoever asked for the system; it is far more useful to them than the precise description itself, because it is short and every item on it is something they have an opinion about. Note the reframing this permits: getting one of them wrong is not a mistake on your part. It is evidence that the starting point could not determine the answer, which is a property of the request rather than a failure of the implementer — and the list is what makes that visible instead of leaving you to be blamed for guessing.

One structural addition makes the list much more useful: separate the questions about *what should happen* from the questions about *how things are laid out*. They go to different people, they are settled by different arguments, and mixing them is what makes a requirements conversation unproductive. Do the substantive half first, with the layout deliberately left abstract, and handle the representation questions as a second pass once the behaviour is agreed.

**Source:** [Software Development: A Rigorous Approach](../works/software-development-a-rigorous-approach.md) — chapter 21's treatment of the telegram analysis problem: the reproduction of the original natural-language specification, the identification of two distinct sorts of problem with it — questions of what processing is required, and problems caused by the representation of the information content — and the decision to separate them by giving an abstract specification of the processing first; the explicit enumeration of six interpretations that had to be made in order to write the formal version, covering whether overlength items are charged, whether they are modified for output, whether digits count toward length, whether a flag or a count is wanted, and whether the delimiters appear in the output; and the accompanying argument that it is certainly possible to disagree with some of these decisions, that this is precisely the problem with natural language specifications, that a wrong interpretation is not an error but a symptom of the weakness of the starting point, and that the attempt to formalize uncovers the problems before development begins so the questions can be resolved with the user much earlier than would otherwise be possible.
