# Justify skipping work by showing what you skipped was empty, and prefer a filter that can only err toward doing it

**Lesson:** Most real speedups are subtractions: the fast version does the same thing as the slow one except that it declines to explore paths the slow one explored. The argument that such a change is safe has a shape worth naming, because it is much lighter than the argument people reach for. You do not have to show that the two versions compute the same answer by comparing their answers. You show that the branch you removed would have contributed nothing — that under the conditions in which the skip fires, the omitted work provably yields the empty contribution. Then the results are equal because one of the two summands was zero, and the whole thing rests on a property of the slow version rather than on any reasoning about the fast one. This is the second dividend of having kept the direct formulation around: it is the thing in which the emptiness claim is stated.

The corollary shapes what kind of test you should be looking for. Since the argument only needs the skip to fire when the work is empty, it does not need the test to fire *whenever* the work is empty. That asymmetry is enormously valuable, because it licenses cheap approximate tests: a filter that answers "certainly nothing here" or "possibly something here", never confusing the first for the second, is sound by construction no matter how crude it is. Getting a false "possibly" costs you a little wasted work and no correctness. So the design question stops being "how do I compute exactly which branches are dead" — usually as expensive as exploring them — and becomes "what is the cheapest signal that is never wrong in the direction that matters." A summary byte, a precomputed hint attached to the rule, a hash of what is present: all of these are legitimate because their errors are confined to one side.

Two habits follow. Establish where the work is before you subtract any: instrument the running system and find the loop that is actually executing an absurd number of times, rather than the one that looks expensive. And keep the pruned version's justification anchored to the unpruned one, so that when a later change alters what the unpruned version computes, you can see immediately whether the emptiness claim still holds. An optimization whose correctness argument has drifted loose from its baseline is indistinguishable from a guess.

Also the chapter's "Modifications" section, where unacceptable performance on a large grammar was diagnosed by inserted counters showing an enormous execution count in the state-set insertion loop, and answered with a hash vector used to determine that an item is definitely not present.
