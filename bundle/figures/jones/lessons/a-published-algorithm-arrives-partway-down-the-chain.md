# A published algorithm arrives already partway down the development chain, so work out which step it is

**Lesson:** An algorithm you take from the literature is not a specification and it is not code. It is a design that someone else carried several steps down from a specification and then stopped writing about, and the single most useful question to ask on receiving one is which step it is. Above it sit the steps that were taken and not shown — the statement of what is wanted, and whatever intermediate formulations turned that statement into the shape you are looking at. Below it sit the steps nobody has taken yet: choosing representations for the structures it mentions, turning its recursions into something the machine executes cheaply, deciding what happens on input it was never described as handling. Both directions are work, and treating the published form as either end of the chain gets you into different trouble.

Treat it as a specification and you inherit an artifact with no argument attached. Every clever move in it is unexplained, so the parts you have to change — and there always are some, because your problem is not exactly the paper's — you change by guesswork. Reconstructing the missing levels above is what converts a copied algorithm into one you can modify: the intermediate formulation, usually a direct and hopelessly inefficient statement of the same computation, is where the reason the algorithm gets the right answer actually lives. That intermediate level will never be executed by anything. It is still worth writing down, because it is the only thing that makes the published version's cleverness attributable to something.

Treat it as an implementation and you underestimate what is left. The published form is written to be understood by a reader who already knows the problem area, in whatever notation made the idea clearest, with the representation decisions deliberately unmade because they would have obscured the idea. Those decisions are yours, they are the ones that determine whether the thing performs, and they are exactly the ones the paper gave you no help with. The reliable signal that this is being misjudged is duration: a competent person working directly from a paper on a nontrivial algorithm can spend weeks and still ship something wrong, and the error is almost never in the idea they copied. It is in the layers they did not realize they were inventing.

**Source:** [Software Development: A Rigorous Approach](../works/software-development-a-rigorous-approach.md) — chapter 18's development of Earley's recognizer: the introduction's statement that the given algorithm could be documented as the third development step and that this should be the starting point for developing a program, repeated at the head of that step, which adds that everything before it is theory of parsing presented only to convey an understanding of the problem; the parenthetical noting that the 1971 development followed the original paper's state-set presentation directly whereas the recursive-function formulation shown here gives deeper insight into why the algorithm works; the warning that the first non-rigorous development of a program from the original paper took many weeks and still resulted in a program with errors; and the remarks opening the third step that simply programming the recursion will not be efficient given typical compiler implementations and that a representation would still have to be chosen for the integer sets the functions return.
