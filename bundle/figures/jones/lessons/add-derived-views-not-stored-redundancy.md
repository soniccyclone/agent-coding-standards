# When descriptions get clumsy, add derived views rather than stored redundancy, and keep two whole structures if each earns its keep

**Lesson:** There is a pressure that arrives reliably as the set of operations grows: some operation becomes clumsy to state because the thing it wants is not directly held, and the obvious remedy is to hold it. Resist that specific remedy. Anything stored redundantly has to be kept consistent with what it duplicates, which means a legality condition, which then has to be re-established by every operation that touches either copy and complicates every check that an operation is achievable at all. The cheaper fix is a named function that derives the wanted thing from a minimal structure. At the point of use it reads exactly as well as the stored version, and it carries no consistency burden anywhere. The general rule is to grow the vocabulary of derived views rather than the amount held, and to treat a proposal to store something computable as a proposal to add an obligation to every operation in the system.

Sometimes, though, the tension is not about one field but about the whole arrangement, and then compromise is the wrong move. One arrangement may need almost no legality conditions while another lets several operations mention only the part they actually touch — genuinely different virtues, neither reducible to the other. When that happens, keep both and record the mapping between them explicitly in each direction. That this is possible at all is exactly the condition for the two being interchangeable, so the existence of mappings both ways doubles as the check that you have not accidentally described two different things. You then state each operation against whichever arrangement suits it, and you have paid one bounded price — the two mappings — instead of distorting every operation to fit a single compromise structure.

The last observation is the one most often skipped. Two arrangements can be interchangeable and still be far from equally pleasant to reason with. Marking a distinguished element by having it refer to itself, or by its absence from what is held, describe precisely the same thing; one of them makes an operation total and removes a case split from every definition, the other makes a termination argument easy to state. There is no way to know which you want by inspection, so try both on the properties you expect to need and let that decide. Interchangeability is a fact about what can be expressed, and it tells you nothing at all about what will be convenient to prove — so the choice among equivalent arrangements is a real engineering decision about the body of reasoning you are about to write, not a matter of taste to be settled quickly and regretted later.
