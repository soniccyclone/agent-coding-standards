# Derive the invariant from the goal by putting the progress variable where the target value stands

**Lesson:** The received wisdom about loop invariants is that finding one is an act of invention with no method behind it. That is true of the hard cases and badly overstated for the ordinary ones, where there is a mechanical starting move that gets you most of the way. Take the statement of what the whole loop is supposed to achieve. Find the quantity that measures how far the loop has got. Substitute the second into the first, in the position occupied by the value the loop is counting toward. What comes out is the core of the invariant: it says that the work done so far bears the same relationship to the progress marker that the finished work is required to bear to the target. The finishing argument then falls out for free, because when the loop stops, the marker has reached the target and the invariant has become the goal.

That substitution gives you the essential clause and nothing else, and the rest of the invariant is a different kind of content that should be recognized as such. Everything remaining is bookkeeping: assertions that the things which must not move have not moved. These are dull, they are easy to forget, and forgetting them is the most common way an otherwise correct-looking invariant fails to imply what you want. Do not hunt for them by inspection. Attempt the obligations with only the essential clause in place and let the failures name the missing conjuncts — one obligation will fail for want of knowing a value was preserved across the body, another for want of knowing the target itself was untouched. The checks are better at finding the omissions than you are, and using them that way turns invariant-writing into an iterative process with feedback rather than a single leap.

The wider point is about what a design heuristic is worth. There is a persistent belief that anything you cannot fully mechanize you must do by pure insight, which leaves people with no method at all for the eighty per cent of cases that have structure. A partial heuristic that produces the right shape and then tells you how to find your own omissions is worth far more than a complete algorithm for a narrower class, because it is applicable while you work and it degrades gracefully when the case is genuinely hard: you still know what the answer must look like, and you still know what will tell you it is incomplete.
