# The defining equation is the specification: if two programs satisfy the same recursion, they are the same program

**Lesson:** Most attempts to prove a program correct start by reasoning about what happens when it runs — the sequence of states, the loop invariant, the induction on step count. There is a cheaper move available whenever a function is defined by a guarded equation that mentions itself. Such an equation does not merely describe a procedure; it *pins down* a unique function on every input where the unwinding terminates, because the equation is exactly the rule you would use to compute the value. So if you take two different expressions, both defined everywhere the equation is, and show by ordinary algebraic rewriting that each of them satisfies that same equation, you have shown they agree — everywhere. No trace, no step counting, no induction on anything but the shape of the equation itself.

The consequence for how one thinks is that program equality becomes a subject for calculation rather than argument. The paper demonstrates this on ground everybody already believes — commutativity and associativity of addition, distributivity of multiplication, associativity of list concatenation, that reversal is its own inverse — and the demonstrations all have the same flat shape: propose an auxiliary equation, rewrite both candidates until each visibly satisfies it, done. Two supporting habits make the rewriting go through. Conditional forms get their own algebra, with a short list of equivalences and rules of substitution and replacement that let case-analysis-by-prose collapse into symbol pushing, including the refinement that inside a branch you only have to prove things *under that branch's guard*. And the whole point of having such a calculus is the transformation pipeline it enables: write the version whose correctness is obvious, then move by equivalence-preserving steps to the version that is fast, or thrifty with storage, or fused with some auxiliary process — and let the chain of equivalences, not a fresh act of faith, carry the correctness across.

The method is honest about its boundary, and the boundary is instructive. Recursion induction establishes equality only on the domain where the shared equation terminates; it says nothing about termination itself, and the paper openly admits it has no good technique for proving convergence and cannot hope for a complete one. It also shows what happens if you forget which facts about your domain you are actually assuming: addition's defining equation is satisfied just as well by an operation on the vertices of a binary tree, so the theorems that need genuine integer structure only follow once the successor and predecessor axioms are stated. Equational reasoning over a definition is not a substitute for knowing what your data is.

A programmer who works this way writes definitions that are worth reasoning about — small, guarded, self-referential, free of state threaded through the side — because that form is what makes the proof technique applicable at all. They treat "these two implementations are the same" as a claim to be settled by rewriting rather than by testing both. And they factor out shared recursive skeletons deliberately, since the skeleton is the thing two candidate implementations can both be shown to satisfy.

**Source:** [A Basis for a Mathematical Theory of Computation](../works/a-basis-for-a-mathematical-theory-of-computation.md) — the section introducing recursion induction and the run of worked equivalences that follows it on arithmetic and on list operations, together with the preceding treatment of conditional forms as an algebra with its own axioms, canonical forms, and premiss-sensitive replacement rule. The motivation appears earlier, in the list of goals a theory of computation should serve.
