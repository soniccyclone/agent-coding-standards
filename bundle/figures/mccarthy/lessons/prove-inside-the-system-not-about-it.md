# Build a formalism for proving things about particular programs, not for proving things about the formalism

**Lesson:** By 1961 there was no shortage of rigorous accounts of computation, and the striking claim of this paper is that none of them were any use for the thing a programmer needs. The available theories had been optimized for a different job: establishing which problems are unsolvable, characterizing what a class of machines can reach in principle, proving results *about* the formalism as a whole. That research program produced real theorems, but it produced almost nothing that helps you say something true about the algorithm currently in front of you, and the paper names the reason bluntly — notations built to make metatheorems convenient turn out to be too cumbersome to write actual algorithms in. So the design criterion it adopts instead is in-system provability: pick the formalism in which proofs about specific programs are easy to state and easy to carry out, and accept that this is a different optimization target from elegance of the surrounding metatheory.

The diagnosis of *why* the older formalisms fail is more interesting than the complaint. Machine models and the classical recursive-function apparatus both encode control decisions in the data domain — you steer the computation by arithmetic on numbers, or by tape-head contortions — so a program's branching structure is present only in simulated form, reconstructible but not visible. Anybody who has written down a machine model to compute something familiar notices they had to invent tricks, and the tricks are precisely the gap between the notation and the thought. A formalism with direct case-selection has no such gap, so the written form of an algorithm stays close to the reasoning that produced it, and the resulting expressions are short enough that proofs about them are tractable. Adequacy of expressive power is not the question; every one of these systems reaches the same functions. The question is whether the structure of the thought survives the transcription.

The same criterion is turned on proof itself. Logicians had, quite reasonably for their purposes, designed formal systems so that theorems *about* those systems would be provable; nobody had optimized for a human sitting down to write a proof *in* one. Once a machine is available to check proofs, the economics change: a checker can be asked to do far more work than a reader ever could, so it becomes reasonable to leave gaps for the machine to search out, which makes proofs cheaper to write, which is what would make proving programs correct preferable to running test cases until the failures stop. The generalized notion of a formal system that follows from this is deliberately minimal — a decidable relation between a claim and a candidate justification — because that is all the structure the goal actually requires.

The practitioner's version of this is a habit of asking what a notation, type system, specification language, or proof tool was *tuned for*, and refusing to assume that a system's theoretical credentials transfer to the job you have. Power is not fit. If you find yourself inventing encodings to say something ordinary, the formalism is telling you it was built for a different purpose, and the right response is to look for one whose primitives match the structure of your reasoning rather than to get better at the encoding.

**Source:** [A Basis for a Mathematical Theory of Computation](../works/a-basis-for-a-mathematical-theory-of-computation.md) — the introduction's survey of why numerical analysis, computability theory, and finite automata each fail to support a practical theory; the later comparison against machine models and minimalization-based recursion, which locates the failure in indirect control flow; and the discussion of machine-checked proof and its consequences for how formal systems should be designed. The closing section restates in-system proof as the paper's organizing difference from prior work.
