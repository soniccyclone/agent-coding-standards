# A recursive definition has two solutions, and which one you meant is the difference between an obligation and a constraint

**Lesson:** Write a property as an equation whose right-hand side mentions the property itself, and you have not yet said what you mean. Such an equation generally has more than one solution, and the two extreme ones say opposite things. The smallest solution is the one built up from nothing by repeated application, so it contains only what some finite number of steps forces in; it is the right reading of a claim that something must be discharged, reached, completed. The largest solution is the one pared down from everything by repeated application, so it contains anything that survives arbitrarily long; it is the right reading of a claim that something must never break. Emerson treats the choice between these two as the thing that separates promises from prohibitions, and it is the same distinction a programmer knows informally as liveness versus safety without usually noticing that both come from one equation read two ways.

The practical payoff is that once the reading is fixed, the algorithm is fixed too. Smallest solution means seed the computation with the empty answer and grow; largest means seed with everything and shrink. Either way you iterate until the answer stops changing, and over a finite domain that happens in a bounded number of rounds. What guarantees convergence is not cleverness but a syntactic restriction: the recursive occurrence must not appear under a negation. That restriction buys monotonicity, monotonicity buys a chain that only moves one direction, and a chain that only moves one direction over a finite lattice has to stop. This is an unusually clean instance of a principle worth carrying around: a small, checkable rule about the shape of a definition can be exactly what makes the definition computable, and enforcing that rule costs less than reasoning about termination case by case.

The consequences show up outside verification. Dataflow analyses, reachability computations, transitive closures, constraint propagation, incremental build graphs, and cache invalidation cascades are all fixed-point computations where somebody chose a seed and a direction, usually implicitly. When such a computation produces the wrong answer in a cyclic case, the cause is very often that the code computes one extreme while the requirement wanted the other. A loop in a dependency graph either means "nothing here is satisfied" or "everything here is satisfied," and those are the two seeds. Choosing between them is a specification decision, not an implementation detail, and it should be written down.

A programmer who thinks this way, on meeting any self-referential definition, immediately asks two questions: which solution do I want, and is the recursion monotone. If the answer to the second is no, they know that iterating may never settle and that a fix means restructuring the definition rather than adding an iteration cap. And when reading someone else's convergent loop, they read the initial value as a claim about intent rather than as a default.

The 1981 paper's model-checking section shows the same idea concretely, computing an eventuality property as a sequence of successively weaker approximations that spread outward from the states where it holds immediately.
