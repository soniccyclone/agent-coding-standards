description: Argues that software's difficulty splits into essential complexity (the inherent conceptual structure of the problem) and accidental complexity (friction introduced by tools and process), and that most proposed productivity fixes only attack the accidental half. Brooks surveys the candidate "silver bullets" of the mid-1980s — high-level languages, object orientation, AI, automatic programming, better environments — and argues none delivers an order-of-magnitude gain because the essential difficulty of specifying, building, and verifying a system's conceptual structure cannot be automated away. The essay's lasting contribution is less any single prediction and more the essential/accidental framing itself, which became a standard lens for judging whether a new tool addresses real difficulty or just surface friction.

# No Silver Bullet: Essence and Accidents of Software Engineering

**Venue/year:** Delivered at the IFIP Congress '86 (Dublin) and published in the proceedings, "Information Processing 1986," pp. 1069-1076; reprinted with light revisions in IEEE Computer 20(4), April 1987, pp. 10-19. Also circulated as UNC Computer Science technical report TR86-020 (September 1986).
**Source:** https://www.cs.unc.edu/techreports/86-020.pdf — UNC Department of Computer Science technical reports archive, institutional host, verified live (200 OK, application/pdf).
