# Method raises the floor of design and never the ceiling; the gap between competent and excellent design is not procedural

**Lesson:** Bad design can be replaced by decent design through teaching. Practices can be written down, curricula built, and the average lifted, and doing so is entirely worthwhile since the spread between the best practice in this field and the ordinary practice is wider than in most engineering disciplines. But the step from decent to excellent does not come from the same source. What separates a sound design method from an unsound one is knowledge that transfers; what separates a good designer from an outstanding one does not, and repeated measurement of individual output has found differences approaching an order of magnitude among people of equivalent experience, with experience itself a poor predictor.

The evidence for the ceiling is in the artifacts. Designs from the strongest hands come out smaller and faster and involve fewer independent notions to be held at once, and they took less work to produce. That is a primitive-count observation as much as an aesthetic one: the same function is being delivered from a smaller basis, which is why the result is simultaneously easier to hold in mind and cheaper to run. Look at which systems have attracted genuine devotion over decades, and they are consistently the work of one designer or a few in close agreement, while the systems assembled by committee are competent and unloved. Discipline can free a strong designer and cannot animate a weak one.

For an individual, believing this redirects effort from hunting for the process that will make you good toward studying designs that are better than yours and working directly for the people who produced them. For an organization it implies something sharper and almost never done: design talent should be identified early, tracked, mentored, and rewarded with the same deliberateness and the same visible status that management talent receives, because the technical quality of everything shipped ultimately rests on it. Treating great design as a procedural output is a category error, and it leads to spending all the developmental attention on the ladder that is easier to see.

**Source:** [No Silver Bullet: Essence and Accidents of Software Engineering](../works/no-silver-bullet.md) — the closing section on cultivating designers, which distinguishes the poor-to-good transition, achievable by promulgating practice, from the good-to-great one, and draws the organizational consequence. The chapter on team structure in [The Mythical Man-Month](../works/mythical-man-month.md) supplies the measured productivity spread among experienced programmers that the argument rests on.
