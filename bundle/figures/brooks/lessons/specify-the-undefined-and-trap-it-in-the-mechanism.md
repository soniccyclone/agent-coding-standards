# State what you do not guarantee as carefully as what you do, and make the mechanism reject it, or the running implementation becomes the specification

**Lesson:** A specification has two jobs and everybody only remembers one. The first is to say what conforming behaviour is. The second is to mark, explicitly and exhaustively, the places where it is deliberately silent. Those places come in grades that are easy to miss: variation permitted between two different members of a family, variation permitted between two units of the very same member, and variation permitted within a single unit before and after it has been serviced. Each grade is a distinct promise not being made, and each has to be written down as such. The second job is the harder piece of writing and the one that determines whether the first job's promise means anything, because a promise whose boundary is undrawn is a promise about everything, including the parts nobody thought about.

The reason the boundary must be drawn is that a running system always answers. Ask it a question the document never addressed and it does not decline; it does something, and whatever it does becomes the operative answer the moment anyone builds on it. The empirical record here is brutal: emulating an earlier machine on a later one turned up dozens of behaviours that were side effects of operations the manual had declared invalid, all of them in real use, all of them now part of what the new machine had to reproduce. Accidental behaviour promoted to contract this way costs more than embarrassment. It can foreclose better mechanisms outright — leftover state in a register after an arithmetic operation, once depended upon, forbids the faster algorithm that would not have left it there. An implementation used as a definition always over-specifies, because it necessarily decides everything, including all the things whose decision should have been left open.

Drawing the boundary on paper is therefore not enough; the mechanism has to enforce it. If the constructs the document calls invalid are detected and refused rather than quietly tolerated, then no program can come to depend on them, and the freedom the specification carefully reserved stays actually free. Without that enforcement the tolerant realisation is the one everyone writes against, and code appears that works beautifully on one member of the family and not another — which destroys the compatibility the whole exercise existed to provide. A programmer who has internalised this writes the "results here are not dependable" clauses first, treats every unpoliced permissiveness as a promise being made without consent, and reads a fielded system's undocumented conveniences as future liabilities rather than as features.

**Source:** [Architecture of the IBM System/360](../works/architecture-of-the-ibm-system-360.md) — the intermodel-compatibility discussion of what strict compatibility means for invalid programs, and the boundary-alignment passage where the general rule is that documented invalidities are caught by the hardware and raise an interruption distinct from a malfunction; also [The Mythical Man-Month](../works/mythical-man-month.md), whose chapter on disseminating architectural decisions praises the compatibility appendix that enumerates the intentional silences, and recounts the accumulated side-effect behaviours found when emulating an older machine.
