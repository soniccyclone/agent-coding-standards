# Building is a different activity from discovering, so novelty earns nothing; what you make is tested only by what someone else accomplishes with it

**Lesson:** Two activities look similar from outside and have opposite success conditions. In one, you build apparatus in order to learn something, and a new fact or a new law is itself the achievement, publishable on its own terms. In the other, you study in order to build, and what you have made is worthless until it works in someone's hands. Programming belongs to the second, and almost all of what programmers produce is instrumental: not a thing that satisfies a human want directly, but a thing with which others make the things that do. Getting this classification wrong is not a matter of professional pride; it silently swaps in the wrong acceptance test.

Under the discovery frame, difference counts. An unprecedented construct is worth having because it is unprecedented, and the natural thing to do with it is to publish it and move on. Under the building frame that reasoning collapses: an artifact meant to be used is judged by usefulness against its cost, and novelty is not a term in that expression at all. However much craft went into an implement's form and finish, none of it counts as evidence; only its performance in the work it exists for does. The strongest version of the criterion locates the judgment entirely outside the maker, making the maker's success strictly derivative of the users' — which means you cannot grade your own work and have to go find out how theirs went. Two demanding practical versions follow: the thing has to be easy enough that a busy senior person can use it, and good enough that they choose to. Junior people will endure any awkwardness that advances their own deadline, so their willingness proves nothing.

The failure mode this guards against is retreat into tractability. Faced with a messy real problem, it is always possible to abstract it into a version that admits clean treatment, and the abstraction frequently discards the part that made the problem a problem. The work that follows is rigorous, publishable, and about something else. The same drift shows up in language: vocabulary that grows specialised faster than it grows precise, until the writing circulates only among people who write the same way. Guarding against this is not modesty. It is keeping the acceptance test located where the value is, which requires staying in contact with someone who will actually try to use the thing and tell you it does not work.

**Source:** [The Computer Scientist as Toolsmith II](../works/computer-scientist-as-toolsmith-ii.md) — the opening argument distinguishing the two dispositions by their purpose rather than their daily activity, the passage deriving the success criterion for a maker of instruments from that distinction, the enumeration of how a misapplied self-image drives out attention to users, and the two thresholds offered later as tests of whether a built tool has actually landed.
