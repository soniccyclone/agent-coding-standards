# Take a problem from someone who owns it, on their terms, because a borrowed problem refuses the simplifications you would grant yourself

**Lesson:** Working on somebody else's problem, defined the way they need it rather than the way you would prefer it, produces better technical work than choosing your own. The claim sounds backwards, since serving an outside need looks like a tax on the real research. It holds because of what the arrangement removes rather than what it adds. A problem you own can be reshaped whenever it resists, and the reshaping is invisible; a problem someone else owns cannot. The degenerate cases you would have excluded — the coincidences, the ill-conditioned inputs, the configurations that "won't occur in practice" — stay in scope, and handling them is where the work that generalises actually comes from. The whole problem has to be faced, including the parts that are not mathematically interesting, and facing them drags you into territory you would never have entered by choice.

The second thing the arrangement removes is the ability to grade your own homework. When success is defined by someone whose own work depends on the outcome, there is no way to declare victory on a proxy. This is worth more than it sounds, because self-assessment failure is not usually dishonesty; it is the ordinary drift of judging a thing by the criteria it happens to satisfy. An external owner supplies a criterion you cannot adjust, which keeps the honest accounting of what worked and what did not.

The costs are real and should be accepted deliberately rather than discovered. A meaningful fraction of the effort goes into work that helps the collaborator and advances nothing of your own — producing what they need for their purposes, on their schedule. The senior people on both sides must do the negotiating themselves, since that part cannot be delegated to anyone junior. And you have to learn enough of the other domain to understand its vocabulary, its principles, and what its practitioners are actually trying to find out, which is a genuine investment of time. The structural condition that makes it survive is that neither side is the other's contractor: each funds its own participation, so the collaboration continues only while it remains worth continuing to both. An arrangement held together by an obligation after one party has stopped benefiting is worse than no arrangement.
