# Design vocabulary is scale-relative: fix the integration level before arguing about the word

**Lesson:** Software's long-running definitional fights — what counts as an object, a service, a module, a component — are unresolvable in the form they are usually posed, because the terms are scale-relative and the disputants are standing at different scales. Nobody argues about whether "small" means the same thing to a physicist and a geologist; the adjective takes its meaning from the magnitude of the thing it modifies, and everyone accepts that without confusion. This work's proposal is that composition units behave the same way, and that the productive replacement for the definitional argument is a ladder of integration levels borrowed from hardware: gates, blocks, chips, cards, racks. At each rung the joinery is different, the appropriate coupling discipline is different, and the population of people who work there is different. The word means what the rung makes it mean.

The payoff of the ladder is division of labour, and specifically the kind that limits how much anyone has to hold in mind. Someone assembling from boards does not reason about doping profiles; someone building a board does not reason about the layout inside a chip. Each rung's joinery encapsulates a level of complexity so completely that the level below becomes something a consumer does not merely ignore by convention but has no way to reach. Software has all these rungs already, this work argues, scattered across mutually hostile languages rather than arranged as a stack, which is why the field keeps comparing tools that are not competitors and why any single language gets asked to serve constituencies with nothing in common.

The sharpest consequence sits near the top of the ladder, and it is a claim about control flow. A unit that does nothing until a caller lends it a thread is, from outside, indistinguishable from data with procedures attached — inert, sequential, and comprehensible only to someone who already thinks like a machine. Real-world parts do not work this way; they are all running at once. So the work identifies a distinct kind of unit that carries its own thread of control and communicates asynchronously through channels rather than by being called, closer to a coroutine or lightweight process than to a subroutine. Units of that kind compose concurrently by default, they do not need an inheritance story at all, and they admit a direct manipulation interface precisely because their independence is real rather than notional. A programmer who takes this seriously stops asking whether a design is properly object-oriented and starts asking, at each boundary, what granularity is in play, whether the units on either side own their own execution or borrow it, and which of those choices the surrounding language is silently forcing.
