# Standardize the artifact, and let the method that produces it stay free

**Lesson:** Software culture spends its standardization budget on procedure. We agree on languages, on methodologies, on review rituals, on the shape of a pipeline, and then hope that agreeing on how work is done will cause the work's outputs to become interchangeable. Every other engineering field inverted this long ago: it fixes the thing — a fastener with a stated thread pitch and load rating, a valve with a stated pressure tolerance — and leaves each supplier free to make it however they like. This work's central move is to notice that we picked the harder and less useful half of that pair, and that most of the arguments consuming the field's attention are arguments about process dressed up as arguments about quality.

The reason process standardization underdelivers is that a process is not the interface anyone actually consumes. When you standardize a process, only one contender can hold the title, so each new candidate has to defeat the incumbent across the whole industry before anyone can rely on it, and every level of an organization is forced to work in whatever tooling won that fight, regardless of whether it suits their skills or their problem. When you standardize an artifact instead, the property being fixed is exactly the one a downstream consumer needs, plurality below the interface becomes harmless rather than chaotic, and the consumer stops needing to hold any model of how the piece was made. That is the whole reason a homeowner can repair plumbing: not because plumbing is intrinsically simpler than software, but because someone already pushed the complexity of pumps and thermostats down a level and closed it off behind a stated specification. A field that never does this leaves every practitioner rebuilding the same substrate and reasoning about it from scratch, so nothing anyone learns about a component generalizes past the week they wrote it.

The test this work applies to its own reframe is worth stealing independently: a better frame is one under which a previously chaotic mess turns out to have simple structure. Judged that way, the product-centered view earns its keep, because the endless comparison of languages and methodologies stops being an unresolvable dispute over which is best and becomes a category error — a fight over process in a field that never got around to specifying its products. A programmer who takes this seriously changes what they push for on a team. Instead of proposing that everyone adopt the same framework, they propose that a boundary be given a stated, checkable contract and that whoever lives behind it be left alone. They treat "we all write it the same way" as a weak substitute for "the piece is specified well enough that how you wrote it stopped mattering." And when they see a component that nobody outside its authoring team can adopt, they look first for the missing consumer-facing specification rather than for a missing coding convention.
