# Build the mechanism on the event the machine can actually observe

**Lesson:** A great deal of engineering effort goes into detecting or preventing events that the substrate simply cannot see. When a mechanism is built on such an event, no amount of cleverness rescues it; every implementation is a partial approximation that leaks, and the leak is structural rather than a bug to be fixed in the next version. The move this work makes is to stop trying and to go looking for a nearby event the substrate observes trivially, then to redesign the mechanism around that instead. The instance it works through is that a piece of software has essentially no way to notice being duplicated, while counting its own invocations is close to free — so an economics built on duplication is doomed and an economics built on invocation is straightforward. The general form is a design question worth asking early: what can this layer see without being asked, and can the thing I want be expressed in terms of that?

The reframe has a second half that is easy to miss. Once the mechanism rests on the observable event, the previously unenforceable property stops being a liability and becomes usable. Free duplication, which under the old mechanism was the thing bleeding value out of the system, becomes the distribution channel, the advertising, and the reason a consumer's environment can acquire a missing dependency on demand without anyone making a purchasing decision first. That inversion is the tell for a good reframe: not that the leak has been plugged, but that the leak now does work for you. Programmers meet the same pattern whenever they try to enforce something above the layer that owns it — client-side validation of data the client controls, license checks in code the licensee can edit, quotas enforced by the caller.

The uncomfortable part is where the mechanism has to live. If the measurement is the foundation of everything above it, then it has to be established somewhere the parties it constrains cannot reach, which in this work's proposal means dedicated tamper-resistant hardware rather than software, on the reasoning that a software implementation of the metering is exactly as trustworthy as the least trustworthy party running it. That is a general constraint on trust: a property can only be relied upon by parties who cannot forge it, so the enforcing mechanism must sit strictly below every party it binds. A programmer reasoning this way asks two questions of any guarantee they are about to build on. Which physical or privileged layer actually establishes this, and who is capable of editing that layer. If the answer to the second includes the party being constrained, the guarantee is decoration. The same reasoning also cautions that a mechanism accurate enough to be useful is accurate enough to be repurposed, so usage data collected for one purpose has to be treated as data that will eventually be asked for by someone with a different one.

**Source:** [Superdistribution?](../works/superdistribution.md) — the central asymmetry the piece is built on, that an electronic artifact cannot monitor its own copying but can readily monitor its own use, together with the argument that treating ease of replication as an asset rather than a liability follows from it; the discussion of why the metering apparatus is placed in tamper-resistant silicon, and the reply to a correspondent's piracy objection, carry the trust-layer half of the lesson.
