# Deciding when a connection is checked decides who is allowed to make it

**Lesson:** Static checking is usually argued about as a correctness question: does catching a mismatch earlier make the program safer. This work reframes it as a question about authority and reach. If a container's element type must be resolved when the container is compiled, then the container's author has to name, in advance, the set of things anyone will ever put in it. That is a demand to see the future. It is satisfiable when the author and the user are the same person on the same afternoon, and it becomes impossible the moment the piece is meant to be compiled once and handed to consumers the author will never meet, whose types do not exist yet. Deferring the check to the moment of use is what converts a piece of code from something a team shares by recompiling their whole world into something that can be shipped as a finished artifact.

The framing that makes this precise is the distinction between treating a program as a sealed world, where all interactions among parts are declarable when the compiler builds that world, and treating it as an open one, where a part meets its collaborators only in the environment it is eventually installed into. The sealed model buys real things: a rejected mismatch before execution, and a mechanical shape that maps well onto the machine, since a call resolved at compile time is a jump and a call resolved at use time is a lookup. Those are the machine's virtues, and they are worth having at the granularity where machine efficiency dominates. What the sealed model cannot buy is a component whose users were unknown at the time it was built. So the honest position is neither preference. It is that the appropriate binding time varies by where in a producer-consumer stack the connection sits, and that a language offering exactly one coupling discipline has decided that question on the programmer's behalf for every level at once.

This is the reason the work treats a single-mechanism language as deficient regardless of which mechanism it chose. A tightly coupled language lacks the loose joinery needed at the level where independently built parts meet; a uniformly late-bound environment lacks the tight joinery needed where the code must sit close to the hardware. The interesting design target is an environment where several coupling disciplines coexist and the programmer picks per joint according to what that joint has to survive. A programmer who internalizes this stops asking whether a language is statically or dynamically typed as though it were a moral property, and starts asking, joint by joint, whether both ends of this connection are known to the same author at the same time. Where they are, bind early and take the speed and the checking. Where they are not — plugin boundaries, extension points, anything published as a binary or crossing an organizational line — recognize that early binding there is not extra safety but a hard limit on who can ever participate, and that a way of stating the requirement without naming the implementations is the thing actually needed.
