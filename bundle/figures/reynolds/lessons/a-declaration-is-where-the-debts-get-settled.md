# A declaration is a settlement point: it grants a fixed set of facts and pays off exactly the debts naming it

**Lesson:** Reasoning that judges a fragment in isolation is out of step with how anyone actually reads code. A reader moving inward through a program collects information as they descend: each declaration tells them something about the name being introduced, and everything inside the scope may be understood in the light of it. A method that infers a fragment's properties without reference to where the fragment sits throws that accumulated information away and then wonders why the fragment cannot be characterized. The fix is to make the accumulation explicit — every claim carries the conditions it needs, and the enclosing constructs are what supply them.

That gives declarations a role they are rarely credited with. A declaration is not primarily a request for storage; it is the point at which a specific, finite bundle of facts about a name becomes available, and simultaneously the point at which every outstanding obligation mentioning that name is settled. Work inside the scope runs up debts — this name behaves properly, this name does not collide with that one — and the declaration is where they are paid, because the thing being introduced is fresh by construction and so all of those facts are true of it. What is left over, the conditions that do not mention the name at all, passes outward unchanged to be settled somewhere further out or, eventually, at the program's boundary.

The part that repays attention is that there is no discretion in the settlement. Which obligations a declaration can discharge is determined entirely by the form of the declaration and by which names appear in each obligation; you cannot choose to absorb an inconvenient condition, and you cannot fail to absorb a qualifying one. This has a practical consequence and a design consequence. Practically, it gives an exact reading of what a declaration is worth: it tells you everything you may assume about the new name, and nothing else, which is a much sharper statement than "the variable is local." As a design principle, it says that when the residual obligations at the outermost level are large, that is a real measurement of how much the code demands of its surroundings — and the way to reduce it is to move declarations inward, so that more of the demand gets settled locally instead of escaping.

**Source:** [The Craft of Programming](../works/the-craft-of-programming.md) — the opening of Section 3.3.7, which identifies as the fundamental shortcoming of the earlier chapters' method that a statement's specification is always inferred independently of its context, contrasts this with a reader descending through a block-structured program and using each declaration's information about the identifier being declared, and states that in specification logic this information is conveyed by assumptions; the rule for simple variable declarations with its side condition that the identifier not occur free in the surviving assumptions or in the pre- and postconditions, on the grounds that the identifier's meaning outside the block is unrelated to its meaning inside; the worked discharge of the good-variable and non-interference assumptions by an integer declaration; and the remark that there is no choice about which assumptions a declaration discharges, since the rule tells us everything we can assume about the identifier and nothing else. Also Section 3.3.6's observation that a tableau must now be prefaced with the union of the assumptions used at each step, and that each assignment introduces assumptions later eliminated by declarations and other binding mechanisms.
