# Duplication that refuses to factor means you are missing a kind of parameter, not a clever trick

**Lesson:** A recurring shape of impasse: one option forces you to write the same routine again for every kind of thing it operates on, the other lets you write it once but destroys the checking that made the first option worth having. Sorting is the standard example — a separate sorted-array routine per element kind, or one routine and no static guarantee at any call site. Confronted with two unacceptable options, the wrong instinct is to argue about which is less bad, and the right one is to ask what the two versions of the duplicated routine actually differ in. If they differ in something your language cannot take as a parameter, the impasse is not a trade-off, it is a missing parameter kind, and the fix is to introduce one rather than to pick a side. Here the thing being varied is a type, so types become passable — a category of argument that did not previously exist.

Adding a parameter kind is only admissible if it comes with a rule restricting its use, and the restriction is what preserves the property you refused to give up. A caller may hand over a type, so the checking that used to depend on knowing the type concretely must now be recoverable from the abstracted form alone, which constrains how the parameter may be mentioned inside the body. The specific constraint worth generalizing concerns what may cross the boundary from outside: anything in the surrounding context whose own type refers to the name being abstracted must be excluded, because inside the abstraction that name means something different from what it meant outside. A boundary that admits references whose *types* depend on the thing being abstracted is not merely leaky in a scoping-hygiene sense; it is incoherent, since the same name denotes two different things on the two sides. When designing any encapsulation, the closure condition to check is dependency through types, not just direct mention.

The compensation for the added parameter kind is that a separate feature you were about to design disappears. Introducing a type with a chosen representation and its own operations is, once types can be passed, an ordinary application: abstract the region that uses the type over both the type and the operations, then apply it to a representation and implementations. What looked like two language features — generic routines and programmer-defined abstract types — is one, and the second needs no mechanism of its own. Two observations follow. Prefer the parameter-passing formulation as the primitive, because the derived feature then inherits its guarantees automatically. But do not ship the primitive as the interface: the raw form is genuinely hard to read, and notation that names the intent belongs on top of it. Compactness of the core and usability of the surface are separate goals, and conflating them costs you one of them.

**Source:** [Towards a Theory of Type Structure](../works/towards-a-theory-of-type-structure.md) — the introduction's polymorphic-sort problem posed as the dilemma between per-type duplication and loss of syntactic checking, resolved by admitting types as a restricted new kind of parameter; the illustrative-language section deriving a complex-number representation as an ordinary application of a type-abstracted expression, with its own remark that the result is hard to read and wants syntactic sugaring; and the side condition in the syntax of type abstraction, explained by the observation that the bound type name means something different inside than outside.
