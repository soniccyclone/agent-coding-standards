# Give every jump destination a precondition, and a non-local exit becomes an ordinary obligation

**Lesson:** Control that leaves a fragment other than by falling off its end looks like it defeats before-and-after reasoning, since there is no "after" to describe. The way through is to stop treating the departure as an event at the departure point and treat it as an obligation owed to the arrival point. Attach to each destination a condition on the state — what must be true of any computation that shows up there — and the rule for a jump becomes simple: it is legitimate exactly when the state where it occurs satisfies the destination's condition. Nothing about the jump's own aftermath enters into it, because there is none; the fragment's postcondition can be anything at all, and choosing the strongest, unsatisfiable one is a useful way of recording that control never continues past that point.

Two structural facts make this work. The first is that a destination's condition must be established by *every* route that can reach it, the falling-through route as well as every jump, so it is a genuine meeting point where several lines of reasoning converge — which is also exactly what makes it a good place for a comment. The second is that the condition is not a property of the jumping fragment, it is a property of the surroundings the fragment sits in. A departure statement in isolation says nothing; the same text is correct in one context and wrong in another. Anything that reasons about fragments purely on their own terms therefore cannot handle non-local control at all, and the extension you need is the ability for a claim to depend on what the surrounding names mean.

Reasoning about a whole region then follows the same pattern as recursion: assume each destination's condition while checking each piece, and the assumption is discharged when the region is closed off, because the conditions were chosen to be exactly the pieces' own preconditions. And the design guidance falls out of the same picture. A departure whose purpose is to simulate a structured construct the language already provides is pure loss, since the destination's condition ends up restating what the construct would have given you. A departure that abandons remaining work on a condition — quit the scan, the answer is found — expresses something the structured forms genuinely cannot say, and the resulting annotation is short and informative. Judge the construct by whether the destination's condition is easy to state.
