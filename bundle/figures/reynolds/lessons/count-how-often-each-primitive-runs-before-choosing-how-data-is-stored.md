# There is no best representation, only a best fit to a frequency profile you have to measure first

**Lesson:** Ask which layout is best for a collection and the question has no answer, because the candidate layouts are complementary rather than ranked. One makes membership tests and single-element edits constant but forces a full sweep of the universe to find an arbitrary member or count what is present. Another makes emptiness and size immediate but pays linearly for membership. A third buys logarithmic lookup and gives back linear insertion. Every one of them is optimal for something and terrible for something else, and the something-else is not an implementation defect you could fix with more care — it is the structural price of the thing it made fast. So a representation argument conducted in the abstract, over the operations a data type could support, is unwinnable by construction.

What makes it winnable is a count. Once you have a description of the algorithm at the level of the problem's own concepts, you can bound how many times each primitive is invoked — this one runs once at startup, that one runs at most once per edge, this other one at most once per vertex — and those bounds are the entire input to the decision. They convert an unanswerable comparison of layouts into an arithmetic comparison of totals. This is worth naming as the actual deliverable of the abstract phase. People think the abstract program exists to be proved correct; correctness is one output, and the operation frequency profile is the other, and the second is what makes the next phase a calculation instead of a preference. Notice this also means the counts are the last thing you can learn before representation is fixed: past that point, times and space are dominated by layout, and the abstract description has nothing further to say about cost.

Two consequences follow that people resist. First, different variables of the same type should get different representations when their profiles differ, and refusing on grounds of uniformity is choosing a worse program for the sake of a symmetry nothing needs. Second, a value that crosses the boundary out of your program does not have a profile you own — its consumer's access pattern is half the input, and the honest options are to accept the consumer's preference, to maintain the value in two representations at once, or to convert on the way out. Picking your own preferred layout for an output and calling it done is not a decision, it is a decision deferred onto somebody who cannot revisit it.

**Source:** [The Craft of Programming](../works/the-craft-of-programming.md) — Section 5.1.3's four representations of a finite set (array enumeration with and without duplicates, ordered array, characteristic vector), introduced with the statement that no universally best way exists and that optimizing one primitive inevitably penalizes another, so a wise choice depends on which primitives the abstract program uses and with what relative frequencies, and that distinct variables of the same type often warrant distinct representations; together with Section 5.1.2's derivation of execution bounds for each part of the reachability program and the remark that these bounds are as close as one can come to understanding efficiency at the abstract level yet are crucial for deciding representations, and Section 5.1.4's caution that because the result set is an output its representation must suit the downstream consumer, which in a real application might force a different choice, simultaneous computation in more than one representation, or conversion after the fact.
