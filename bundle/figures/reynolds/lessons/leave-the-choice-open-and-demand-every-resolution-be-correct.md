# Leave a choice unmade when it cannot affect correctness, and require every resolution of it to work

**Lesson:** At many points in a design you face a decision that has to be recorded in the artifact even though nothing depends on it — which of two equally informative tests to perform, which of two equally valid steps to take next. Making such a choice costs you twice. A reader cannot tell it apart from the choices that matter, so every arbitrary commitment adds to the pile of things that look load-bearing and are not; and the commitment usually destroys a symmetry that was present before it. The alternative is to describe the artifact in a form that permits both, and to say plainly that either is acceptable. What you get back is not just brevity: the version that declines to choose often displays a structure the committed version conceals, because the structure was the symmetry the choice broke.

Deferring choices is worth much more when the reason you cannot decide yet is that the information has not arrived. A design expressed at the level of the problem's own concepts frequently has decisions in it that only become answerable once a representation is settled — which of two equivalent traversals is cheaper depends on how the structure is laid out. Committing early means either guessing or dragging representation questions up into a description that was supposed to be free of them. Leaving the description open lets each choice be settled where the knowledge to settle it actually exists. And some choices are never yours: when work is genuinely spread across independent devices or processes, which of several possible orders occurs is decided by the world, and a description that admits only one of them is simply wrong about what it models.

All of this rests on one discipline, and getting it wrong turns the technique into a trap. An artifact that permits several behaviours is correct only if *every* behaviour it permits is correct. This is worth stating explicitly because a different and superficially similar notion is in circulation, under which permitting several behaviours means succeeding if some one of them succeeds — a useful idea in its own setting and a catastrophe here. Under the demanding reading, leaving a choice open is a strengthening of your obligation, not a weakening of it: you have promised more, and in exchange you have earned the right not to decide.

**Source:** [The Craft of Programming](../works/the-craft-of-programming.md) — Section 4.2.6 on transition diagrams and indeterminacy, which defines a node as indeterminate when more than one outgoing arc is permissible for some state, holds that such a diagram is correct if every behaviour it allows meets the specification, exhibits the exponentiation diagram made indeterminate by declining to choose between the two tests at one node, and gives three reasons for the growing importance of indeterminacy — intelligibility gained by avoiding unnecessary choices that do not affect correctness, the need to leave an abstract program indeterminate so that choices requiring knowledge of the data representation can be postponed, and parallel processing where the program actually executed may be indeterminate — closing with the emphasis that an indeterminate program is correct only if all of its possible executions are correct, in contrast with the automata-theory and artificial-intelligence notion under which some execution suffices. Also Section 4.2.7's merging diagram, where leaving two nodes indeterminate reveals the symmetry between the two input segments as a reflection of the diagram along a diagonal.
