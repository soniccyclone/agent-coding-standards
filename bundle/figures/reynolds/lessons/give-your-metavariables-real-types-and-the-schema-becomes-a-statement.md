# Give the metavariables real types and the rule schema collapses into an ordinary statement

**Lesson:** A rule stated as a pattern — "for any statement S and any condition P, if this holds then that holds" — lives one level above the thing it talks about. The placeholders are not part of the object language; they are notation in your head, and instantiating them is an operation only you can perform. That extra level is a real cost. It means you need a second set of conventions governing substitution into patterns, that the patterns cannot be composed with the objects they describe, and that nothing checks whether a given instantiation was legitimate. Collapse the level instead: admit identifiers whose type is *statement*, whose type is *condition*, whose type is *claim*, and the rule stops being a pattern and becomes a single ordinary statement about those identifiers. Applying it becomes the same substitution operation you already have, subject to the same type-correctness discipline.

Getting there requires letting the specification language be strictly larger than the executable one rather than a mirror of it. It must share the executable language's binding discipline exactly — same notion of scope, same insensitivity to the choice of bound names, same substitution — because the whole point is that one substitution mechanism serves both. But it must also contain things the machine cannot run: quantifiers over infinite ranges, data types with no representation, and above all abstraction over the new kinds of phrase, so that a concept used in specifications can be given a name, parameterized, and reused exactly the way a procedure is. The specification language gets the same factoring tools as the program, which is what stops specifications from being long and unreadable as they scale.

One discipline is non-negotiable once you do this. Because the same text can typecheck under more than one assignment of types to its free names, and may be valid under some assignments and false under others, the types of the free identifiers are part of the claim and must be written down. This is the direct analogue of the fact that a phrase's admissible contexts are determined by what its binder established. It also completes a pleasing circle: rules about the language are now sentences in the language, and the machinery that keeps ordinary programs from being combined nonsensically is the same machinery that keeps rules from being instantiated nonsensically.

**Source:** [The Craft of Programming](../works/the-craft-of-programming.md) — Section 3.3.3, which insists that the languages of assertions and specifications be as similar as possible to the programming language with identifiers having the same kinds of meaning and binding and substitution behaving the same way, then adds assertion and assertion-procedure phrase types so that the while-statement inference rule of Chapter 1 can be written as a single universal specification over identifiers of type logical expression, statement and assertion and recovered by ordinary type-correct substitution; the definition of an ordering concept as an assertion procedure and its generalization to one parameterized by an arbitrary relation supplied as another assertion procedure; the note that assertions may be uncomputable in principle and so cannot occur in executable phrases, and that data types absent from executable programs will be used; and the closing emphasis that the phrase types of free identifiers must be stated because a specification may be syntactically correct under several type assignments yet universal under only some.
