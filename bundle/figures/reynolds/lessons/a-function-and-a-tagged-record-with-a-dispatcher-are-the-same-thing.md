# A function is a tagged record of its free variables plus one dispatcher, and the conversion is mechanical

**Lesson:** Any program that passes functions around can be rewritten, by rote, into one that does not. For each place in the text where a function value is created, introduce a distinct kind of record whose fields hold exactly the free variables that expression's body refers to; replace the expression with construction of that record; and write one dispatcher which, given a record and an argument, branches on which kind it is and executes the corresponding body with the fields rebound. Every application of a function value becomes a call to the dispatcher. Two facts make this worth knowing beyond the trick itself. The information needed to represent a function is precisely its text plus the values its body borrows from the surrounding context — nothing more — and the number of distinct record kinds is bounded by the number of places in the program that create functions, which is finite even though the number of functions created at runtime is not.

The methodological point is that the transformation should be carried out blind, driven only by the syntax and without regard for what the program is for, and only afterwards interpreted. Do it that way and the records you are forced to invent turn out to be the artifacts you would otherwise have designed by hand and argued about: the pairing of code with captured bindings that implementations call a closure; a linked list of single bindings, terminated by an empty one, with lookup walking the chain; and when the same procedure is applied to control information, a linked list of pending instructions, each accepting a current value and producing the next — that is, a stack of activation records, and a program that is a state-transition machine rather than a recursive one. Deriving these rather than inventing them changes their status from design choices you must defend to consequences you can verify.

The same derivation explains something otherwise puzzling: why competing machine-like designs for the same language differ in fussy, seemingly arbitrary details. The representations chosen during the transformation are correct but not unique, so every arbitrary decision made along the way shows up as a cog in the resulting machine. Different choices give a different machine that is equivalent. So when you meet several designs that agree on behaviour and disagree on structure, suspect they are all first-order representations of one higher-order formulation, and look for that formulation — it is where the content is, and the differences you were comparing are noise. A related habit: when a mechanical transformation produces something your language does not permit, read the illegal construct charitably rather than backing out. Here it demands a record whose own field points at itself, and once you accept that reading you notice the self-reference is never used for anything but recovering the record you already have, so the field can simply be deleted.

**Source:** [Definitional Interpreters for Higher-Order Programming Languages](../works/definitional-interpreters-for-higher-order-programming-languages.md) — section 6's elimination of higher-order functions by tabulating each function-producing expression with its global variables and introducing dispatchers, its remark that the transformation was justified without regard to the meaning of the program being transformed followed by the identification of the resulting records with closures and with linked-list environments, the derivation and subsequent deletion of the cyclic environment field, section 7's identification of the defunctionalized continuations as a list of instructions making the interpreter a state-transition machine, and section 8's observation that the machine's cogs and wheels are artifacts of non-unique representation choices.
