# Fix a component's contract at exactly the set of connections it permits — the right level of description is neither what it computes nor what shape it has

**Lesson:** Ask what a component's declared interface is *for* and the usual answers are both wrong. "It documents what the component does" is too much: a description rich enough to pin down the computed result is a second implementation, and maintaining two implementations of the same thing is how they diverge. "It records the shapes of the values crossing the boundary" is too little, and answers a question nobody asked — the shapes are a means, not the point. The productive answer is that a contract exists to determine, without opening the component, precisely which ways of wiring it into a larger system will work and which will fail. Nothing else. Read that way, a contract is not a summary of the interior at all; it is the complete enumeration of admissible connections, and its correct level of detail is derived rather than chosen — as coarse as possible while still deciding every wiring question, as fine as necessary and no finer.

Two things fall out immediately, and both are things people usually try to get by other means. Substitutability becomes definitional instead of aspirational: if the contract *is* the set of legal connections, then two components sharing one are interchangeable in every context by construction, so swapping a failing implementation for a working one, or a correct one for a faster one, needs no further argument. And the boundary becomes genuinely opaque with no loss — you are entitled to know only whether you may connect and how, never what is inside, and that entitlement is exactly enough. The discipline this imposes is uncomfortable in a good way: any fact about a component that callers depend on but the contract does not state is a latent breakage, and any fact the contract states that no caller could use is dead weight that will eventually constrain a reimplementation for no reason.

The same view answers a design question that otherwise gets settled by accretion. When a system's vocabulary is too weak, the tempting fix is to keep bolting on whatever is missing — this type of collection, then that one, then trees, then the one your current project needs. Each addition is locally justified and the sequence never ends, and what it costs is precisely the modularity it was meant to serve, because every addition is one more thing every consumer of the system must know about. The alternative is to add a single mechanism for abstraction and derive the rest inside the system, so that a construction written once is instantiable at whatever the caller needs. The reason this works is the same reason as before: a general construction's contract quantifies over the connections rather than enumerating them, so one component can serve a family of situations that would otherwise each have demanded their own primitive.

For a programmer the concrete habit is to write interfaces by working backwards from composition failures. Ask what a caller could get wrong, make the contract exactly strong enough to reject that, and stop — resisting both the urge to narrate the algorithm and the urge to leak internal representation "in case someone needs it."

**Source:** [Proofs and Types](../works/proofs-and-types.md) — the discussion of operational significance in the Curry-Howard chapter, which asks whether a type says too much or too little and settles on reading it as a specification of admissible pluggings between closed units, with substitutability for repair or optimization as the stated payoff; and the opening of the chapter on Gödel's system, which contrasts endlessly adding built-in data types against adding one abstraction scheme, on modularity grounds.
