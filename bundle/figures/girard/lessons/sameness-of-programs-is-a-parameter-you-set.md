# "Same program" is a parameter you set, not a fact you discover — and where you set it decides what you can decide

**Lesson:** Ask whether two pieces of code are the same thing and you will be handed an answer as though the question had one. It does not. If sameness means agreeing on all inputs and outputs, the relation has an unambiguous meaning but is useless to work with and can hinge on facts nobody knows — you can write down two procedures built from visibly different ideas whose input-output behaviour coincides exactly if some unresolved mathematical conjecture happens to be true. Whether they are "the same" is then hostage to a question about number theory that has nothing to do with either of them. If instead sameness means agreeing up to a stock of admitted rewritings, the relation becomes usable — but only once you have chosen the stock, and the choice is yours. Admit commutativity of addition and the two obvious ways of summing a pair become the same procedure; withhold it and they are permanently distinct. Neither answer is discovered. Both are consequences of a decision.

Once you see the decision, you can see what it buys and costs, which is the useful part. The stock of admitted rewritings is exactly the dial that sets which programs your tooling can identify, and turning it up does not monotonically improve anything. Adding a plausible extra rewriting can hand you something you wanted cheaply — the source notes one that produces a serviceable notion of behavioural equality almost for free — while costing simple structural properties you were relying on elsewhere, so the model you obtain is adequate in the narrow sense and disappointing in every other. Turning the dial also moves the boundary of the decidable: with one setting, deciding whether two things are the same is a finite calculation, and with another it stops being one. Worth noticing that the decidable settings are not the naive ones, and that the relation being computable at all is a consequence of hard structural results rather than something the definition gives you.

The stance this produces is that a notion of program equivalence is a designed artifact with a specification, and its specification should be written down. Which distinctions must the relation preserve, because downstream reasoning depends on them? Which must it collapse, because otherwise you drown in irrelevant variation? Is the result something a machine can decide, and if not, are you prepared for that? These are answerable questions, and answering them deliberately is different from inheriting whatever equivalence your tooling happens to implement — which is the normal situation, and the reason people are routinely surprised by what their tools consider identical.

A programmer working this way stops asking whether two implementations are equivalent and starts asking equivalent *with respect to what*. It changes how you read a refactoring, a compiler optimization, a caching layer, or a test that asserts two computations agree: each of them silently commits to an equivalence, and the commitment — not the code — is where the interesting risk lives.

**Source:** [Interprétation fonctionnelle et élimination des coupures de l'arithmétique d'ordre supérieur](../works/interpretation-fonctionnelle-et-elimination-des-coupures.md) — the opening section's contrast between graph-based and algorithmic identity, including the pair of procedures whose extensional agreement depends on an unrelated open conjecture and the observation that algorithmic identity depends essentially on which simple transformations are admitted; and the introduction's discussion of adjoining further reduction rules, which notes that the choice of rules answers to no clear necessity, that one such addition yields a cheap behavioural model at the cost of the properties one actually wanted, and that identity of normal forms is decidable without being primitive recursive.
