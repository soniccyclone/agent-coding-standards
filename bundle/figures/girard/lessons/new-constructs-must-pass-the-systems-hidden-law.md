# Extending a system is not free composition: every construct owes a matching destructor, and whether yours qualifies is a test rather than a taste

**Lesson:** There is a widespread belief that a formal system is a bag of stipulations — that its rules were chosen by convention and you may therefore add more by the same right. That belief is false in a way you can check. Underneath a well-made system sits a law relating each way of building a thing to each way of taking it apart, and the law is not decorative: it is what guarantees that computation in the system terminates, that results are unique, that the system's meaning does not collapse. So when you propose a new construct, you are not proposing a stipulation. You are proposing to preserve or break an invariant, and there is a concrete criterion for which. The criterion is whether every confrontation between building and tearing down can be locally resolved — whether a construction immediately followed by its matching destruction can always be rewritten into something simpler, in every combination, with no residue.

The practical force of this is a rule about symmetry of obligation. If you introduce a way of producing a value, you owe a way of consuming it that lines up with it exactly, one clause per construction case, and vice versa. Additions that violate this are recognizable by a specific symptom: their consumers need to be told something extraneous — a target that has no structural relationship to the thing being consumed — and once that extraneous parameter is in the notation, it starts multiplying. Every pairing of your ill-formed consumer with every other consumer becomes a separate rewriting case that must be handled, and the case count grows as a product rather than a sum. Twenty-one special cases is not a lot of work to grind through; it is a diagnosis. And the correct response is not to grind, but to notice that the awkward construct is not sacred and its rules can be redesigned, and meanwhile to state the general case, which is invariably more comprehensible than any of its specializations.

The complementary trap is adding capability by fiat when you cannot find the lawful version. It is always possible to bolt a needed operation onto a system as a primitive with hand-written behavior. It will work, and it will quietly cost you the ability to reason about the system, because the addition does not correspond to anything in the structure that made reasoning possible — so every subsequent proof, and every subsequent extension, has to carry it as a special case. Systems built this way are also brittle in a specific direction: they tolerate changes that respect the underlying structure and break under changes that do not, which is exactly backwards from how brittleness usually feels, because the offending change looks harmless.

For a programmer the discipline is to treat "can I add this?" as an empirical question about an existing invariant rather than a question of API taste. Find the law your system actually relies on — the pairing between constructors and eliminators, the round-trip that must hold, the normalization your tooling assumes — and make satisfying it a precondition for merging a new primitive. Features that cannot satisfy it are still buildable, on top, in terms of the ones that can.
