# When a property is out of reach for the whole system, prove it uniformly on finitely generated slices and lift the family

**Lesson:** Suppose the property you need cannot be established for your system with the resources you have — not because you have been insufficiently clever but for a structural reason you have already identified. The usual responses are to weaken the claim until it becomes provable, or to import heavier machinery and accept the resulting dependency. There is a third route, and it is often the best one. Carve the system into slices, each generated from a finite amount of data; prove the property for an arbitrary slice, in a way that uses only a bounded amount of reasoning power *independent of which slice*; and then use a lifting principle to turn that indexed family of local results into the single global statement you wanted, inside the weak setting after all. What was globally out of reach is recovered as a uniform local result plus one act of aggregation.

The load-bearing word is *uniform*, and honoring it is the whole discipline. It is easy to prove a property slice by slice with an argument that grows as the slices grow — and useless, because then the family has no finite common description and there is nothing to lift. The work is in arranging the decomposition so that a fixed, bounded set of assumptions suffices for every slice, with the growth absorbed by the *data* generating the slice rather than by the *reasoning* about it. Here that arrangement is what a substitution property buys: because the general case can be reconstructed from the finitely many generators by mechanical substitution, the seemingly unbounded stock of assumptions needed across all slices collapses to the finitely many attached to the generators. Getting that collapse is the design problem; once you have it, the aggregation step is routine.

Two consequences are worth carrying. The first is that this reframes what a bounded-resource proof is *for*. A proof that only works on bounded pieces is not a weaker version of the theorem you wanted; it is the input to a construction that yields the theorem you wanted, and the decomposition should be designed with the lifting step already in mind rather than discovered afterwards. The second is a caution the source is explicit about: the lifting principle is not free, and the cost of applying it is itself sensitive to the property being lifted, growing with the complexity of the statement. There are cases where the aggregation genuinely cannot be performed from inside a slice, and that limitation is informative rather than embarrassing — it locates exactly which strength the global claim requires and why no rearrangement of the local proofs will supply it.

For a programmer this is the honest version of "prove it modularly." The temptation is to verify components and assert that the system is therefore correct, which is a wish rather than an argument. The real pattern is to define the decomposition so that each piece is generated from finite data, establish the property for an arbitrary piece with reasoning that does not scale with the piece, and then state explicitly what principle carries you from all-pieces to the whole. If you cannot name that last principle, you have a family of local results and no theorem.

**Source:** [Interprétation fonctionnelle et élimination des coupures de l'arithmétique d'ordre supérieur](../works/interpretation-fonctionnelle-et-elimination-des-coupures.md) — the introduction's account of local formalizability and the reflection scheme as a way to obtain results not globally formalizable, and the third part's section proving the normalization property for finite subsystems, where a substitution lemma reduces an unbounded stock of required assumptions to the finitely many attached to the generating set, followed by aggregation; together with the closing remark that the lifting step's cost grows with the complexity of the statement lifted.
