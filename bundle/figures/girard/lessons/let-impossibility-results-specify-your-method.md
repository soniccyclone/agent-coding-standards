# Before investing in an argument, ask what known limit tells you where it must live — and disqualify any method that would also establish something false

**Lesson:** Impossibility theorems are usually filed under discouragement — the things you are told you cannot have. Used properly they are the opposite: they are the cheapest available specification for a method you have not built yet. The pattern is to notice that the property you want to establish would, if established, imply something a limit theorem says cannot be established by tools of a certain strength. That deduction costs you an afternoon and buys you a hard constraint: every argument formalizable within those tools is guaranteed to fail, so you can stop generating them. More than that, the constraint is *positive*, because it names the specific capability your argument is missing and therefore must import. Knowing in advance that your reasoning has to reach beyond a given level of expressive power is what stops the search from being blind, and it is available before you have written a line of the proof.

The companion check is even cheaper and even more often skipped. When a candidate method occurs to you, before asking whether it works, ask what else it would prove. If the same reasoning, applied unchanged, would also establish the property for a nearby variant of your system where the property is known to be false, the method is dead — and it is dead independently of whether you can locate the step that is wrong. This is a genuinely different kind of refutation from finding a hole. It says the method cannot possibly be sensitive to the thing that distinguishes the true case from the false one, so any apparent success is bookkeeping. Applied habitually it saves enormous amounts of work, because the attractive naive approaches to hard invariants are almost always insensitive in exactly this way.

What survives these two filters tends to be structurally unlike what you set out to prove, and the departure is deliberate rather than reluctant. Instead of reasoning directly about the property you want, you define a stronger, more logically involved property indexed by the structure of the objects, arrange it so that it visibly entails what you wanted, and then prove the strong version by induction — accepting that its statement is harder to hold in your head, because that difficulty is the imported strength. This is the specific move that combinatorial intuition cannot reach: intuition looks for a simple invariant, and a simple invariant is precisely one weak enough to be handled by the tools you already proved insufficient. It is also worth being honest that the exact conditions such a property must satisfy are typically not derived from first principles. They are found by trial, kept because they survive every case, and only later understood.

For a working engineer the transfer is direct and unglamorous. When a correctness argument, a test strategy, or a static analysis keeps almost working, stop iterating on it and ask two questions: is there a known result that says something of this shape cannot succeed, and would my approach also "prove" the buggy version of this system correct? A yes to either is a signal to change categories rather than try harder, and both questions are answerable in far less time than one more attempt.
