# Definable at every instance is not definable generically — the quantifier adds a constraint that pointwise evidence cannot see

**Lesson:** Here is a reasoning error that feels airtight. You have an operation you want to offer generically. You check it: for this concrete instantiation it is implementable, and for that one, and in fact for every concrete instantiation you can name it is implementable, by an obvious construction. So you conclude the generic version exists and add it to your vocabulary. The conclusion does not follow. A generic operation is not the family of its instances; it is a single object required to behave the same way at all of them, and that requirement is a genuine additional constraint which no amount of instance-by-instance evidence can test. The instances can each be satisfiable while nothing satisfies all of them at once in the coherent way genericity demands.

What makes this worth internalizing rather than merely noting is how the failure presents itself. It does not show up as a missing case or a type error at the point of the addition — everything looks fine, and the operation's intended meaning seems perfectly clear. It shows up somewhere else entirely, as the loss of a global property the whole system was built to have. In the case examined here, a small scheme with an obvious reading, individually definable at each closed instantiation, is admitted uniformly, and the immediate consequence is that termination fails: one can construct a term that feeds the generic operation to itself and cycles forever. The generic form gave you exactly the self-application the instance forms could not express, and self-application is where guarantees go to die. Notice also that the usual proof technique goes silent rather than shouting — the argument you would use to establish soundness becomes circular, requiring you to know the answer for an arbitrary instantiation before you can define the answer at all. Circularity in your own soundness argument is a symptom, not merely an inconvenience.

The positive content is that genericity has a *price*, and the price is a uniformity obligation that deserves to be stated. Something offered for all instantiations must do the same thing at all of them, which means it cannot inspect what it has been instantiated with and branch on the answer. Case analysis is available at the level of ordinary arguments and unavailable at the level of what you have abstracted over — and the tempting operations to add generically are precisely the ones that quietly need to look. Being unable to make that vague requirement fully precise is not an excuse to ignore it; the source is candid that a general account of uniformity was an open problem, and that the boundaries of the possible were being mapped by negative results — impossibility proofs showing that certain generic capabilities, such as a single equality test usable at every instantiation, cannot exist in any model at all.

A programmer who takes this on stops treating "it works for every type I tried" as evidence that a generic version is sound, and starts asking what the generic version would let a caller do that no single instantiation permits. Usually the answer is self-application, reflection, or comparison across instantiations — and that is exactly where the guarantee you were relying on breaks.

**Source:** [Interprétation fonctionnelle et élimination des coupures de l'arithmétique d'ordre supérieur](../works/interpretation-fonctionnelle-et-elimination-des-coupures.md) — the counterexample appendix to the second part, which exhibits a scheme definable at each closed instantiation, shows that admitting it uniformly permits a self-applying non-terminating term, and explains why the normalization argument becomes circular in that case; together with the introduction's discussion of objects of universal type as necessarily uniform over instantiations, and the appendices deriving impossibility of a universal equality test.
