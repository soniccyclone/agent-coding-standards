# An answer and the question it answers are different objects — a formalism that identifies them has thrown away the half you get paid for

**Lesson:** Write down an arithmetic identity and notice what the equals sign is doing. If the two sides were genuinely the same thing, nobody would ever have needed to assert their equality — the assertion is only interesting because getting from one side to the other took work. So the two sides are not the same object. They agree on the thing they ultimately name, and differ in what you must do to arrive at it. The received tradition in logic made a deliberate choice here: keep only what is named, discard the arriving. That choice paid off spectacularly for its own purposes, which is why it became invisible as a choice. But it means the mainstream account of what a function *is* — a completed table of argument-result pairs — is not merely an abstraction over computation, it is a claim no machine can honor. The table is infinite; the memory is not. What actually exists in the machine is a bounded process, and the process is the thing the standard account has no vocabulary for.

The consequence for design is that "same behavior" is a much weaker relation than it appears, and the difference lives exactly where engineering happens. Two components can name the same input-output relation and be nothing alike in what they cost, when they demand their arguments, whether they can be interrupted, or how they compose under concurrency. A formalism that can only talk about the named relation will call them equal and go quiet on every question you actually had. That is not a small gap. The properties that matter for real software — that a component can be substituted safely, that composing two of them does not blow up, that a modification here does not corrupt behavior there — belong to the discarded half. Historically, the neglected tradition was rescued from irrelevance by exactly this: computing supplied it with problems that the result-only view was structurally unable to state.

What makes this a *stance* rather than a complaint is that it tells you what to be suspicious of. Any time your semantics interprets execution as equality — reduction becomes identity, the running of the program becomes a fact that was always true — you have bought clarity by making cost, timing, and order unsayable, and you should know you have bought it. That trade is often correct; the failure mode is not noticing you made it. Conversely, the reason a purely syntactic account keeps disappointing is symmetric: notation is a poor object of study, structureless and accidental, and the symmetries you find in it are really symmetries of the process it clumsily records. The honest position is that neither half is the truth. The deep invariants of computation are properties of the process, and our current best access to them is through the shape of the notation that records it.

A programmer who internalizes this stops treating a signature or a specification as a full description of a component, and starts asking which questions the chosen abstraction has silently made unaskable. It also reframes what an optimization is: not a change that preserves meaning while altering irrelevant detail, but a change to the process that preserves the named result — which is only irrelevant if you have already decided the process does not count.
