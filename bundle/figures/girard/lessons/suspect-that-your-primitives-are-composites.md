# Suspect your most familiar primitive of being a composite, and let a semantics that disturbs you go looking for the seam

**Lesson:** The construct you never question is the best candidate for decomposition. Function-space had sat at the base of every account of computation for decades, treated as irreducible, and it turned out to factor cleanly into two independent operations: one that manufactures the freedom to reuse an argument, and one that consumes an argument exactly once. Nothing about the old notion was wrong; it was simply coarse — a single symbol standing where two distinct capabilities had been fused. The same turned out to be true of conjunction, of disjunction, of negation. Each split into a version that shares its surrounding context and a version that divides it, distinctions that the older formalisms could not even state because they had been collapsed before anyone looked.

What makes the move findable is a stance about what a semantics is for. One tradition uses semantics as certification: build a model, prove completeness, and declare the system correct and finished. That stance can never reveal a seam, because it takes the syntax as the fixed thing to be blessed. The productive stance is the opposite — hunt for a model that *bothers* you, one whose definitions are simple enough to read directly and which therefore shows internal structure where you expected an atom. Simplification of the model is the enabling step: the decomposition of function-space became visible only after the domain machinery was stripped down far enough that the definition of a function space was short enough to stare at. Complexity in your semantics hides exactly the joints you are looking for.

The corresponding lesson for a working programmer is that a lower primitive count is not achieved by deleting features. It is achieved by discovering that two features you thought were separate are the same thing applied differently, or that one feature you thought was atomic is two things always used together. Both directions reduce the basis, and both require noticing something about the existing vocabulary rather than inventing new vocabulary. When you find such a split, the finer primitives usually turn out to be *more* expressive than the fused one, not less, because you can now write combinations the fused symbol made unsayable — bounds and distinctions that simply had no notation before.

The discipline has a cost worth stating plainly: which of the newly separated pieces counts as the more primitive one is often genuinely undetermined, since each is definable from the other plus negation. Decomposition buys you structural insight and finer expression; it does not hand you a canonical bottom. Being honest about that is part of doing it well.

Also [The System F of Variable Types, Fifteen Years Later](../works/the-system-f-of-variable-types-fifteen-years-later.md) — the earlier paper that performs the enabling simplification, replacing the received notion of domain with a stripped-down one whose definition of function space is short enough to read directly. Also [Proofs and Types](../works/proofs-and-types.md) — the chapter on the semantics of disjunctive types, which carries out the split in full view: an interpretation that resists the obvious construction is worked through until the arrow and the sum each factor into a reuse-licensing operation composed with a single-use one, and that factorization is named as the origin of the whole later framework.
