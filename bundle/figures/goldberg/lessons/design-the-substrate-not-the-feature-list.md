# When you cannot know what will be built, build the substrate and hand the specification job to whoever has the problem

**Lesson:** There are two ways to serve a wide population of users, and only one of them scales. The first is to survey the intended uses and provide for each of them; applied to a truly general audience this converges on a system stuffed with provisions that fits nobody, because the union of everyone's requirements is not a design. The second is to refuse the survey: treat the range of future uses as unknowable in principle, and put the effort into a substrate general enough that a person with a problem can state the tool they want in it. The design decision this work makes is to deliberately give up authorship of the applications in exchange for authorship of the medium — the burden of deciding what a specific tool should do moves to the only party who actually knows, the one who needs it.

The catch, stated plainly in the same argument, is that this only pays off under two conditions. The general medium of expression has to be carefully and comprehensively worked out, because everything anyone will ever build now bottoms out in it and a gap in the substrate is a gap in every future tool. And enough general machinery has to already exist that nobody begins at zero — a substrate with no accumulated stock is an invitation nobody can accept. So the strategy is not "provide less"; it is to move the investment from breadth of anticipated features into depth of a small, uniform expressive base plus a stock of reusable parts. Compare the difference between the two classes of manufactured things this work distinguishes: a device that pre-decides its applications is powerful along the axes its makers chose and obstructive along every other, while a raw material offers many dimensions and fine grain, and can be turned to uses its supplier never conceived. The target is a thing with the second's openness and the first's power, which is a demand on the substrate, not on the feature count.

What makes this more than a slogan is the evidence standard it implies. If the whole point is unanticipated use, then the system is validated not by how well it does what the designers imagined but by what appears that they did not imagine — an unfamiliar user building, unprompted, a tool the team had already built and never showed them; practitioners in other fields (animation, music, circuit design, decision analysis) constructing their own working instruments rather than requesting features. And there is a sharper consequence about what "writing" means in this medium: the artifacts a user produces are not inert documents but running processes that simulate and decide, which means the ability to write is the ability to make tools. Literacy in a computational medium therefore cannot stop at reading — a population that can only consume what others authored is not literate in it, and a system that only permits consumption has not delivered a medium at all.

A programmer who believes this designs differently in one concrete way: when a request arrives for feature number thirty, the response is to ask what expressive gap in the base made the previous twenty-nine necessary, and to close that instead. It also changes who is allowed to extend the system. If specification is genuinely delegated, there can be no second-class tier for user-written extensions — the mechanism the outsider uses has to be the same mechanism the system is built out of, or the delegation is nominal.

**Source:** [Personal Dynamic Media](../works/personal-dynamic-media.md) — the closing argument that asks whether a machine intended for everyone must collapse under competing demands, answers it by contrasting pre-committed manufactured goods with open raw materials, and states the resulting division of labor between medium designer and user; the earlier survey of systems built by children, animators, musicians, and a decision theorist is the evidence offered for it.
