# Carve a domain by what its things do and what they need coordinated, and say out loud over what interval your idealizations hold

**Lesson:** When this book builds a substantial application it demonstrates a modeling method that contradicts the obvious one. The obvious method takes the nouns the domain hands you — in its worked example, the locations, the staff, and the customers of a service business — and makes each a kind. The method actually used notices that all three are, behaviorally, the same thing: something that enters a situation, carries out a sequence of activities, and leaves. That commonality becomes the general kind, and the apparently different categories become variations on it. The lesson is that domain vocabulary tracks social roles, while a program needs to track behavior, and the two only sometimes coincide. Where they don't, following the vocabulary produces three near-identical descriptions and no shared structure.

The second carving in the example is by coordination requirement rather than by subject matter. Things that participants need from each other are classified by what synchronization they demand: a quantity that is used up, a quantity that is borrowed and returned, a quantity that is replenished over time, and — distinctly — another participant whose own activity has to be interleaved with yours because neither can proceed alone. That last case is the one a resource-count model cannot express, which is exactly why it earns its own treatment. Sorting a domain by what its interactions demand of the coordination mechanism, rather than by what the interacting things are called, is what makes the resulting framework cover cases nobody enumerated.

Two further habits in this material are worth naming. First, the treatment of time: the book lays out two possible representations, one where a clock advances uniformly and everyone gets a chance to act at each step, and one where the clock jumps to whenever the next scheduled thing happens, and it picks the second and says why. Making logical time an explicit, manipulable part of the model is what allows a crowd of nominally independent activities to be interleaved deterministically, giving the appearance of simultaneity without any real concurrency — a design choice, not an implementation detail, because it determines how everything else is written. Second, the intellectual honesty about idealization: having introduced the category of things that are borrowed rather than consumed, the authors immediately concede that nothing is really in that category — borrowed things wear out too — and that the category means only "not consumed within the span the model cares about." That parenthetical is the whole discipline. An abstraction that quietly assumes an idealization is fragile; one that states which interval the idealization holds over tells a future reader exactly when the model stops applying.

A programmer working this way starts a domain model by listing what each candidate entity *does* and merging entities whose activity has the same shape, then classifies their interactions by required coordination before writing any of them. And every simplifying assumption gets its validity window written next to it, so the model's edge is documented rather than discovered.

**Source:** [Smalltalk-80: The Language and Its Implementation](../works/smalltalk-80-the-language-and-its-implementation.md) — Part Three's simulation chapters: the passage that considers three role-based classes and replaces them with one behavioral superclass, the classification of resources by the kind of synchronization they require including the participant-to-participant case, the comparison of uniform-tick and next-event clock representations with the reasoning for choosing the latter, and the aside qualifying what "not consumed" can honestly mean. Also [Smalltalk-80: The Interactive Programming Environment](../works/smalltalk-80-the-interactive-programming-environment.md) — the language-fundamentals chapter's amusement-park walkthrough, which makes the same point from the other side: the intended purpose of a model decides which parts of a situation get represented at all, so components irrelevant to the questions being asked simply do not exist in the design.
