# Aim to be a middle layer: extend a base you refuse to replace, and become a base others need not replace

**Lesson:** Two pressures pull in opposite directions whenever a field needs better tools. Practitioners want vocabulary that matches their problem, which produces a proliferation of special-purpose languages nobody can maintain or port. Everyone else wants one general language, which produces a tool that expresses nothing in the practitioner's terms. The resolution is to stop treating these as competing products and treat the general language as a substrate: a base designed so that problem-oriented vocabulary can be added on top by ordinary users, in the language itself, without a new compiler.

Downward, this means a strict extension of something people already have rather than a fresh invention. Choosing an existing algorithmic language as a subset is partly a concession to users who should not have to learn another one, but the deeper reason is that a base whose structure invites extension gives you its entire semantics for free and lets you spend all your design effort on what is genuinely new. The discipline that goes with it is to improve the base while extending it rather than bolting a domain veneer onto its side, and to arrange the new model so that programs in the old style remain exactly the degenerate case of the new one. Compatibility then is a structural property of the semantics, not a translation layer.

Upward, this means the mechanism for grouping definitions must be able to package a whole family — data, operations, and further definable families — under one name that a program can pull in as a prefix to its own text. Once that exists, a domain layer is just a definition, layers stack (a simulation vocabulary on the base, a job-shop vocabulary on the simulation one), and the cost profile inverts nicely: the occasional user works entirely in the topmost vocabulary and never needs the full base, while the expert has the base available and can extend the vocabulary himself. Notice what this asks of a designer: your job is not to anticipate the domains, it is to make the domain layers cheap enough that someone closer to the problem will write them. The unglamorous corollaries — separate compilation, a stable standard, an explicit process for controlled extension — are load-bearing, because a substrate that cannot be extended without renegotiating the whole language is not a substrate.
