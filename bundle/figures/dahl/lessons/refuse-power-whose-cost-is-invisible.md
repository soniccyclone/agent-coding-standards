# Refuse expressive power whose cost is invisible at the point where it is used

**Lesson:** The strongest argument against a feature is not that it is expensive. Expensive features are fine when the person invoking one can see that he is invoking it. The fatal property is a feature whose use looks ordinary while its consequence lands somewhere the programmer is not looking — in the storage he can no longer reclaim, in the fragmentation he will not notice until much later, in the lifetime he has unknowingly extended. A parameter mechanism that passes an unevaluated expression rather than a value is a fair example: it is genuinely more general, it reads exactly like the cheap kind at the call site, and in a setting where objects outlive their creators it quietly pins everything the expression could touch. Someone will write a program in which nothing can ever be released, and nothing in the text he wrote will tell him so.

Concluding "then leave it out" is unpopular, because the feature is real and the loss is real. The reasoning that justifies it is a comparison, not a reflex: what does the generality actually buy, and what does it cost when the machine underneath is not a simple stack? Where the answer is that the cost is out of proportion, the restriction is the design, not a compromise on the design. The same reasoning shows up in the opposite direction in the same body of work. Components with interleaved lifetimes are flatly incompatible with allocating everything in nesting order, so the language accepts explicit restrictions on how such components may be declared and parameterized — restrictions that exist purely to keep an efficient allocation scheme possible. And it shows up again in the choice of granularity for independently allocated things: making every small variable its own allocatable object is not neutral, because a store fragmented into small pieces is a store that either needs compaction or degrades until it collapses.

What generalizes here is not any of the individual verdicts, all of which belong to a particular era of storage management. It is that a language or interface designer is responsible for the cost profile his users will not be able to see, and that a feature's price must be legible at the site where the price is paid. Where legibility is impossible, the honest options are to remove the feature or to restrict it until its cost becomes bounded and predictable — never to ship it and rely on discipline nobody has the information to exercise. A programmer holding this view asks of every convenience: if this turns out to be the expensive thing in my system, will anything in the code point at it? A no is a reason to redesign, not a reason to document.

**Source:** [Class and Subclass Declarations](../works/class-and-subclass-declarations.md) — the virtual-quantities discussion and the exchange with the audience afterwards, where Dahl argues that unrestricted call by name for classes costs out of proportion to its gain and would invite programs in which no data can ever be released without the author realizing it, and separately explains the granularity choice for independently allocated objects in terms of store fragmentation. Also [SIMULA - an ALGOL-Based Simulation Language](../works/simula-an-algol-based-simulation-language.md) — the section on parameters and non-locals, which states plainly that interleaved component execution is incompatible with a simple run-time stack and accepts declaration restrictions to keep an efficient storage scheme available.
