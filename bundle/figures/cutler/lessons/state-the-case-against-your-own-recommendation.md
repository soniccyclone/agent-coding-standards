# A technical recommendation earns its authority by containing the strongest available argument against itself

**Lesson:** The structure of a persuasive technical argument is not advocacy followed by rebuttal. It is a statement of the problem in terms both sides accept, an enumeration of the genuinely available options including the ones the author dislikes, and then a symmetric accounting of what each option gives and costs — with the merits of the disfavored option stated as strongly as its proponents would state them, and the weaknesses of the favored option stated without softening. What this buys is not fairness for its own sake. It buys the reader the ability to check the reasoning rather than only the conclusion, and it removes the cheapest way to dismiss a recommendation, which is to notice an obvious objection the author appeared not to have considered.

The symmetry has to be real to work. Conceding that a rival approach is available today, performs competitively, has multiple suppliers, and is better than its nearest alternative is not a rhetorical gesture; it is the concession that makes the subsequent argument about foundations worth reading, because it establishes that the author understands why anyone would choose otherwise. Likewise, naming that your own preferred path is underfunded, single-sourced, and has no product that meets the time-to-market window is what makes the recommendation an engineering judgment rather than a preference. A document that only lists advantages for one option and disadvantages for the other has told the reader which conclusion was reached first.

There is a second, subtler discipline in the framing. The goal set out at the start is not to win but to reach a decision, or failing that to establish a process by which a decision can be reached — an acknowledgement that a decision made and held is the actual deliverable, and that the author may not be the one who makes it. This is what allows a strongly held recommendation to coexist with genuine deference on the outcome, and it is also why the recommendation can legitimately include a request about how the decision is treated afterward rather than only about which way it goes.

**Source:** [DECwest/SDT Agenda: PRISM vs. MIPS](../works/decwest-sdt-agenda-prism-vs-mips.md) — the whole document's shape: an opening statement of what the meeting should accomplish, a problem statement, three named strategies each given its own pros and cons including the favorable case for the option the author argues against and the funding and sourcing weaknesses of the one he recommends, and only then a recommendation.
