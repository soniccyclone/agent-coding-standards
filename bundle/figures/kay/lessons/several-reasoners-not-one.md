# Design for several distinct reasoning faculties that compete, and give each one work it is good at

**Lesson:** It is convenient to model the person using a system as a single general-purpose reasoner, and it is wrong. There are several distinct faculties in play, with different jobs and different failure modes: one that tracks where things are and manipulates them directly, one that recognizes, compares and arranges configurations at a glance, and one that carries long chains of abstract inference. They arose at different times and communicate with each other poorly, so they do not cooperate by default — they compete for control, and whichever has control determines what is currently thinkable. Any design that engages only one of them is not merely leaving capability on the table; the unengaged faculties tend to interfere rather than idle.

The immediate practical payoff is a diagnosis for being stuck. A problem that is trivial when arranged spatially can hold someone for hours when they attack it symbolically, and the reason is not difficulty but that the wrong faculty is holding the problem and will not release it — a state that a run of analytical work makes far more likely. The response is to change the representation so the problem lands in a different faculty, rather than to apply more effort in the one that is blocked. For a system this means keeping several live views of the same state available at once, so switching faculty does not require switching task or losing position; for a person it means treating "draw it", "sort them into piles", "work it by hand on a small case" as debugging techniques of the same standing as reasoning harder.

The faculties also divide the labor of creative work in a way worth exploiting deliberately. The comparing, pattern-spotting faculty is restless by design — its job is to keep sweeping over a scene and changing context — which makes it very good at finding unexpected structure and very bad at finishing anything. The symbolic faculty holds one context for a long time, which makes it good at finishing and prone to getting stuck. So a workspace crowded with simultaneously visible material feeds discovery, while a notation supporting long unbroken chains feeds completion, and a design that only supplies one of the two has picked which half of the work its users will be able to do. The strongest arrangement is graded: ground new material in direct manipulation, move to visible arrangement and comparison, and let symbolic abstraction take over once the intuitions are in place — with the path back down still open. Two things follow that are easy to miss. Removing interference matters as much as adding support, so quieting the faculties not relevant to the current work is a real design lever. And the mapping is not a metaphor to be pushed: trying to do genuinely symbolic work in a purely pictorial register is a persistent temptation that wastes years, because each faculty is being asked to do the job it is worst at.

**Source:** [User Interface: A Personal View](../works/user-interface-a-personal-view.md) — the account of Bruner's reinterpretation of Piaget via the covered-glass experiment, the resulting model of separate enactive, iconic and symbolic mentalities with distinct jobs that interfere and compete, the report that a subject who has been given consecutive analytic tasks can be blocked for hours on a problem trivial to solve figuratively, the argument that creativity in mathematics and the arts sits in the non-symbolic mentalities while completion sits in the symbolic one, the description of removing interference and facilitating attention, and the closing caution that trying to do symbolic thinking iconically held back progress.
