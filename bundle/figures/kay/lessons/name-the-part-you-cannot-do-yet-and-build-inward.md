# Name the part you do not know how to do, build inward from both ends, and treat the models as instruments to be discarded

**Lesson:** An honest plan for something genuinely new has a hole in the middle, and the discipline is to draw the hole rather than paper over it. Marking the unsolved region explicitly — this is where we currently have no idea, and everything else is arranged around finding out — is what keeps the project from quietly redefining success as whatever the known techniques can already deliver. It also tells you where to work. The two ends are approachable: the bottom, where the machine's crudeness has to be absorbed, and the top, where the person's experience has to be produced. Build inward from both, and the unsolved middle stops being an open field and becomes a shrinking gap with hard interfaces on either side. A gap with known boundaries is a far better problem than a gap defined only by ambition.

The artifacts produced during that inward march should be understood as instruments, not deliverables. Their purpose is to tell you how much irreducible content a region holds and where the formulation is wrong, and that information is extracted by building and then, usually, by discarding. The trap is that a working model acquires defenders the moment it works, and a project that cannot throw away its successful experiments has converted a measuring device into an obligation. Deciding in advance that most of them are disposable is what buys the freedom to build them roughly and quickly, which is the only way to run enough of them to learn anything.

There is a bootstrapping paradox in all of this that is worth accepting rather than resolving: you generally have to build something like the system before you can build the system, and the something-like has to be built with the wrong tools because the right ones do not exist yet. The corollary is that abstract reasoning about a new architecture stays airborne indefinitely unless it is forced to produce a real, debugged example that lies outside what current thinking already covers. Pick that example deliberately — close enough to the known that it is achievable, far enough outside that it cannot be reached without inventing the next thing — and let the difficulty of actually finishing it be what drags the design down to earth.
