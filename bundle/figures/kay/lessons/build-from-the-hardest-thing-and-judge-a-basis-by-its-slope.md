# Make the hardest thing you need your one primitive, and judge a small basis by its slope rather than its size

**Lesson:** When a system needs both an easy capability and a hard one, the reflex is to build the easy one first and add the hard one later as a special provision. That ordering is backwards, and it is why so many systems have an elegant core plus a set of exceptions bolted to the outside. Identify instead the most demanding thing the system genuinely has to do, make that the thing you get right, and then obtain everything easier by restriction. Deferring evaluation is harder than evaluating, so a construct that defers by default and evaluates on demand can supply the eager case, while the reverse requires an escape hatch. The general form of the move is to look at which of two capabilities can simulate the other, and put the simulating one at the bottom.

The reason to care about a small basis at all is not economy. A basis can be tiny and universal and still be worthless to think in, because expressing anything of interest in it takes an amount of contorted text out of all proportion to the interest of what is being expressed. The property actually worth optimizing is the relationship between the two: how quickly the interestingness of what you can say rises with the complexity of saying it. Call it slope. A steep slope is what makes a small set of ideas feel powerful rather than merely minimal, and it is why a general-purpose machine language and a good high-level language can be equally universal while only one of them is worth writing in. Any argument that a design is good because it has few primitives is incomplete until it says what those primitives let you reach and at what cost in expression.

That criterion is also what a designer's sense of beauty is for, once it is made explicit rather than left as taste. The forms that hold up combine economy of statement with breadth of application, with the property that understanding one of them illuminates things beyond it, and with getting to its results in few steps. A design that is compact but yields nothing beyond itself fails the third; a design that is broad but takes forever to get anywhere fails the fourth. The example worth holding in mind is not a mathematical identity but a living cell: uniform enough on the outside to compose with anything, general enough to differentiate into every specialization the organism needs, and tolerant enough on the inside to accommodate the accumulated inelegance that practical function requires. Elegance and practicality are not being traded off there; the uniform interface is exactly what buys room for the mess behind it.

**Source:** [The Early History of Smalltalk](../works/the-early-history-of-smalltalk.md) — the account of studying the evaluation machinery of the functional-language tradition and concluding that the right approach is to take the hardest and most profound thing needed, make it excellent, and derive the easier things from it, prompted by asking why a language would base itself on the argument-evaluating form when the non-evaluating one can supply both; and the reflection on what beauty means in this setting, which pairs economy, generality, illumination and finesse, rejects universal machine languages as beautiful on the grounds that expressing anything interesting in them is turgid, introduces the requirement of a good match between the interest of a result and the complexity of expressing it, and takes the cell as the model in which a uniform external interface is what permits useful inelegance inside.
