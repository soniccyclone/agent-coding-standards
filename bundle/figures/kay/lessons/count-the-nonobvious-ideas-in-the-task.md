# When capable people fail at a task you think is easy, count the non-obvious ideas it contains

**Lesson:** The most useful diagnostic available to a system's designer is the moment when competent, motivated people, who have just demonstrated fluency with the mechanics, stall completely on a problem the designer considers small. The instinct is to blame the interface, the syntax, or the documentation, and to fix those. The better move is to take the solution you would write, and enumerate the ideas in it that are not derivable by someone who has not already met them — not the operations, which are visible in the manual, but the conceptual moves. The count is routinely startling, and it explains the failure without appealing to aptitude. Some of those ideas are of the kind that took a civilization a long time to find and that nobody reconstructs by unaided reasoning; a person lacking one is not going to be helped by better tooling.

This distinguishes two things that get conflated when people ask whether a system is easy to use. Knowing the vocabulary and mechanics of a medium is one accomplishment, and it can be acquired quickly, often by exploration alone. Knowing the body of ideas that the medium is used to express is a separate accomplishment that accumulates over years and does not follow from the first. A population that has the first without the second will look successful on every short assessment and will be unable to produce anything of its own — the analogy being a reader who can decode a sentence but cannot follow, let alone write, a sustained argument. Designers who conclude from early successes that the barrier has fallen are usually observing the fraction of any population that takes to a pursuit naturally, and mistaking it for the effect of their design.

Two things do help, and both operate on the design activity rather than on the notation. The first is an intermediate artifact between having a vague sense of the problem and writing anything that runs: a decomposition into the participating parts and the requests that pass between them, with each part's internal method left described in ordinary language. This separates deciding what the pieces are from making any piece work, which is the step novices cannot do simultaneously, and the informal notes become the commentary afterward. The second is sequencing by chain length. People who can apply one transformation confidently, and who will happily explore forward one step at a time, are often unable to plan two or three ahead toward a chosen goal — so what has to be taught is not more operations but small combinations of them, a couple of moves that point at an outcome, in the way one learns openings in a game. Neither of these is a property of a language, which is why arguments about language features do not resolve the difficulty.

**Source:** [The Early History of Smalltalk](../works/the-early-history-of-smalltalk.md) — the account of teaching adults who progressed quickly through the mechanics and then could not build a simple record-keeping tool even after being shown how, followed by counting the non-obvious ideas in the small program and finding seventeen, some of them comparable to the arch in being very hard to discover if not already known; the conclusion drawn about literacy requiring a body of ideas beyond reading and writing ability; the description of the design-template intermediary that let learners decompose a situation into parts and messages with methods planned informally in English; the caution that early successes reflected a self-selecting minority; and the later finding that learners can chain forward one transformation at a time but cannot plan two or three toward a goal, so combinations are what must be taught.
