# Read the previous generation as almost a new thing, take an extreme position to force the new one out, and be willing to burn what already works

**Lesson:** There are two ways to read a piece of good prior work. The ordinary reading takes it as a better version of the thing it improved on, extracts the improvement, and applies it to the same purposes as before. The productive reading refuses that framing and treats the work as an incomplete sighting of something that does not exist yet, then goes looking for what that something is. The difference is not subtle and it is not a matter of ambition: the same paper, read the second way, yields a different field. What licenses the second reading is usually a quantitative trend nobody disputes — a resource getting cheaper by orders of magnitude on a schedule — because a large enough change in the numbers invalidates the assumptions the old purposes were built on, even though the artifact in front of you was built under them. Sequences of real advances turn out to be chains of such rereadings, each generation's canonical system being the previous generation's better-old-thing looked at properly.

Having spotted a candidate new thing, the way to find out what it actually is, is to hold it more absolutely than the evidence yet justifies. Adopt the position that everything without exception is going to be built this one way, and the awkward cases stop being reasons to keep a second mechanism around and start being problems that must be solved. That is what produces the invention: a moderate position lets you retain the old mechanism for the hard parts, and the hard parts are exactly where the new idea would have had to be developed. The extremity is a working method rather than a belief, and the difference shows in whether you can say what would count as its failure.

The corresponding hazard is that this works, and what works then captures you. A sufficiently strong system defines the shape of what people build in it, so its applications end up resembling the system rather than resembling any new idea, and its designers stop being able to see past it — the same closure that the old thing had, arrived at from the other direction. Every genuinely new idea appears to follow the same arc, from unnoticed to operational to central to orthodox, and the last transition happens inside its inventors as readily as anywhere else. The only defence anyone has found is periodically proposing to abandon the working system outright, not because a replacement is ready but because the proposal is what surfaces which of your commitments are still reasoned and which have become furniture.

**Source:** [The Early History of Smalltalk](../works/the-early-history-of-smalltalk.md) — the abstract and introduction, which frame the paradigm shift as the refusal to see the previous decade's work as a better way of doing what was already being done, tie that refusal to the cost-per-volume trend that made the old assumptions void, and describe deliberately extreme positions taken so that the new styles would have to be invented; the recurring account of new ideas passing from barely noticed through operational to central and finally to inflexible orthodoxy; the periodization at the end proposing that each major language came from reading its predecessor the second way; and the retreat called to argue for discarding a working system on the grounds that even inadequate tools reshape their users and that a compelling system makes its own applications resemble it rather than resemble anything new.
