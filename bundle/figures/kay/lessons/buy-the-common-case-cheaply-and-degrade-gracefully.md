# Buy the common case with a cheap approximate mechanism and let the rare case be slow, rather than paying bookkeeping on every operation

**Lesson:** Two habits wreck small systems: trying to serve every conceivable use, and trying to be correct-and-fast in every conceivable circumstance. The second is the more expensive, because insisting on good behavior in the last few percent of cases usually means instrumenting all of the other cases to detect them. Bookkeeping on every access, exact usage records, a maintained pool of free identifiers, a directory consulted on each reference — these are the machinery of guaranteeing the rare case, and they are paid for continuously. The alternative posture is statistical: decide what fraction of the time the system must be good, drive the overhead of that fraction toward nothing, and accept an honest, gradual worsening outside it. A system built this way spends most of its life behaving as though it had far more resources than it does.

The mechanisms that implement this share a shape worth recognizing. A scrambling function turns an expensive content-addressed lookup into a direct one that succeeds outright the great majority of the time and falls back to walking a short chain otherwise — with the collision rate itself monitored, so the structure is rebuilt when the approximation stops holding. A fresh identifier is obtained by drawing at random and testing whether it is in use, which is almost always right on the first attempt and costs nothing to maintain, instead of tracking which identifiers are free. Choosing what to evict is done by sampling at random with the odds weighted by a category the system already knows, rather than by recording every access; something evicted while genuinely in demand simply returns, and having been chosen randomly, is unlikely to be chosen again immediately, so the population converges on the right occupants without anyone having measured anything. In each case an exact algorithm is replaced by a cheap random or hashed one whose error is self-correcting.

Two properties follow that matter more than the speed. First, behavior becomes largely independent of the exact resource limits, so the same programs run unchanged on a smaller or larger configuration and the difference shows up as the fraction of time spent in overhead rather than as a rewrite. Second, the failure mode under load is a smooth slowdown toward the speed of the slower resource rather than a cliff, which is a far better thing to hand a user than a system that is excellent until it stops. The design question to carry away is not "how do I make this always fast" but "what is the cheap mechanism that is right most of the time and wrong harmlessly."

**Source:** [The Reactive Engine](../works/the-reactive-engine.md) — the chapter on the pragmatic environment, which names doing everything for everyone and demanding full efficiency at all times as the two bottlenecks, adopts an explicitly statistical stance of minimizing overhead for the ninety-odd percent case, and argues for graceful degradation in which the machine merely appears to slow toward the speed of secondary storage and behaves largely independently of its storage limits; together with the memory chapters, where hashing simulates associative lookup with a monitored collision rate and rebuilds when it is exceeded, fresh segment identifiers are drawn at random and tested rather than tracked, and eviction is a weighted random draw over segment classes chosen specifically to avoid any per-access bookkeeping.
