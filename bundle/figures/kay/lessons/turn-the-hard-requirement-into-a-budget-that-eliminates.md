# Convert the one non-negotiable requirement into a numeric budget, and let the budget eliminate whole option classes

**Lesson:** Feasibility arguments stall when they are conducted as comparisons — this component against that one, weighed on features. The move that unsticks them is to identify the single requirement that is genuinely not negotiable, the one that if surrendered means you are building something else entirely, and convert it into arithmetic: watts, ounces, dollars, milliseconds. Once the requirement is a number, entire families of options fail on the arithmetic rather than on judgment, and they fail quickly and without argument. A component that draws amps while displaying is not a weaker candidate, it is disqualified; so is the mature, cheap, universally available alternative that assumes mains power and a deep cabinet. Elimination by budget is faster than evaluation by merit and it produces no ties.

What is left after the eliminations is usually not a shortlist but a statement of the property nothing currently on the table has — something that consumes energy only when its state changes, not while it is being read. That reframing is the real payoff. A shortlist confines you to what exists; a stated missing property tells you what to go find, what to fund, and what to invent, and it lets you specify a system around a part nobody has shipped yet while being honest that this is what you are doing. Design proposals that never perform the elimination step never reach this statement, and so they never notice that their whole approach depends on a component that is not merely unavailable but impossible under their own constraints.

Two disciplines keep the budget honest. First, the number should be derived from what the system displaces, not from what is currently buildable: an existing recurring expenditure that the new thing would absorb, summed over its service life, yields a defensible target and often suggests the distribution model at the same time. A target pinned to what today's technology can hit produces either timidity or fantasy, and an unreachable target derived from displacement is still useful because the shortfall is denominated in something someone can act on. Second, find out which term in the cost model actually dominates before optimizing anything. Where per-unit overheads — testing, packaging, handling, setup — dwarf the cost of what the unit contains, the optimization is to reduce the number of units rather than to simplify each one, so combining functions into a single package is nearly free while splitting things for tidiness is expensive. The same inversion governs round trips, syscalls and deployment artifacts; the instinct to make each piece smaller is wasted effort when the invoice is charging per piece.
