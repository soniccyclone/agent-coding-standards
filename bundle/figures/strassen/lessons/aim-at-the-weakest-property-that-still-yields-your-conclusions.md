# Aim at the weakest property that still yields your conclusions, and find out first whether a general proof could exist

**Lesson:** You believe an object has a strong, clean property, you cannot prove it, and the temptation is to keep attacking the clean statement because it is the one that would be satisfying. Two cheaper questions come first. The first is what you actually need the property *for*. Trace forward from the property to the conclusions you want and find the weakest hypothesis that still supports them. Very often the strong property was doing far less work than its strength suggests: if the conclusion you need only requires that the region be connected, then a weakened shape condition that implies connectedness suffices, and that condition may be within reach when the strong one is not. Chasing the elegant statement when a shabbier one closes the same argument is a common and expensive misallocation, and the way to notice you are doing it is to write out the downstream dependencies explicitly rather than assume the strong hypothesis is minimal.

The second question is whether the property is even the kind of thing that could have a general proof. Your object lives in a class, and the class is usually closed under some construction — build a new instance from old ones and it is still an instance. Take an easy instance, apply the construction, and check whether the property survives. If you can exhibit a member of the class that fails the property, you have learned something structurally decisive at low cost: no argument that consults only the class-level axioms can ever work, because such an argument would have to prove the false statement too. Any proof must therefore reach in and use something specific about your object. That is not discouraging, it is directional — it eliminates the entire space of formal attempts you would otherwise spend months in, and it tells you the proof, if it exists, is about your object's particular content.

The remaining obligation is bookkeeping, and it is what keeps the situation honest. State the conjecture you believe, separately and plainly, together with the list of consequences it would deliver, so a reader can see exactly what is riding on it. State the weaker result you actually proved, and which of those consequences it already secures. Anyone who later proves the conjecture then knows precisely what they have bought, and anyone building on your work today knows precisely which of their conclusions are conditional. The failure mode this prevents is the worst kind of drift: a conjecture that gradually gets treated as known because nobody wrote down which results depend on it.

**Source:** [The Asymptotic Spectrum of Tensors](../works/the-asymptotic-spectrum-of-tensors.md) — the introduction and sections 5 and 6: convexity of the logarithmic spectrum of matrix multiplication is conjectured, its consequences are listed, and the paper proves instead only that the region is star-shaped relative to a triangle of cheaply-obtained interior points, which suffices for connectedness and hence for the spectrum of cubic matrix multiplication to be a closed interval, which in turn gives the sharpest form of the summation theorem for those tensors; and the closing example, which uses the class of spectra being closed under nonnegative integral polynomial maps to construct a spectrum that is not logarithmically convex and does not even contain the convex hull of its own gauge points, so no framework-level argument for convexity can exist.
