# A property that only holds once you enrich the environment is a property of the environment

**Lesson:** Two settings differ only in what constants are available — one has a poorer supply, the other a richer one, and every object in the poor setting has an obvious image in the rich one. It is tempting to treat the richer setting as the real one and the poorer as an inconvenience, because the richer setting is where the theory is comfortable: more things factor, more limits exist, more identities are available. But the transfer of claims across that boundary is one-way, and the direction is the opposite of the convenient one. If a construction exists in the poor setting it survives when you enrich, so upper bounds move upward freely. If a construction exists only after enriching, nothing follows about the poor setting at all — and this is not a technicality about pathological cases. There are objects whose cost is genuinely lower once extra constants are on hand, and objects whose cost provably depends on which constants exist. So a method demonstrated in the rich setting is evidence about the rich setting and about nothing else, and if you will deploy in the poor one you have validated the wrong thing.

The productive follow-up is to stop treating the gap as a hazard and start measuring it. Ask how much the enrichment can possibly be worth: is there a bound, in terms of how big the extension is, on how much cheaper an object can become? A bound like that turns the boundary from a wall into a conversion rate, and lets a result obtained in the comfortable setting be cashed out, with a stated loss, in the setting you care about. Often the bound is only obtainable conditionally, under some regularity assumption nobody has proved — and that is still worth having, for a reason that inverts its apparent weakness. A conditional bound on the gap is a refutation instrument: exhibit one object whose cost drops by more than the bound allows, and you have not merely found a curiosity, you have killed the assumption. Deriving the sharpest, most checkable consequence of a premise you suspect is false is how you go hunting for the counterexample, so proving a conditional theorem you do not believe the hypothesis of is a legitimate and pointed move rather than a hedge.

The engineering form of this is mundane and constantly violated. A system verified with a richer library, a more permissive runtime, a larger word size, a stronger consistency model or an extra oracle has been verified in a setting the production one is a sub-case of, and the passing result transfers in the wrong direction. What you want is either a demonstration in the impoverished setting itself, or a quantified statement about what the enrichment could have been worth — and the moment you try to write that quantified statement down, you find out whether you understand the dependence at all.

**Source:** [Relative Bilinear Complexity and Matrix Multiplication](../works/relative-bilinear-complexity-and-matrix-multiplication.md) — section 5 on extension of scalars: restriction transfers in both directions between a ring and a free extension in the stated senses but the ordering after extension does not imply the ordering before, demonstrated by a concrete example over a splitting field together with a cited lower bound showing the un-extended object genuinely costs more; and the proposition bounding, under the additivity conjecture and in characteristic zero, how far scalar extension can reduce cost, which the paper offers explicitly as a route to disproving that conjecture.
