# Isolate the axioms your argument actually used, then find out whose theory you have landed in

**Lesson:** You build a construction inside a concrete setting, driven by concrete examples, and at some point you notice that the derivation never touched the concreteness. Nothing in it depended on the objects being what they were; it used a handful of structural facts — that combination behaves like addition and multiplication, that the comparison relation agrees with the ordinary order on the trivial objects, that no object is unreachably far above another. Two things follow, and the second is worth far more than the first. The obvious payoff is reuse: strip the setting away, list the structural facts as axioms, redo the construction from them, and it now applies to every setting satisfying the list, including ones you have no interest in today. The larger payoff is that a short axiom list is *searchable*. Structures defined by a few order-and-arithmetic compatibility conditions have been studied by people who were not thinking about your problem at all, and if your object satisfies a known list, their representation theorem is yours for free.

That is the move to plan for: do the abstraction not just to generalize but to make contact. Having isolated the axioms, prove that the object you constructed satisfies a named property from someone else's field, and then cite rather than reprove the structure theorem attached to that property. The entire apparatus that turns your comparison relation into a compact space of numerical evaluations can arrive as a citation, because the hard work was done decades earlier for reasons unrelated to yours. What remains for you is verification: showing, condition by condition, that your object qualifies. That verification is real work and it is the honest form of the shortcut — you are not assuming the inheritance, you are earning it, and the axiom list is precisely the checklist.

The corresponding failure is to stay inside your own vocabulary and build the general machinery yourself, which is both more work and epistemically worse, because a bespoke construction has no theorems attached to it and nobody else's results compose with it. So when a derivation feels suspiciously formal — when you catch yourself proving something without consulting what the objects are — stop and take that as the signal to hoist. Write down what you used. Give the resulting condition a name. Then go looking for who else has that name, because the odds that a structure characterized by three compatibility axioms is unstudied are low, and the cost of checking is an afternoon against the cost of rebuilding a representation theory from scratch.

**Source:** [The Asymptotic Spectrum of Tensors](../works/the-asymptotic-spectrum-of-tensors.md) — section 2, which the introduction motivates by observing that the definition of the asymptotic preorder and the derivation of its properties are purely formal despite having been inspired by a concrete theorem, and which therefore constructs the preorder and the spectrum from an abstract commutative ring with a positive cone spanning it and a preorder satisfying three named conditions; the resulting preorder is then proved to satisfy a condition for which a representation theory already existed in the work of Stone and Kadison, whose theorem — supplying the compact Hausdorff spectrum, the point-separating homomorphism, and the reflection of the order — is imported rather than reproved.
