# Learn the theory, then judge the product against it

**Lesson:** Date is explicit about the bias running through fifty years of his writing: get the foundation right and understand it properly before building on it, because products change constantly and principles do not. This is not an argument for theory over practice but a claim about where a practitioner's leverage sits. A dominant product's feature set has to be relearned every few years and gives you no vantage point from which to evaluate the next one. A small, stable theory takes comparable effort to learn once and thereafter lets you read a vendor's claims, a standard's text, or a new paradigm's marketing and say precisely which parts are the underlying idea, which are approximations to it, and which are mistakes. The theory is worth learning because it is the instrument of judgment, not because it is purer.

The most instructive part is the pedagogical reversal he documents across editions. He had originally taught the abstract model through the industry's concrete language on the sensible principle that the concrete comes easier. Watching the distance between the two widen, he concluded that continuing would be actively misleading, and inverted the order: the model first, in its own terms and its own notation, with the language covered honestly but separately and in the context of each topic it touches. The generalizable claim is that a corrupted implementation of an idea is a bad teacher of the idea, because every deviation is silently absorbed as part of the concept, and the learner ends up unable to tell the accident from the essence. When an implementation is close to the theory, teaching through it is a gift; when it has drifted far enough, the same move damages understanding, and the choice of which situation you are in is an empirical question that must be revisited, not a stylistic preference.

Alongside this sits an unusual commitment to his own corrigibility. He borrows Russell's refusal to be embarrassed by changing his mind, on the grounds that in any field where genuine knowledge accumulates, refusing to revise is the disgrace rather than the reverse, and he applies it to himself: successive editions of his textbook contradict earlier ones, and he offers no defense of the earlier text beyond that it was the best available at the time. This makes the theory the authority rather than the author, which is the same relocation of authority he demands of readers with respect to products. It also means the practitioner's confidence should attach to arguments and not to positions, since a position adopted on someone's word cannot survive the argument being improved.

A programmer who works this way spends learning budget disproportionately on the small durable model underneath whatever tool is in front of them, and keeps a written account of where the tool departs from it, because that list predicts exactly where the bugs and the migration pain will show up. They also state their design opinions with the reasoning attached, so that the opinion can be overturned without the whole design collapsing.

**Source:** [An Introduction to Database Systems](../works/an-introduction-to-database-systems.md) — the eighth edition's preface, where the author names his foundation bias, explains why he stopped using the commercial language as the teaching vehicle for relational concepts, describes the book's shift from describing the field as it is to prescribing what it should be, and closes by borrowing Russell's defense of changing one's opinions. Also [Databases, Types, and the Relational Model: The Third Manifesto](../works/databases-types-and-the-relational-model-the-third-manifesto.md), whose opening chapter deliberately omits product and commercial features because foundations outlast the things built on them, and whose insistence on rejecting the incumbent language outright is defended as the precondition for any proposal that hopes to last.
