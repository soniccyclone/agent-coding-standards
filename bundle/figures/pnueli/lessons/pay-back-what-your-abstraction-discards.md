# An abstraction that discards something must pay it back as an explicit, calibrated assumption

**Lesson:** Modelling concurrent execution as one linear order of steps is a lie of enormous convenience. It reduces parallel activity to a choice of whose turn it is next, which makes the whole apparatus of reasoning about sequences apply directly. It also destroys real information: two genuinely independent actions become an arbitrary order, so the model cannot tell parallel composition from a nondeterministic choice between orderings, and it cannot see where in a run a choice was actually committed. The right response is neither to reject the abstraction nor to forget what it cost. It is to name each thing the abstraction can no longer see, and then reintroduce, as an explicit assumption on which runs count as real, exactly the part you cannot do without.

Fairness is that repayment. Interleaving makes it representable for one participant to be starved forever while another runs; nothing in the model forbids it, and in the physical system it does not happen, so the model must be told. What makes this a lesson rather than a technicality is how much precision the repayment demands. Assume too little and you cannot prove that anyone ever gets served. Assume too much and you have silently granted a second guarantee nobody asked for: a naive requirement that a continuously available step must eventually be taken also forces every repeated internal choice to be resolved fairly, which is a different promise about the language, one an implementer may not want to make. Separating "each participant makes progress" from "each repeated choice is resolved evenhandedly" keeps them independently adoptable. And the strength needed is not uniform across mechanisms: steps that merely wait for a lock need a stronger assumption than steps that are simply ready, because a contended lock is available only intermittently, and an assumption that only covers continuously available steps never fires.

There is a further discipline worth stealing: check a proposed assumption against reorderings that carry no physical meaning. If two adjacent steps belong to different participants and touch nothing in common, swapping them describes the same real execution, so any distinction your assumption draws between the two orderings is an artifact. Run that test and the right strength of a fairness condition can be argued for rather than guessed: a guarantee should be owed when one participant is genuinely stuck waiting while another cycles past the meeting point, and not owed when the two merely happened to be in the right place at the same moment, because that coincidence can be reordered away. A programmer who has internalized this treats every simplifying model — an event loop that pretends handlers are atomic, a memory model that pretends stores are ordered, a scheduler that pretends threads are interchangeable — as carrying a ledger of discarded facts, and expects to write down, calibrate, and defend the assumptions that pay the ledger off.
