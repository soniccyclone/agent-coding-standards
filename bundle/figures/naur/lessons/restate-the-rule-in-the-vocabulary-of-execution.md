# Restate each structural rule in the vocabulary of execution, then read the static restrictions off it

**Lesson:** A rule about the structure of a text and a rule about the behaviour of a machine are the same rule seen from two positions, and a definition that gives only one of the two views leaves half its audience guessing. So write both. Say first, in textual terms, that a declaration is valid for exactly one region and that outside it the name may mean something else. Then say the same thing again in temporal terms, marking it as the dynamic reading: on entry to the region the declared names take on the significance their declarations imply, names already meaning something outside are for the duration given a new significance, names not declared here keep their old one, and on exit — through the closing bracket or through a jump out — the local significances are lost. The second statement adds no rules. It gives the implementer the vocabulary they actually work in, and gives the reader a way to check their understanding of the first statement against a second, independent phrasing.

The reason to do the work in that order is that the dynamic reading is where restrictions come from, and restrictions derived are far better than restrictions decreed. Once you have committed that a region's declarations are elaborated at the moment of entry, questions that looked like matters of taste answer themselves. Can a size expression mention names local to the region it is sizing? No — at the instant it must be evaluated those names have not yet taken on their significance, so it may depend only on names non-local to that region. What follows for the outermost region, which has nothing outside it? Only constant sizes are possible there. Neither of those is an arbitrary prohibition to be memorised; each is a consequence a reader can re-derive, which means it will survive being forgotten. Fix also how often the evaluation happens — once per entry — since a rule about when a thing is computed is incomplete until it says how many times.

Then, if you provide an escape from the lifetime rule, make it a marker on the declaration and state its cost in the same breath. Marking a quantity as surviving between activations is only meaningful against a background commitment that unmarked quantities do not: the value of an unmarked local on re-entry is undefined, and saying so is what gives the marker content. Keep the escape honest at the edges too — a surviving collection whose size is recomputed on each entry retains defined values only within the most recently computed bounds. That is the shape to imitate whenever you add a persistence or caching option to something otherwise scoped: name what persists, name what does not, and say what happens to the part of the persisted thing that the new activation does not cover.

**Source:** [Revised Report on the Algorithmic Language ALGOL 60](../works/revised-report-on-the-algorithmic-language-algol-60.md) — the opening of section 5, which restates the one-block validity of a declaration as a dynamic account of entry and exit, then introduces the own declarator against the explicit background that unmarked declared variables have undefined values on re-entry; section 5.2.4, where the requirement that bound expressions be evaluated at entry yields the restriction to non-local dependencies and thence the constant-bounds-only consequence for the outermost block, together with the once-per-entry evaluation rule; and section 5.2.5 on values of a surviving array being defined only within the most recently calculated bounds.
