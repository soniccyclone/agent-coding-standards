# Somebody progresses and everybody progresses are different guarantees, and which one you can have is decided by your primitive

**Lesson:** Manna and Pnueli separate two liveness claims that everyday language conflates. One says that if anybody wants in, somebody eventually gets in. The other says that if this particular participant wants in, that participant eventually gets in. They give the first a name of its own and prove it about several algorithms whose stronger version is simply false — including one where they leave the falsity as an exercise for the reader. The gap is not a proof artifact. In a mutual-exclusion protocol built on an atomic read-modify-write instruction, an unlucky participant can lose every race forever while the system as a whole never stops making progress. The system is live and the individual starves.

The reason the gap exists is the part worth carrying around, because it is not about the algorithm at all. It is about which fairness guarantee the underlying mechanism provides. A participant whose enabling condition is continuously true is eventually served under the weak assumption that everyone honors. A participant whose enabling condition keeps flickering on and off, true whenever it is checked but not continuously, needs the stronger assumption — the one that promises service to anyone infinitely often ready. Manna and Pnueli are explicit that a fetch-and-add instruction does not carry the stronger guarantee while a semaphore does, and that this single difference is why one supports individual progress and the other only communal progress. Two mechanisms that look like equally good mutual-exclusion primitives, both atomic, both correct for safety, differ in which liveness claims can be proven of anything built on them. That difference lives in the hardware, not in your code, and no amount of restructuring the algorithm recovers it for free.

What you can do instead is bootstrap, and their construction for it is elegant. Communal progress guarantees that somebody reaches the protected region. Whoever gets there is, by the exclusion property, running without interference, and can therefore be conscripted as an arbiter: before leaving, it scans for a waiting participant and designates it as next, and a gate at the entrance holds everyone except the designee. Individual progress is then derived from communal progress plus an arbitration round that costs nothing extra because it runs inside a region already proven exclusive. The weaker primitive plus a explicit hand-off yields the stronger property.

A programmer who has internalized this asks, of every liveness claim in a design, whose progress is being promised. They read "the queue drains", "the lock is not deadlocked", "requests are eventually served" as ambiguous until the subject is named, and they know that the individual version needs either a primitive with the stronger fairness guarantee or an explicit turn-passing mechanism layered on top. They also recognize the free real estate: any region already known to be exclusive is where arbitration can be done cheaply, because nothing can interfere there.

**Source:** [Temporal Verification of Reactive Systems: Progress](../works/temporal-verification-of-reactive-systems-progress.md) — the Response for Parameterized Programs chapter: the distinction drawn between communal and individual accessibility for the turn-setting program, the discussion of add-and-store statements as lacking the strong-fairness property semaphores have and therefore supporting only the communal claim, and the follow-on program that recovers individual accessibility by having the process inside the critical section arbitrate the next entrant past an entrance gate.
