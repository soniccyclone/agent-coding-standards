# Define the shape of a counterexample first; the proof and the checking algorithm are both readings of it

**Lesson:** Manna and Pnueli close their treatment of progress by characterizing, exactly, what a violation of an eventuality claim looks like. An execution violates it precisely when, from some point on, every state it visits is one that still owes the goal — reachable from a triggering state without having reached the goal in between — and the set of states it revisits forever is closed, mutually reachable, and satisfies every fairness obligation the system imposes. Nothing else can be a violation. Once that object is pinned down, two apparently different activities turn out to be the same activity. A proof rule is a way of certifying no such object exists, and a decision procedure is a way of searching for one. The intermediate assertions and rank a deductive proof needs are recoverable from the structure of the states that owe the goal, which is why their completeness argument can manufacture them mechanically.

The payoff of taking the counterexample as primary is that the search is guided by structure rather than by enumeration, and the guidance is not uniform across the fairness notions — which is the sharpest technical observation in the chapter. Weak fairness is inherited downward: if a candidate region fails to honor it, no smaller region inside it can honor it either, so failure prunes the whole region immediately. Strong fairness is not inherited that way. A region can fail it while some region inside it succeeds, because shrinking the region can remove exactly the states where the neglected action was possible. So the algorithm cannot merely test and discard. It must delete the states that witness the violation, re-decompose whatever is left into connected pieces, and recurse. Two conditions that look like siblings behave completely differently under refinement, and getting that wrong yields an algorithm that reports success on systems that in fact violate the property.

The general habit is to define the failure object before designing either the argument or the checker. Ask what a violating execution must look like — what must hold forever, what must recur, what must be neglected — and get that characterization tight and provably exhaustive. Then a proof is the demonstration that the characterization is unsatisfiable, a test is a search for a witness, and the two stay in agreement because they were derived from the same definition. When the argument and the checker are instead designed independently, they drift, and the drift is invisible until something ships wrong.

The second habit concerns refinement behavior. Whenever a property is checked over regions that get subdivided — a graph search, a static analysis over program fragments, a test-shrinking procedure, an incremental verifier — ask whether failure of the property on a region actually implies failure on all sub-regions. If it does, failure is a pruning tool. If it does not, failure is only a reason to try harder somewhere smaller, and treating it as pruning is a soundness bug rather than an optimization.

**Source:** [Temporal Verification of Reactive Systems: Progress](../works/temporal-verification-of-reactive-systems-progress.md) — the Response Under Fairness chapter's completeness sections, where the assertion characterizing goal-owing states is defined and shown to itself entail the goal, and the assertions and rank for a complete deductive proof are constructed from it; and the finite-state algorithmic sections, where computations are put in correspondence with fair strongly connected subgraphs, and the search algorithm exploits the downward inheritance of the weak fairness condition while handling the strong one by deleting witness states and re-decomposing.
