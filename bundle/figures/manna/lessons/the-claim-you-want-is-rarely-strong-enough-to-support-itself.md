# The claim you want is rarely strong enough to support itself, and the failed check tells you what is missing

**Lesson:** Step-induction over a program's executions only closes if the assertion you are carrying is preserved by every step considered in isolation, knowing nothing but the assertion itself. Manna and Pnueli are careful to separate that self-supporting quality from truth, and to point out with a two-line example that the gap is real: an assertion can hold in every reachable state and still fail to be preserved when you look at a single step, because the step is examined from states the assertion permits but the program never actually reaches. The consequence is counterintuitive and central. To prove what you want, you often must prove something that says more, because saying more excludes the phantom states that were breaking the induction. Strength, in this setting, is what makes a claim provable rather than what makes it hard.

The method for finding that stronger claim is the part worth stealing, because it makes the search mechanical instead of inspired. Attempt the check, simplify it as far as it goes, and look at what is left over. If the residue is not a tautology, the residue is the missing claim: adopt it as something to be proven, and proceed. Their Peterson proof is exactly this loop — the mutual-exclusion check collapses to an implication relating one process's position, the other's position, and the shared signature variable, and that implication, which nobody wrote down in advance, becomes the next thing to establish. Working the Dekker proof the same way turns up a further claim about what the turn variable must be while a process sits in its exit region. A failing verification condition is not a dead end reported by the machinery; it is the machinery telling you the content you forgot.

Two structural consequences follow. First, established claims accumulate as context: their incremental rule lets every previously-proven invariant be assumed while checking the next one, so verification produces a growing library of facts about reachable states rather than one monolithic argument, and each new fact makes the next one cheaper. Second, and sharper, an assumption you needed in order to finish an argument is a statement about the design, so it is either a lemma you owe or a bug you just found. Manna and Pnueli hit this squarely in their liveness proof: their diagram tacitly assumes the competitor is never parked in certain positions while a process waits, and they stop to check whether that could happen, observing that if it could, the algorithm would deadlock and the property they are proving would simply be false. The proof's residue was, at that moment, indistinguishable from a bug report.

A programmer who works this way stops treating a stuck correctness argument as a failure of the argument. They read what the argument still needs as a specification of missing knowledge, then ask the one question that decides everything: is this something the system guarantees, in which case prove it and add it to the pile, or is it something the system merely happens to do, in which case there is a defect. They also expect the assertion they end up proving to be stronger and uglier than the property they cared about, and they don't view that as a detour — it is the normal shape of the work.

**Source:** [A Temporal Proof Methodology for Reactive Systems](../works/a-temporal-proof-methodology-for-reactive-systems.md) — the discussion distinguishing inductive from merely invariant assertions and the remedies of strengthening and of incremental proof; the Peterson mutual-exclusion derivation where a non-tautological leftover implication is adopted as a new invariant; and the analysis following the accessibility diagram where a tacit assumption about the competitor's location is examined and tied to the possibility of deadlock.
