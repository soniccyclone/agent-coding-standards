# Write the specification in the formalism it specifies, and optimize it for its readers rather than for its output

**Lesson:** A description of one language usually gets written in another, and that other language then needs describing in turn. Landin cuts the regress by using his core notation for both jobs at once: it is what programs are translated into, and it is also what the translation itself is written in. He is honest that this dual role is partly a coincidence of what he happened to be studying, but he names two real dividends. The reader is not asked to learn an extra language whose only purpose is to talk about the first one. And the description doubles as evidence — an account of a full imperative language, written as a purely applicative program, is a non-trivial demonstration that the notation can carry serious work rather than a claim that it could. A formalism that has never been used to build anything substantial has no track record, and self-application is the cheapest way to acquire one.

The second half of the lesson is about which artifact you are allowed to make ugly. Landin states his priorities: the translation function itself should be short and transparent, and he is willing to pay for that with worse output, accepting that the expressions his mapping produces are neither concise nor transparent themselves. That is exactly the right trade for the artifact he is writing and exactly the wrong one for a production compiler, and the two are easy to confuse because they have the same shape. A definitional translator exists to be read and reasoned about by humans deciding whether it says the right thing; a compiler exists to emit good code. Optimizing either for the other's figure of merit produces something that fails at both. He notes in passing that many of his modelling choices are the same choices a real implementation must make — which is what makes the confusion tempting — while keeping clear that his answers were chosen for a different reason than an implementer's would be.

The consequence is a way of writing specifications that are neither prose nor products. Executable, because they are programs in a formalism with a defined meaning, and so mechanically checkable against examples. Legible, because nothing in them is bent toward efficiency. Landin's own layout convention makes the point almost comically literal: he explains in a footnote that his large formulas are indented by a rule about where a subexpression's characters may sit relative to its first character, so the structure of an intimidating definition can be recovered visually. Someone who cares only about output does not bother writing that footnote.

For practice, two things follow. First, when you build a language, a protocol, or a domain-specific formalism, write the next non-trivial thing you need in it, including the tooling for it if you can — that is the honest test, and its failures are the ones you would otherwise ship to users. Second, when writing an executable specification, say out loud that legibility beats performance in this artifact, and resist every optimization that costs clarity. The moment a reference implementation starts being judged on speed it stops being a reference.

**Source:** [A Correspondence Between ALGOL 60 and Church's Lambda-Notation: Part II](../works/correspondence-algol-60-church-lambda-notation-part-ii.md) — the introduction's note on the notation serving both as the object of analysis and as the medium of the analysis, the stated preference for a concise and transparent semantic function even at the cost of the expressions it produces, the concluding observation that the description is itself a substantial purely functional program, and the footnote defining the indentation discipline used to keep the large definitions readable.
