# Design the space your artifact sits in, and give every decision that could have gone otherwise its own level

**Lesson:** Landin's target is not a language but the habit of building languages one at a time. He observes that anything with more than one implementation ends up a family anyway, whether or not its designers admitted it, and that a great deal of criticism aimed at a particular language is really criticism of one arbitrary point in a space nobody bothered to map. So the useful object of design is the space: settle the framework that all members share, and let each member be a choice of the things that legitimately vary. A new language then becomes a coordinate rather than a construction project, and the question "why is it like this here?" always has an answer of the form "because this dimension was set to that value."

The mechanism he offers for getting there is a ladder of descriptions where each rung refuses to commit to exactly one class of decision. The lowest rung fixes characters and type faces; strip that and you have something committed only to the order of textual elements and how they group; strip that and you have a tree of grammatical categories with no notion of how it is written down at all; strip that and you have a far leaner tree of applications and abstractions into which the richer categories all map. Every rung is a place where a whole family of choices was localized so it cannot contaminate the rungs above it. The payoff is that questions become answerable in isolation — you can argue about brackets without touching what the language can express, and argue about what it can express without touching how it is written — and Landin notes, importantly, that the questions about structure and the questions about meaning cut across each other rather than nesting, so treating them as one list is what produces confusion.

The same laddering explains why he can factor language design into two genuinely independent parts: what programs look like, and what abstract things they can talk about. The first is a matter of taste, medium and human engineering; the second is what determines whether the language is any good for a given kind of work. Conflating them is what produces the ritual claim that a language is oriented toward some application area when the orientation actually lives entirely in the stock of primitives it was handed. A designer who has separated the levels can retarget a language by changing that stock alone and can say precisely what did and did not change.

A practitioner who takes this on stops asking "what should this thing be?" and starts asking "what varies, what is invariant, and where does each varying decision belong?" The immediate discipline is that when a design question feels impossible to settle, it usually means two independent decisions have been fused and the argument is over their product rather than over either one; splitting them makes both tractable. The longer-term discipline is that the invariant core has to be small enough for the whole family to be described by what is left over, which keeps pressure on it. Landin is honest that the picture is a research program more than a finished result — he presents the ladder without answering most of the questions it raises — and that honesty is part of the method: the value is in having a frame that makes the open questions individually askable.

**Source:** [The Next 700 Programming Languages](../works/next-700-programming-languages.md) — the opening argument that a language is better launched as a family, the enumeration of questions that adding a supporting-definition construct forces you to answer separately for structure and for meaning, and the section laying out the four-level stack from a concrete written form down to bare applicative expressions.
