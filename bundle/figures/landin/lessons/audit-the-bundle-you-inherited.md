# Features arrive welded together; audit which dependencies are real and which restrictions are accidents

**Lesson:** Landin opens by refusing to take a familiar bundle at face value. Jumping arrives in every language of his day alongside two companions — a mutable store and user-written destination names — and everyone treats the association as necessity. He asks instead whether either companion is load-bearing. Can a language jump without having assignment at all? Is there some operation that does the work of jumping and is meaningful before any notion of a written label exists? Both answers turn out to be yes, and the yeses are what produce a construct nobody had. The method generalizes past his subject: whenever two capabilities always appear together, at least one of the two associations is probably a habit of implementation rather than a fact about the concepts, and the habit is invisible precisely because nothing in the ecosystem contradicts it.

The complementary half of the audit is to look at analogies you already accept and notice where they only hold in a corner. Landin observes that in the language he is analyzing, a label behaves so much like the name of a procedure that takes no arguments and returns no result that the jump keyword itself carries no information and a processor could ignore it. Most people stop there, satisfied with an explanation. He runs the analogy backwards and asks what would stand to procedures that take arguments and produce results as a label stands to the degenerate kind. That question has no answer in the existing language, which is exactly the point: an unfilled cell in a correspondence is not a curiosity, it is an unexplored region of design space with a specification already attached. The missing thing must be a destination that takes arguments and delivers a result, and that specification is enough to go build it.

Both halves depend on having a frame precise enough to state the questions in. Landin is explicit that claims about languages in general are usually vague and claims about one language are usually parochial, and that his escape from both is a model of language structure built earlier for exactly this purpose. That is the discipline behind the method: an audit of what depends on what is only meaningful if you have somewhere neutral to stand, so building the neutral vocabulary is prior work you do to make the design questions askable at all.

What a practitioner does differently is treat every co-occurrence in an inherited system as a claim requiring proof. When a subsystem is only reachable through a particular transport, when a lifecycle hook is only available on a particular kind of object, when an error mechanism is only usable from a particular kind of frame — ask whether the pairing follows from anything, or whether it is just where things happened to be built. And when you find yourself explaining that A is really a special case of B, do not stop at the explanation; find which parameters of B the special case pinned down, and unpin them one at a time. Landin also demonstrates the honest end of the exercise: having built the general thing, he says outright that the familiar special case may turn out to be the least useful member of the family, and elsewhere flags an awkward interaction between his new operator and recursive definition as something that further experiment should judge rather than something he has resolved.

**Source:** [A Generalization of Jumps and Labels](../works/generalization-of-jumps-and-labels.md) — the three questions posed in the introduction about whether jumping requires assignment or requires labels, the reversal of the label/parameterless-procedure analogy into a demand for the parameterful and result-returning counterpart, and the closing statement of what the analysis separated.
