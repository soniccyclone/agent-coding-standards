# Written order is a claim about order, and most programs assert far more of it than they mean

**Lesson:** Landin reframes the old sport of rewriting programs to eliminate jumps as one instance of something larger: reducing how much of a program's content is carried by the order its parts are written in. Sequencing is not free notation. Every time you put one command after another you have asserted that this one happens first, and the machine, the compiler and the next reader are all entitled to believe you. When the assertion is true it is essential; when it is incidental — when you wrote the steps in some order because text has to have an order — you have handed down a constraint you never intended, and nothing downstream can tell the two cases apart.

The cost is easiest to see where the accidental constraints outnumber the real ones. Multiplying two matrices requires a great many individual products whose order genuinely does not matter, so long as the right ones are added into the right sums; an ordinary imperative rendering fixes one arbitrary order for all of them, and every later attempt to reorganize the computation for speed has to first prove that the order it is about to violate was never load-bearing. Composing expressions instead of decomposing into commands leaves precisely the ordering that the data dependencies demand — a partial order, not a total one — and Landin's own formulation of the point in the discussion following the paper is that turning nested expressions into a sequence of commands means volunteering a great deal of unnecessary information about sequencing. Under-specification here is a feature: what you did not say is exactly the freedom an implementation is allowed to use.

He also cares about this for reasons that have nothing to do with speed, and this is where the lesson earns its keep beyond parallelism. His observation is that reducing explicit sequencing tends to yield programs that are easier to understand, debug, change, and drop into something larger — and the mechanism is the same. Where order does not appear, order does not have to be tracked, and a reader who does not have to hold a history in mind can judge a fragment on its own. This is also why he treats the practical route as gradual rather than doctrinal: merge independent assignments, turn assignments whose significance is local into local definitions, grow the arms of conditionals so branching replaces jumping. Each step converts asserted order into either real dependency or nothing at all.

The habit this produces is to look at any sequence in your code and ask which of its adjacencies are actual dependencies. The ones that are not should be expressed in a form that does not imply them — a set rather than a list, a comprehension rather than a loop with an accumulator, a composition rather than a series of statements over shared state. Two payoffs follow: an implementation gains room to reorder, batch or distribute the work without needing your permission, and a reader stops having to reconstruct which of your orderings mattered. Landin's closing note on where this gets hard is worth keeping too — the sequencing that resists elimination tends to cluster around failure and what must be done in response to it, which is a fair warning that the last of the order to go is the order that concerns what happens when things go wrong.

**Source:** [The Next 700 Programming Languages](../works/next-700-programming-languages.md) — the section on eliminating explicit sequencing, with its recipe for progressively converting imperative order into dependency and its observation about failure-handling being the residue, together with the exchange in the paper's recorded discussion contrasting the partial order that composed expressions impose against the total order commands impose.
