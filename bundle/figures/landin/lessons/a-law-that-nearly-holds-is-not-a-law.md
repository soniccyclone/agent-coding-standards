# A rule that almost always holds buys nothing; price every feature in the laws it breaks

**Lesson:** Landin points out that nearly every language carries folklore of the form "a construct shaped like this can be swapped for one shaped like that" — and that in nearly every language such statements hold with exceptions. He refuses to treat this as a pedantic complaint. His position is that an exceptionless equivalence is a different kind of object from one that mostly works: only the exceptionless kind can be applied to a fragment inside a larger program without first proving the surrounding program is not one of the exceptions. Once you have to check the context, the rule has stopped being a rule and become a hypothesis, and every mechanical use of it — optimizing, checking a program against a condition, constructing a program that satisfies one — has to carry the checking machinery around with it. He therefore sets out the equivalences of his system as the primary specification, organized by what they range over: substitution inside a subexpression, the handling of names the user introduced, the constructs whose meaning is really a hidden built-in, and whatever the chosen primitives contribute.

The second half of the thought is the accounting that falls out. The most valuable of his rules — replacing a name by what it was defined as — turns out to hold only within the part of the language that has neither assignment nor his jump-like construct. He does not hide this or wave it away; he states the restriction and identifies the resulting research problem as finding the regions of a program where the imperative features can be safely ignored. That inverts the usual way a feature is evaluated. The naive ledger records that assignment was added and the language got more capable. The honest ledger records that assignment was added and a law that previously held everywhere now holds only somewhere, so every argument built on that law must be re-scoped, and the tooling that relied on it needs a way to know which region it is standing in. Capability was bought with reasoning power, at a price nobody wrote down.

This is why he cares about the ordering property that says a rewrite can be carried out in several ways and still land in the same place. A confluence result like that is what makes a pile of local rewrite rules usable as a calculus rather than as a bag of tricks whose outcome depends on the order you happened to try them. Without it, "simplify this expression" is not a well-defined operation. With it, the whole set becomes a proof system, and he demonstrates the point by deriving one program's equivalence to another as a named sequence of rule applications — the kind of thing a machine could check or perform.

A programmer who internalizes this evaluates abstractions by their laws rather than by their features. Before adopting a construct, ask what identities become false in its presence: does it break the substitution of a value for its name, the commuting of two independent steps, the interchangeability of a call with its inlined body? Whatever breaks is what refactoring, compiling and reviewing will silently get wrong in exactly the cases nobody tests. And when a feature genuinely must be added at the cost of a law, the follow-up obligation is to define the boundary of the region where the law survives, sharply enough that anyone can tell which side of it they are on.

**Source:** [The Next 700 Programming Languages](../works/next-700-programming-languages.md) — the section arguing for equivalences that hold without exception and grouping them by scope, the frank restriction of the substitution rule to the non-imperative subset together with the "safe areas" research problem it raises, and the worked derivation of one program's equivalence to another as a sequence of named rule applications.
