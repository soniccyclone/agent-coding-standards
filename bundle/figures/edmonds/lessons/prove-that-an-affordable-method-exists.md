# Whether an affordable method exists at all is a claim you can prove or refute, so state it and pick a cost measure that cannot be gamed

**Lesson:** Most engineers stop reasoning once they have established that a procedure terminates. Edmonds's argument is that termination is the uninteresting half: a procedure that enumerates every combination also terminates, and it is worthless. The interesting question is how the work grows with the input, and that question is a mathematical proposition in its own right, not a benchmarking chore. Once you are willing to say out loud "no method whose cost grows only algebraically exists for this problem," you have something that can be attacked, refuted, or proved, and the search for a fast method acquires a target instead of a hope. The posture that goes with it is disciplined humility: a conjecture that no cheap method exists is legitimate precisely because nobody knows, and admitting the ignorance is what makes the conjecture worth stating rather than a claim of defeat.

Making the claim precise forces a choice of cost measure, and the choice matters more than it looks. Asymptotic growth is the right currency for two reasons the paper makes explicit. First, it is unriggable: you cannot flatter an algorithm by making it artificially expensive on small inputs, because the measure only cares about behavior as inputs grow. Second, it judges a method against itself rather than only against whatever rivals happen to exist, so a result about it survives the arrival of a better competitor. Underneath both sits an assumption worth naming, since it is the same move logic makes with computability: any reasonable procedure can be re-expressed in elemental steps whose costs are comparable across machines, so the growth rate is a property of the method rather than of the hardware it was measured on. That is what licenses reasoning about cost at design time, before any code exists, and it is why a conceptual description of an algorithm can carry a rigorous cost claim while saying nothing about registers or instruction timings.

The same section that establishes the measure warns against worshipping it. A rigid criterion applied as a gate will reject methods that are excellent in practice but whose growth nobody has characterized, and it will reject whole families of useful engineering because they were never designed to satisfy it. The measure earns its keep by motivating the search for cheap methods and by letting the goal be stated as a concrete conjecture, not by serving as an admission test. A programmer who has absorbed this does two things differently: before writing anything, states what growth rate the problem is believed to admit and why; and when a method fails that standard, treats the gap as an open question about the problem rather than as a verdict on the method.
