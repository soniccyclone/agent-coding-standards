# When cost tracks the magnitude of your numbers, restructure the computation to follow their digits: solve a coarse version, then refine, and bound how far each answer can be from the next

**Lesson:** A method whose work grows with the size of the numbers in its input is doing something equivalent to counting, and the remedy is the same one that turned tally marks into positional notation: make the computation's structure mirror the data's representation rather than its value. Edmonds and Karp build a sequence of derived problems by truncating the low-order bits of every quantity, solve the crudest one, and then reintroduce one bit of precision at a time, each stage inheriting the previous stage's answer scaled up as its starting point. The total effort ends up proportional to the number of digits it takes to write the data down instead of to what the data denotes. The authors name the analogy themselves: the relation between this scheme and the original is the relation between binary and unary arithmetic.

Two properties make the scheme work, and neither is free. The first is that the coarse problems are genuinely cheaper for a structural reason — at reduced precision the improvements available are on a coarser scale, so fewer of them are needed — rather than merely being smaller instances. The second is the part that carries the proof burden: an answer to the coarse problem, scaled up, must be provably close to an answer for the finer problem. Closeness is not automatic. Doubling an optimal coarse answer generally yields something that is feasible but not optimal for the next stage, off by at most a bounded amount per element, and the whole approach lives or dies on that "at most". Edmonds and Karp supply a repair step and a numeric measure of how far the warm start is from acceptable, then show each repair strictly reduces that measure and the measure starts small, which bounds the repair work by the structural size. Refinement is cheap because the drift is bounded and the bound is proved, not hoped for.

Generalized, this is a discipline for any computation whose cost is sensitive to precision, resolution, or magnitude: image scales, geometric tolerances, monetary amounts, iteration counts driven by convergence thresholds. Approach the target resolution through a ladder of coarser ones, and treat each rung's output as the next rung's initial state. But the design work is not the ladder — that part is easy to imagine. The design work is establishing that a solution at one resolution is within a bounded, cheaply repairable distance of a solution at the next, and having a concrete monotone quantity that the repair drives down. Without that, a warm start is just an unjustified guess that may be arbitrarily bad, and the ladder buys nothing.

The habit this instils is to ask, whenever a cost depends on the values in the data rather than on their quantity, whether the values' own encoding suggests a decomposition of the work. Often it does, and the resulting method has the pleasant property that its bound is stated in the same units as the input's length — which is what makes it a bound you can quote to someone with data you have never seen.

**Source:** [Theoretical Improvements in Algorithmic Efficiency for Network Flow Problems](../works/theoretical-improvements-in-algorithmic-efficiency-for-network-flow-problems.md) — the later sections introducing the scaled sequence of derived problems for the transportation and general minimum-cost cases: the two stated reasons the approximate problems are cheaper, the difficulty that a doubled coarse solution fails the finer problem's optimality conditions by a bounded amount, the repair procedure with its monotone reduction argument, and the closing observation that the resulting bound is essentially the length of the input's binary encoding.
