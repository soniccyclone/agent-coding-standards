# Where a method leaves a step's choice free, that choice decides the cost, so resolve it deliberately with a proof instead of letting the implementation resolve it by accident

**Lesson:** The classical procedure for maximum flow is not one procedure. Its central instruction says to find some improvement and apply it, without saying which improvement, and every published correctness argument goes through for any resolution of that "some". Edmonds and Karp show that the unresolved word is carrying the entire performance story. Left unresolved, the procedure can need a number of rounds proportional to a magnitude written in the data, and with data whose values share no common unit it can fail to finish at all, converging on something that is not the answer. Resolve it one way — always take an improvement crossing the fewest elements — and the round count is bounded by a cube of the structural size alone, with no reference to the data's values or their commensurability. The correctness proof did not change. The algorithm, in the only sense that matters to whoever has to run it, did.

Two consequences follow, and the second is the uncomfortable one. First, a specification that leaves a step nondeterministic has not specified an algorithm; it has specified a family, and the family's members differ by more than constant factors. If you inherit such a specification and want a cost claim, the claim has to be attached to a resolution rule, and earning it means a genuine proof — here, that a certain progress measure never moves backwards and must strictly advance often enough. Second, the good resolution can be almost invisible as code. The rule that produces the polynomial bound amounts to servicing pending items in the order they arrived rather than in whatever order they happen to be stored: a queue where an unordered collection was written. The authors remark that it is simple enough to be adopted without anyone noticing. So an implementation may already be enjoying a bound that nobody in the project can state, held in place by an incidental data-structure choice that a future refactor will silently discard.

The paper also shows that the resolution rule is a design parameter rather than a single right answer. A different rule — always take the largest available improvement — yields a bound of a completely different character, logarithmic in the answer's magnitude but requiring the data to be whole numbers, and neither bound dominates the other across the useful range. The authors go further and give a scheme where a chosen weighting on elements induces a corresponding bound, so the family of rules and the family of bounds correspond. That is the mature version of the idea: the free choice becomes an explicit knob whose settings you can reason about, compare, and pick between on the basis of what your data actually looks like.

A programmer who has absorbed this reads their own code hunting for the unspecified steps — which pending item to process, which candidate to try, which order to iterate a set — and asks of each whether any cost claim in the design depends on it. Where one does, the resolution gets named, commented as load-bearing, and defended by the argument that makes it work; where none does, the freedom is real and can be spent elsewhere. The failure mode this prevents is the common one where a system's performance rests on an undocumented accident, behaves well for years, and collapses when someone swaps a container for an equivalent-looking one.

**Source:** [Theoretical Improvements in Algorithmic Efficiency for Network Flow Problems](../works/theoretical-improvements-in-algorithmic-efficiency-for-network-flow-problems.md) — the opening treatment of the classical labelling procedure, its pathological and non-terminating behaviour under an unconstrained choice of improvement, and the two refinements that constrain the choice: the scan-order discipline yielding a bound in the node count alone, and the largest-improvement discipline yielding a bound depending on the data, together with the generalization parameterizing the rule by a weighting.
