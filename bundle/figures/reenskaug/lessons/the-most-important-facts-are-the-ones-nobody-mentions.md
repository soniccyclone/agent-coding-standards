# What matters most about how work gets done is what nobody thinks to mention

**Lesson:** There are three ways to learn how a piece of work actually happens: ask the people in charge, ask the people doing it, or watch it happen. These do not converge on one answer with three levels of accuracy — they produce three genuinely different pictures, each containing something the others lack, because the first two report *perceptions* and perceptions differ from person to person as well as from practice. Treating any single source as the ground truth means silently adopting its distortions.

The part that changes how you work is subtler than "triangulate your sources." The aspects of a job that matter most are frequently so obvious to the people doing it that mentioning them would never occur to them. They are not withholding anything; the knowledge is not available for reporting, because it has stopped being noticeable. Which means the gaps in what you are told are not random — they concentrate exactly on the load-bearing parts. You cannot fix this by asking better questions of the same kind, because the questions are built from your own model of the work, and the invisible parts are precisely those absent from that model. Only watching, and asking a great many small questions about things that seem too trivial to ask about, will surface them.

The same blind spot has a version pointing inward, and it is worth guarding against explicitly: the tendency to conclude people are unintelligent because they lack fluency in your specialty's concepts and vocabulary. Underneath that impression is almost always your own total ignorance of *their* competence, concepts and vocabulary — an ignorance you have no way to see, for the same reason they cannot see theirs. This is why meeting people in their own terms is a working technique and not merely courtesy: if the users have some notation of their own, use it even if you think yours is better, because your notation encodes your model and your model is the thing under test.

**Source:** [Working With Objects: The OOram Software Engineering Method](../works/working-with-objects-the-ooram-software-engineering-method.md) — chapter 7's section on understanding the problem, citing Elanor Wynn's finding that asking managers, asking workers, and observing the work each yield different insights; the accompanying warning that the most important aspects will be too obvious to the participants to mention and must be sought through observation and numerous questions; and the caution about mistaking unfamiliarity with one's own terminology for stupidity.
