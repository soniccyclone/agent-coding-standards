# Your process is varnish over your people's competence, and it only holds where that competence already reaches

**Lesson:** An organization with mature procedures, detailed plans and good tooling naturally attributes its results to those artifacts — that is what they were built for and what management can see. The test comes when the work changes underneath them. A shipyard that had spent years refining its planning formalisms for building tankers switched almost overnight to building offshore oil platforms; the plans were dutifully remoulded, and the production problems turned out worse than the worst case anyone had modelled. The head of planning's conclusion is the useful part: he had believed the formalisms controlled production, and he was wrong — what actually controlled it was a crew of people who knew how to build ships. The plans were a thin coat on top of that, worth a marginal improvement in effectiveness. The trouble came because the collective competence had never encountered the new product.

The implication cuts against the reason formalization is usually funded. If procedures were the mechanism, they would transfer to new problems and reduce dependence on particular skilled people. Because they are a finishing layer over competence, they transfer only as far as the competence does — which is bad news for anyone hoping to convert expertise into a process executable by interchangeable staff, and correspondingly good news for practitioners who suspect they are not replaceable. They are not.

This does not devalue the artifacts, and the distinction matters for how you invest. Patterns, frameworks, documented procedures and reusable components are all worth building; they let competent people be more effective and they spread hard-won solutions. What they cannot do is substitute for the cooperation, creativity and skill they sit on top of. The same limitation shows up in the pattern literature's own origins: the architectural pattern language that inspired the software movement presupposes throughout that the architect knows how to design and the builder knows how to build — it says nothing about notation, structural engineering, or sound construction practice. Its patterns are hints exchanged between people who already share a craft. Its author later tried the theory in practice and was disappointed, concluding that the creative process mattered as much as the patterns. So the honest reading of any methodology is: this is leverage on competence you must already have, and the question to ask before adopting it is whether your team has the competence it is levering.

**Source:** [Working With Objects: The OOram Software Engineering Method](../works/working-with-objects-the-ooram-software-engineering-method.md) — chapter 5's boxed shipyard anecdote and the chief planner's assessment, the surrounding statement that formal methods can support but not replace human cooperation, creativity and competence, and the discussion of Alexander's pattern language presupposing a strong common culture together with his later reported disappointment.
