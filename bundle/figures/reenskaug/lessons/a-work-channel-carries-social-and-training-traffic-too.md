# The channel you are automating is also carrying the social and teaching traffic

**Lesson:** Look at an exchange between two people doing a job and you will see the work content — order five boxes of paper, send this to accounting. Look more carefully and the same exchange is usually carrying at least two other things simultaneously: a social strand that maintains the relationship, and a teaching strand where one person passes on how things are done here. These are interwoven, not sequential, and only the first strand is visible in any description of the process.

The consequence for anyone replacing a manual process with a system is uncomfortable. You will faithfully implement the work strand, because that is the one that appears in the requirements, and you will silently remove the other two, because nobody wrote them down and nobody thought to. The process keeps functioning on paper. What degrades is the transmission of local knowledge and the relationships that make people willing to help each other, and the degradation shows up somewhere else entirely, attributed to something else, long after the change.

The illustration is exact and hard to argue with. A computer centre director grew irritated at the crowd perpetually gathered around a coffee machine that happened to sit next to the printer, and had the machine removed. He then had to hire three additional advisors — because the cluster of people he had read as idleness was the mechanism by which students got their problems solved, and it had been running for free next to the printer. Nothing in a description of how the centre worked would have shown it.

So when you are about to replace, relocate or automate an existing channel, the question worth asking is not only "what work flows through here" but "what else flows through here, and where will it go instead." Sometimes the answer is nowhere, and the cost lands months later on a different budget line.

**Source:** [Working With Objects: The OOram Software Engineering Method](../works/working-with-objects-the-ooram-software-engineering-method.md) — chapter 7's caution about the "soft" aspects of work processes, citing Wynn's finding that office communication interweaves work, social and training aspects and warning that introducing a system for the work aspect may play havoc with essential organizational processes, followed by the coffee-machine-next-to-the-printer anecdote attributed to Weinberg.
