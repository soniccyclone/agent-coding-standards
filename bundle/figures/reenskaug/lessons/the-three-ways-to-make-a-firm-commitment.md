# There are exactly three ways to promise a delivery date, and two of them are honest

**Lesson:** Estimating creative work is compared here to estimating how long a crossword will take: you know roughly where you are, where you want to be, and what stands between. Most of it goes as expected. Some difficulties evaporate on closer inspection — and that gain is more than cancelled by the few that turn out to be genuinely hard. The distribution is asymmetric, which is why averaging your experience does not rescue you, and the author says frankly that he finds such estimation extremely hard despite decades of practice.

From that he draws a trilemma, and its value is in being exhaustive rather than clever. There are three ways to make a firm commitment. Fix the goal and let time and resources move. Fix time and resources and let the detailed specification of the goal move. Or grow the bureaucracy around the project until it dominates the unknown creative part — at which point your estimate becomes reliable because most of the work being estimated is now process rather than invention. That third option is stated without irony and it is worth taking seriously as a description of what large formal organizations actually do, and of what it costs them.

The useful discipline is to notice that all three fix *something* and let something else float, so any commitment claiming to fix scope, schedule and resources simultaneously is not a fourth option — it is one of the three with the floating variable left unstated, and it will be discovered later by whoever is holding it. Asked for a firm date, the honest reply is to name which of the three you are in. The author's own case is instructive: his estimate was wrong, the work was harder than expected, and both time and resources were already fixed by customer commitments — so what actually flexed was scope, absorbed through several iterations that the written-up account compresses into one.

**Source:** [Working With Objects: The OOram Software Engineering Method](../works/working-with-objects-the-ooram-software-engineering-method.md) — chapter 9's boxed note on the difficulty of resource estimation with extensive reuse, which offers the crossword-puzzle comparison, the observation that vanishing problems are more than offset by a few very hard ones, and the three ways of making firm project commitments; followed by the admission that the framework work was harder than estimated while time and resources were fixed, forcing several iterations.
