# Rotate people between building and generalizing, alternately — holding both at once collapses to the nearer deadline

**Lesson:** Given two kinds of work that both must happen — delivering specific systems under deadline, and turning what was delivered into reusable assets — the staffing question is who does which. Three arrangements are on offer and only one survives. Separate populations permanently: this is the standard answer and it fails in both directions, since a group that only builds reusable assets cannot understand the rough-and-tumble of front-line work well enough to build the right ones, and the front line does not adopt what such a group produces. Give everyone both duties at once: this fails through a mechanism worth naming precisely, because whoever holds two mandates simultaneously will serve the one with the nearer deadline, every time, which means the investment work is not merely deprioritized but structurally unreachable. Alternate the same people between the two over time: this is the one that works.

The argument for alternation is symmetric and it is about calibration rather than fairness. There is no bound on how filthy a program a motivated delivery engineer will write to satisfy an urgent request, and the correction is not a standards document but the experience of cleaning up someone else's mess and learning firsthand what the mess costs. Equally, there is no bound on how long a motivated generalizer will spend perfecting a solution to a problem nobody has, and the correction is a period under a real deadline, which teaches which assets actually help and — the author's phrase — the total irrelevance of fancy solutions to non-existent problems. Each role has a characteristic blind spot, each blind spot is invisible from inside the role, and only occupancy of the other role reveals it.

Two further points make this operational. The adoption problem is solved as a side effect rather than addressed directly: circulating people is the transfer mechanism, which is a much better answer than the usual attempts to market internal tooling to colleagues who have no reason to trust it. And the roles being rotated through are roles, not persons — the mapping between them is many-to-many and reassignable, which is what makes rotation a structural option rather than a matter of individual generosity.

The compact form is that all builders benefit from taking their own medicine: being users of what they built, consumers of their own reusable parts, and cleaners of their own output. Whether they will do so is not a question of character but of whether the schedule ever lets them, which is why the timing detail — alternately, not simultaneously — is the load-bearing part of the advice.

**Source:** [Working With Objects: The OOram Software Engineering Method](../works/working-with-objects-the-ooram-software-engineering-method.md) — chapter 10 section 10.3 on job rotation: organization models are role models with many-to-many mappings to people, who should alternate between production and experience collection; the symmetric arguments about the keen production programmer learning what dirty code costs and the keen experience collector learning the irrelevance of fancy solutions to non-existent problems; the "does it scale" answer that a methodology section struggles to get front-line adoption and that someone working exclusively on reusable assets cannot understand front-line programming; and the insistence on alternately rather than simultaneously, because activities with the nearest deadline take priority.
