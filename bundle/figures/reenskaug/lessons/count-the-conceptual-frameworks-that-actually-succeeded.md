# Three conceptual frameworks succeeded in fifty years — price your own proposal against that base rate

**Lesson:** Immediately after describing a promising route to a new general-purpose way of specifying systems, the author interrupts himself with a count: the conceptual frameworks that have actually succeeded across the whole history of the field can be enumerated on one hand, and he names three — the activity network, the database, and the spreadsheet. Introducing a simple, powerful, widely applicable framework is, in his words, clearly not a routine matter. The interruption comes from someone in the middle of proposing one, which is what gives it force.

The value here is a base rate, and base rates are the correction that enthusiasm structurally cannot supply. Every proposal for a new organizing framework is argued on its internal merits — the notion is elegant, it composes, it covers the cases we have tried — and those arguments are the same ones the failures made. What distinguishes the three survivors is not that their ideas were better in some way visible in advance; it is that each captured a shape common enough that a very small vocabulary covered an enormous range of real problems, and each arrived with tooling that made the specification the whole deliverable. Those are extremely demanding conditions and there is no way to verify them from inside the design.

So the practical use is calibration on your own work. Building a small framework for a known domain with a known set of users is ordinary engineering and usually succeeds. Building one intended to be broadly applicable is attempting something with roughly three successes in the field's history, which means the prior against you is overwhelming and should change what you do: aim at a bounded domain, treat wide applicability as an outcome you might discover rather than a goal you design toward, and be suspicious of the specific feeling that your notion is general — since that feeling accompanies every attempt, successful or not.

The wider habit is to ask, of any ambitious category of work, how often it has actually worked and to let the answer set the ambition. It is the cheapest available correction and it applies with most force exactly when a design feels most promising, which is why it has to be deliberate: nothing in the design process generates it, and the author had to stop himself mid-argument to apply it to his own proposal.

**Source:** [Working With Objects: The OOram Software Engineering Method](../works/working-with-objects-the-ooram-software-engineering-method.md) — chapter 11 section 11.3, where after arguing that object technology looks very promising as a vehicle for extensible conceptual frameworks the author cautions against getting carried away, calling it a sobering thought that the successful conceptual frameworks created over the past fifty years can be counted on one hand — the activity network, the database and the spreadsheet come to mind — and that the successful introduction of a simple, powerful and widely applicable conceptual framework is clearly not a routine matter.
