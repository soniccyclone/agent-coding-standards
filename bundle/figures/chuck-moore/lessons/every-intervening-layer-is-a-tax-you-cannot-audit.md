# Count the layers standing between you and the machine, because each one silently sets your limits

**Lesson:** Conventional system software is a stack of languages, each defining the one above it: something describes the compiler and the supervisor, the supervisor describes how jobs are controlled, the compiler describes application programs, and the application describes the form of its own input. A programmer usually knows only one or two of these and is unaware the others exist, yet every one of them constrains what can be attempted and adds to what it costs. The layers were each built by people solving their own problem, and the sum of their decisions arrives as an environment nobody designed and nobody can account for. The right first question about any such stack is not how to work within it but how much of it is load-bearing.

The argument against the tower is not aesthetic. Producing and maintaining that many interlocking languages consumes enormous effort, documenting them consumes more, reading the documentation consumes more still, and after all of it the results remain defective and unpleasant to use. The failure is traceable to the structure: with the definitional relationships spread across independently maintained artifacts, no one is positioned to make the whole thing coherent. Replacing the stack with a single layer removes not just the code but the coordination problem. What remains is two interfaces, one from the person to the system and one from the system to the machine, and both are small enough to be understood by one person.

Collapsing the tower has a consequence that looks like a bonus and is really the point. A system that defines its own compiler, its own storage handling, and its own means of interpreting text can regenerate itself, and once it can do that it no longer depends on the vendor tooling that started it. Ancestral dependence otherwise never ends: whatever built your system dictates its instruction selection, its storage layout, and its compatibility obligations forever. Cutting the line makes those decisions yours again. A programmer who thinks this way treats each layer as something to be justified rather than assumed, and is willing to conclude that an operating system, on hardware with enough memory to hold the whole application, is a historical artifact rather than a requirement.

Also [The Evolution of Forth](../works/the-evolution-of-forth.md) — the philosophy section quoting and expanding that argument, and the account of a tiny nucleus compiling the rest of its own environment from source. Also [colorForth: Programming Language and Operating System](../works/colorforth-documentation.md) — the rationale section, which argues that on modern memory an operating system is unnecessary and supplies drivers and multitasking in kilobytes instead. Also [Forth - The Early Years](../works/forth-the-early-years.md) / [Chuck Moore: The Invention of Forth](../works/the-invention-of-forth.md) — the account of a system able to interpret an assembler that assembles a compiler that compiles the interpreter, and the author's decision to abandon the language-about-language terminology as unhelpful.
