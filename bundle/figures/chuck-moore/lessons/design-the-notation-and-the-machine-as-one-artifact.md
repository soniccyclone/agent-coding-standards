# When the notation and the mechanism are designed together, the translation between them stops existing

**Lesson:** A language that describes computation in terms of two stacks and a small set of operations is describing a machine. Normally that machine is fictional and the real hardware underneath must be persuaded to imitate it, which is what compilers spend their effort on. But the direction of accommodation is a choice. Build the physical instruction set around the operations the notation actually uses and the imitation layer disappears: the sequencing step between one named operation and the next collapses to a couple of instructions, and the compiler's job reduces to selecting instructions that already correspond one-to-one with the source. The generalizable idea is that a persistent, expensive translation between a representation and its substrate is evidence that one of the two was designed without regard for the other, and either end can be moved.

The same reasoning applies well outside processor design. A description language for physical chip geometry, if its constructs are chosen to line up with the interchange format that fabrication consumes, produces that format directly and without approximation, because there is nothing to reconcile. Once the mapping is exact, a single description can be compiled in one context to produce a layout for inspection and simulation and in another to produce the fabrication file, with no separate translator and no possibility of the two disagreeing. Choosing the units of description to enforce the process's geometric constraints does the same work at a lower level: constraints become unrepresentable rather than checked.

The habit extends to numeric representation, where the discipline is to use what the hardware natively does rather than to simulate a more comfortable abstraction on top of it. Combining a multiply and a divide into one operation with a wide intermediate result mirrors what the instructions actually provide and makes scaling and rational approximation direct; a division that yields both parts of its result at once mirrors the same thing. Working in scaled binary fractions instead of simulating floating point turns out to be simultaneously simpler, faster, and more accurate for the quantities involved. The programmer who thinks this way asks, of any abstraction, what it is costing to maintain against the machine, and treats the answer as a design input rather than an implementation detail.
