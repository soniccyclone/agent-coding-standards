# Judge an observation by what it reorganizes, not by whether it shortens the proof in front of you

**Lesson:** Tracing how his construction came about, Scott records a miss of his own and does not soften it. Five years earlier, in a passing remark at the end of a section of an earlier paper, he had written down the exact fact this entire construction rests on — that many spaces contain their own function spaces as well-behaved subspaces — and dismissed it in a single sentence on the grounds that it did not appear to help much in proving theorems. He then reproduces his own dismissal word for word rather than paraphrasing it away, and states plainly that had he taken his own advice at the time he would have seen the whole thing years earlier.

The error was in the criterion, not the observation. "Does this help with the theorems I am currently trying to prove" is a reasonable question and a terrible filter, because it measures an observation's value strictly inside the framework you already have. The observations that matter most are the ones that change which framework you work in, and that kind of value is invisible by construction to a test conducted from inside the old one. So a fact can be simultaneously true, written down, correctly judged useless for the task at hand, and the basis of everything you do five years later. Those verdicts do not contradict each other, which is exactly why the filter feels sound while it is discarding the important thing.

The practical form is to keep a channel for observations that struck you as interesting but not immediately useful, and to go back through it when the problem changes rather than when you happen to remember — because the reason a fact was shelved is a property of the problem you had at the time, not of the fact. And when you discover you shelved something that mattered, publish the miss with the original wording intact, as Scott does. A record of what a competent person dismissed, and on what grounds, teaches more than the polished path does, because it exposes the shape of the filter that let the thing through, and the filter is what will do it again.

**Source:** [Data Types as Lattices](../works/data-types-as-lattices.md) — Appendix B's account of the origins of the construction, where Scott notes that had he taken his own advice from his 1971 paper on continuous lattices he would have seen that many spaces have their own continuous-function spaces as computable retracts, which is exactly the basis of the present construction, and instead quotes his own earlier remark dismissing that representation as not seeming to be of much help in proving theorems.
