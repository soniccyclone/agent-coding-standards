# Condemning an approach obliges you to supply the alternative

**Lesson:** Scott opens his acknowledgments by crediting a collaborator with supplying what he calls the necessary irritation. The man was not a trained mathematician and used a formal calculus as freely as ordinary algebra, assuming the operations made sense without inspecting whether they did. Scott had repeatedly and publicly condemned that calculus as a formal system with no clear foundations. His account of what happened next is the entire lesson in one clause: having said so out loud and often, it fell to him to provide something else.

That is the expensive form of disagreement and the only useful one. Declaring an approach unfounded is cheap and is frequently correct; it is also of no help whatsoever to the person using it, who has work to do and no substitute on offer. The obligation the objection creates falls on the objector, and discharging it is what converts a complaint into a contribution. Notice too which direction the value flowed. The irritation was necessary — someone confidently doing the thing you say cannot be justified is a better prompt than agreement, because their confidence is evidence that something real is working and your objection is evidence that nobody yet knows what. Two people each holding half of that is a research program; either one alone is a stalemate.

The ending is what makes the episode worth keeping. Scott's attempts at a replacement led, over several years and several false starts, to constructions that vindicated the free manipulation he had condemned — the informal practice turned out to be justified, by machinery that did not exist while it was being practiced. So the outcome of taking his own objection seriously enough to build on it was not that the critic won; it was that the practice acquired the foundation it had been missing, and the critic is the one who supplied it. If you set out to replace something you believe is unfounded, be prepared for the honest result to be that you have founded it instead. That is a success, and it is only available to someone who builds rather than argues.

**Source:** [Data Types as Lattices](../works/data-types-as-lattices.md) — the opening of Appendix B, where Scott describes his debt to Christopher Strachey for stimulus, encouragement and necessary irritation, notes that Strachey used the lambda calculus as freely as everyday algebra without examining whether the operations made sense, states that having repeatedly and outspokenly condemned that calculus as lacking clear mathematical foundations it was up to him to provide an alternative, and traces the sequence of attempts that followed, concluding that the algebra was justified though the work of justifying it was tiring.
