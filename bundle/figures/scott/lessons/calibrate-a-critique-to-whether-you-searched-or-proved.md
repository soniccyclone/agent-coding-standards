# Calibrate a critique to whether you failed to find something or proved it cannot exist

**Lesson:** There is a large distance between "I have looked hard for an interpretation of this system and not found one" and "this system has no interpretation," and the distance is invisible from the inside. Both feel like the same conclusion, because a long unsuccessful search produces the same confidence a proof would, without any of the entitlement. The discipline is to state which of the two you actually hold, in the sentence where you make the criticism. A search that has failed licenses "unjustified so far, and here is the specific thing that is missing" — a claim that is useful, actionable, and survives whatever happens next. It does not license "meaningless," which is a claim about all possible constructions and can be destroyed by a single one.

The failure mode has a characteristic shape: confidence in the conclusion gets expressed as contempt for the object, and the contempt then hardens into a research position. Once a formalism has been declared void of content, the possibility that it has content stops being a thing to look for, and the argument for the alternative starts drawing its force from the dismissal rather than from its own merits. The cost lands on the critic — Scott, having written at length against the type-free calculus for lacking any semantical justification, found a semantics for it himself weeks later, and reports the experience as both gratifying and embarrassing. The mathematics was not damaged. The rhetoric was, and it was the rhetoric that had been load-bearing for the program's framing.

What makes this a general rule rather than an anecdote is that the asymmetry is permanent. Negative existence claims about constructions require an argument of a specific kind — a counting argument, a diagonalization, an incompatibility of properties — and if you do not have one of those, you do not have the claim, no matter how many days of failed searching stand behind it. So the useful form of an objection is always the narrow one: name the property the thing lacks, say what would supply it, and leave the door open. Written that way, an objection stays correct in the case where someone walks through the door, and it tells them what to build.

**Source:** [A Type-Theoretical Alternative to ISWIM, CUCH, OWHY](../works/a-type-theoretical-alternative-to-iswim-cuch-owhy.md) — the 1993 preface, which recounts finding lattice-theoretic models for the type-free calculus a month after writing the paper's attack on it, together with the 1969 introduction's admission of having spent many days looking for such an interpretation before concluding the system's purposes could be met another way.
