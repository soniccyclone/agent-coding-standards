# A gap can persist because nobody holds both vocabularies, not because a piece is missing

**Lesson:** Scott reports that after a long stretch of experimentation forced him into the model this paper is built on, he realized it was essentially an idea another researcher had circulated a few years earlier, and — more surprising to him — already present in precise form in work published two decades before that. His diagnosis assigns no blame and is worth quoting in structure if not in words: the researcher with the construction had not connected it to the theory of computation, and the people who had the relevant operators, and knew perfectly well that a great deal could be done with them, had not seen the connection to the calculus. He adds elsewhere that the one definition nobody had written down could have been supplied immediately by that second group if anyone had ever thought to ask.

That is a mode of stagnation worth learning to recognize, because from the inside it looks like nothing at all. No one is stuck. No technique is missing. Both halves are published and both communities are productive. The connection simply has no owner, because it lies in the space between two fields and belongs to neither, and so twenty years go by. The gap is not in what is known; it is in the set of people who hold two vocabularies at once, and it closes only when someone acquires both and notices what they have.

Two things follow for anyone choosing what to work on or how to work. When you find yourself rebuilding something from first principles, the useful question is not whether it was already known — it usually was — but in *which* vocabulary it was known, because the answer tells you where else to look and what else that community is holding that you are not. And the leverage is often not in going deeper in one area but in acquiring working command of two that are not customarily held together. The connecting result tends to be easy once someone can state it in both languages, which is precisely the reason it stayed unstated: the obstacle was never difficulty, only the fact that difficulty is what everyone was scanning for.

**Source:** [Data Types as Lattices](../works/data-types-as-lattices.md) — Appendix B's history of the model, where Scott records realizing after the fact that his construction was essentially Plotkin's earlier idea and was already implicit in the work of Myhill-Shepherdson and Friedberg-Rogers on enumeration operators, explains the twenty-year gap as Plotkin not having connected his construction to recursion theory and the recursion theorists not having seen the connection to lambda calculus despite knowing how much could be done with such operators, and notes in the section-by-section remarks that those authors could have defined abstraction if anyone had ever asked them.
