# Canonical form buys you the proof, not the artifact — and the compiled shape follows the shape you wrote

**Lesson:** There is a mechanical way to turn any description of a fixed-window condition into a machine: push the description into a canonical two-level form, one branch per accepted input pattern, and wire up one subassembly per branch. It always works, the correctness argument is a paragraph, and the machine it produces is grotesque — Kleene's own illustration expands a condition with a compact description into forty branches. He points this out immediately: the canonical form makes the theorem easy and the mechanism complicated. Then he proves the stronger, more useful statement, that a machine can be built directly from *whatever* description you happen to have, canonical or not, with structure mirroring that description's structure.

That mirroring is the second half of the idea and it is what makes description-level effort worth spending. The machine's latency comes out equal to the nesting depth of the expression it was compiled from, and its size tracks the expression's size. Two consequences follow that a designer can act on. First, work spent finding a smaller equivalent description is not cosmetic; it converts directly into a smaller mechanism, which is why the canonical form is the wrong thing to hand a builder even though it is the right thing to hand a prover. Second — and this is the trade that makes the whole thing engineering rather than logic — latency and width are exchangeable. The minimum-latency construction can demand an absurd number of connections converging on a single element, which the physical substrate will not supply; accepting a slightly deeper mechanism cuts that convergence by orders of magnitude at the cost of more elements. Kleene works the numbers on exactly this swap and, elsewhere, shows small hand-found mechanisms beating what his own general method would emit.

The honest part is what he leaves open. He does not know whether the same behaviour's shortest description can be found systematically, whether useful normal forms exist for his notation, or whether deciding that two descriptions denote the same behaviour is even possible; he flags all three as unexamined and notes that his general construction habitually yields tangled descriptions of simple things. That is the right way to hold it: the compilation is understood, the optimization is not, so plan on human judgment at the description level rather than expecting the pipeline to recover from a bad input form.

For a programmer this argues against a specific and very common conflation. The form of an artifact that makes it easy to verify, and the form that makes it efficient, are different forms, and a translation that faithfully preserves structure means the choice of source form is a performance decision. Reach for the canonical, exhaustive, one-case-per-possibility shape when you need to convince yourself or a checker that nothing was missed; reach for the compact shape when the thing has to be built, and expect to find it yourself. And when a generated implementation is too large or too slow, look first at the description it was generated from, because a structure-preserving generator can only be as good as its input.

**Source:** [Representation of Events in Nerve Nets and Finite Automata](../works/representation-of-events-in-nerve-nets-and-finite-automata.md) — the progression in Part I from the canonical-normal-form construction, through the observation that non-canonical descriptions yield far simpler mechanisms, to the general theorem tying latency to expression depth, together with the discussion of trading latency against connection count under substrate limits and the closing list of unresolved simplification and equivalence questions.
