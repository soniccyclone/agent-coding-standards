# Put a language's texts inside its own data domain and make each step a total function, and questions about programs become ordinary computation

**Lesson:** There is a move here worth stealing wholesale, independent of the theorems it was used to prove. To reason mechanically about a formalism, first refuse to look at its equations as meaning anything: treat them as finite strings over a fixed alphabet, and treat the computation rules as string rewriting. Then encode strings as values in the very domain the formalism computes over, so that manipulating a program becomes indistinguishable in kind from arithmetic on its inputs. The observation that makes this pay is that the bookkeeping of symbol manipulation — locate a subterm, replace an occurrence, substitute a numeral — has the same shape as the bounded, guaranteed-terminating recursion the formalism already contains. Once you notice that, the metalanguage does not need to be more powerful than the object language; it needs to be the object language pointed at its own texts.

The second half of the move is subtler and is where most people building interpreters get it wrong. The natural statement of a rewriting rule is conditional and nondeterministic: *if* the term contains a matching occurrence, you *may* replace it. Rules of that shape make the derivation process a relation, and relations do not compose into functions you can index and iterate. So restate every rule as a total operation defined on all inputs, taking a positional argument that says which occurrence to act on, and doing nothing at all when the position names no match. Uniform, single-valued, always defined. That one reformulation converts an open-ended proof search into the mechanical closure of a seed under a fixed family of total operations, which can be enumerated by an outright bounded computation indexed by a step counter. All the difficulty that was diffused across "does some rule apply somewhere" collapses into one place: searching for the step index at which the answer appears.

A programmer who thinks this way designs evaluators, rewriters, type checkers, and proof searches with a total step function at the core: `step(state, choice) -> state`, never failing, never branching outward, with invalid choices mapping to a harmless identity. Everything above it — enumeration, fairness, breadth-first exploration, resource bounds — becomes ordinary control over an index, not a special engine. It also explains why homogeneous representation matters more than convenient representation: the moment programs live in a different universe than data, the metalevel needs its own machinery, its own correctness argument, and its own bugs. And it is the same insight that makes reflection, quotation, self-application, and universal interpreters possible at all rather than being tricks: they are consequences of syntax being representable in the value domain, plus a step relation regularized into a function.

**Source:** [General Recursive Functions of Natural Numbers](../works/general-recursive-functions-of-natural-numbers.md) — the §1 setup that treats defining equations as symbol sequences and notes the kinship between symbol operations and bounded numeric recursion, followed by the replacement of the conditional derivation rules with an indexed family of everywhere-defined single-valued operations, which is what lets the derivable consequences of a system be enumerated by a bounded-construction function.
