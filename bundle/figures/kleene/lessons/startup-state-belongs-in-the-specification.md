# A component correct in steady state can be wrong before it has observed anything, and which specifications are startup-safe is decidable in advance

**Lesson:** A construction can be provably right about every history long enough to contain the thing it is watching for, and still fire spuriously in its first moments of operation, because a freshly initialized machine cannot tell "I have observed a stretch of nothing" from "I have not yet observed anything." Kleene makes the point with a deliberately silly story about robots built to abandon ship after a long quiet stretch followed by a particular sighting; the robots are switched on at the commissioning ceremony and immediately go over the side, having mistaken their own newness for the required quiet. The construction was correct. The specification was satisfiable by a blank past, and blankness is exactly what startup looks like from inside.

What makes this more than an anecdote is that he does not patch it. He asks precisely when the problem can occur, and gets a clean structural criterion: the trouble arises exactly for those requirements that can be met by a history in which nothing at all happens at the moment the watched interval begins. Requirements that demand some activity at their first moment are immune, and can be implemented starting from a completely blank machine with no risk of a false trigger; requirements that do not are provably not implementable that way, and need either an artificially pre-set element, a suppression circuit that inhibits output for the first few moments, or an external always-on signal that supplies a real origin for time. He then observes which of these are plausible in the substrate he cares about and which are engineering hacks he is including only so the theory has no exceptions. Necessary *and* sufficient — not a heuristic, a characterization.

The way of thinking to take from this is that a component's pre-observation condition is part of its specification rather than a detail of its construction, and that the honest question is not "does my initialization work?" but "is this requirement of a kind that any blank-start implementation can satisfy?" That question has an answer determined by the requirement's own shape, available before any implementation exists. Notice also the reframing that dissolves the whole difficulty: if you introduce a signal that is always present in the environment and rewrite every requirement to reference it, all requirements become startup-safe by construction. Given a defect class, characterize it, then find the framing in which the class is empty.

The transfer to ordinary systems is direct and the failure is common. Absence-based conditions — no heartbeat for thirty seconds, no writes since the last checkpoint, no errors during the window — are all satisfiable by an empty record, so a monitor, a cache, or a watchdog that has just come up will assert them on evidence it does not have. The habit worth building is to treat every "hasn't happened" predicate as suspect at startup, and to look for the structural fix: require a positive observation to anchor the interval, or carry an explicit "measurement began here" marker, rather than sprinkling warmup delays wherever a false alarm has already been noticed in production.

**Source:** [Representation of Events in Nerve Nets and Finite Automata](../works/representation-of-events-in-nerve-nets-and-finite-automata.md) — the reconsideration of fixed-window descriptions once time is taken to have a first moment, which introduces the robot cautionary tale, derives the exact necessary and sufficient condition for spurious-trigger-free operation from a blank initial state, and surveys the repair devices along with the always-present-input reframing that makes every requirement conform.
