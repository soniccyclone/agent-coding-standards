# A bounded machine cannot store its history, only a fixed finite classification of it — so design the digest, do not scale the record

**Lesson:** Count the configurations a machine with finitely many finitely-valued parts can occupy, and you have counted everything it will ever be able to distinguish about its own past. Every possible history it might have lived through must be filed into one of those finitely many buckets, and the number of buckets is fixed when you build the thing, before any input arrives. This single observation is the load-bearing idea of the whole analysis, and it cuts both ways at once: it tells you what such a machine can be made to notice, and it tells you what it can never notice, from the same arithmetic. What the machine "knows" is not a recording of what happened; it is a partition of all the ways things could have happened, and the design question is which partition.

Take the alternative seriously enough and it collapses. The naive way to answer "did this pattern occur?" is to keep the relevant stretch of history explicitly, as a fixed window of delay elements. Kleene actually does the budget for that approach applied to a lifetime: to let something observed early influence behaviour much later, the delay chain must be as long as the elapsed interval measured in switching times, and a fresh structural addition is needed for every distinct delay you want to support, duplicated for every kind of observation that matters. The numbers come out well past the hardware available, and there is a second, worse defect — a mechanism sized for a fixed span suffers a hard amnesia horizon the moment it outlives its design span. Windowed recording does not merely cost too much; it is the wrong shape for the problem. Behaviour that must persist indefinitely has to be carried by state that summarizes, and the summary must be chosen so it retains exactly what future decisions depend on and discards the rest.

The same counting argument marks the boundary from the other side, and here Kleene's example is unusually instructive because of what it is *not*. He exhibits a pattern no bounded machine can track — firing precisely at the perfect squares — and the pattern is perfectly computable, indeed sits low in the hierarchy of computable predicates. Nothing infinite or paradoxical is involved. What defeats the machine is only that the process it would have to complete, while finite for each moment, grows without bound as time advances, so no fixed supply of distinguishable states can keep up. The lesson is that a mechanism has a resource boundary distinct from, and much lower than, the boundary of what is computable at all, and mistaking one for the other leads you to look for cleverness where the obstruction is arithmetic.

For anyone building components with fixed memory — a stream processor, a protocol endpoint, an embedded controller, a database trigger — this converts a vague worry into a concrete design step. Ask what decisions this component will have to make, derive from that the coarsest classification of history sufficient to make them, and make *that* the state. If the required classification turns out to grow with runtime, you have learned something decisive before writing code: no bounded implementation exists, and the honest move is to change the requirement or to introduce external storage rather than to enlarge the state and hope.
