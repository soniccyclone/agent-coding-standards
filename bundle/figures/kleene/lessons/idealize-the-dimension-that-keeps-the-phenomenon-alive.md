# Choose which dimension to idealize as unbounded by asking which choice keeps the phenomenon you are studying alive

**Lesson:** Every model of a running system has to decide what to treat as unbounded, and the decision is usually made for convenience. Kleene makes it by consequence instead, and the reasoning is worth copying because both of the obvious choices turn out to destroy the object of study. If the past is taken as infinite — the tidy option, since it removes any special first moment — then a machine's current configuration is no longer determined by what it has observed, and one can prove that nothing except fixed-window conditions is faithfully recognizable at all: the very capability under investigation, remembering something indefinitely far back, is legislated out of existence. He notes explicitly that he had been tempted by that fiction to simplify the theory, and that the theorem is what forbade it. If instead the future is taken as bounded, matching the finite lifetime of any real device, then the questions become uninteresting for the opposite reason — everything reduces to a fixed window and the whole subject of unbounded-duration memory dissolves.

So he takes the asymmetric option that neither of the two natural ones offers: history begins at a definite first moment, and operation continues without end. Both halves are doing work. A finite origin is what makes the current configuration a well-defined function of everything observed, which is what makes memory analyzable in the first place. An unbounded future is what makes it a real problem that the mechanism is fixed and finite, and it is precisely that tension — fixed apparatus, unlimited running time — that stands in for the practical situation of a modest mechanism supporting complicated behaviour over a long life. The idealization is not chosen because it is more realistic than the alternatives; it is chosen because it is the one under which the interesting question is neither trivial nor impossible.

He is also candid about the price, and the candour is part of the lesson. Committing to a precise model means some of its features will not correspond to anything in the reality it abstracts, and he says so, flagging places where his framework attaches decisive weight to single instants in a way the real substrate surely does not, and admitting that certain repair mechanisms he needs for theoretical completeness are not plausible as physical structures. Having chosen the idealization on purpose, he can say which of its artifacts to disregard — a thing you cannot do when the idealization was inherited unexamined.

The transferable habit is to treat "what is infinite in my model?" as a design question with a testable criterion, rather than a default. Before adopting a simplification — unbounded queues, reliable delivery, uniform latency, clocks with no origin, arbitrary retry — ask what becomes provable and what becomes unstatable under it. If the simplification makes the failure mode you are actually studying disappear, it is not a simplification but a change of subject, and the fact that it makes the analysis easier is exactly the warning sign. The productive idealization is often lopsided, bounding one dimension tightly to keep the analysis well-founded while letting another run free to keep the difficulty real.

**Source:** [Representation of Events in Nerve Nets and Finite Automata](../works/representation-of-events-in-nerve-nets-and-finite-automata.md) — the preliminary examples in Part I showing that an infinite past leaves indefinite conditions unrepresentable (proved in the first appendix), the following argument that a bounded lifetime makes the problem uninteresting, and the resulting decision to model a finite past with an unbounded future, along with the accompanying admissions about which features of the model do not correspond to reality.
