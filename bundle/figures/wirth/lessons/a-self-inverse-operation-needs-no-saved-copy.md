# A self-inverse operation needs no saved copy and no precondition

**Lesson:** Two independent parties writing into one shared medium is the classic setup for a locking protocol: before either may write, it must establish that the other is not present, and afterwards it must restore whatever it displaced. The protocol is where the bugs live, because it must be observed at every site and the restoration must survive every abnormal exit. But the protocol is only forced when the writing is destructive — when performing it loses information that has to have been saved somewhere. Choose an operation that combines with what is already there rather than replacing it, and specifically one that is its own inverse, and the entire obligation evaporates. Applying it twice returns the medium to its prior state, so nothing needs to be saved, nothing needs to be restored, and no precondition needs to hold before the first application. The transient overlay can be laid down anywhere, at any time, and lifted by repeating the same action.

That "same action" is worth dwelling on, because it collapses a pair into a single primitive. Where a destructive scheme needs a draw and a separate erase — two operations whose correspondence has to be maintained by hand as the drawing evolves — an involution needs one, and the erase is the draw. A whole class of divergence between paint and unpaint simply cannot occur. It also means an interface can offer both names bound to the same implementation where the hardware has no better support, and bind them to different implementations where it does, without any client noticing: the abstraction is "apply" and "unapply", and whether they are the same procedure underneath is a matter for whoever knows the device.

Locking does not disappear entirely; it is pushed to where it is genuinely needed. When one party wants to write destructively — because it is producing durable content rather than a transient overlay — it must first ask the transient party to lift its overlay from that region, and that request is the whole of the protocol. One operation, invoked by the party that is about to be destructive, on the region it is about to touch. Compare that with a symmetric locking discipline observed by everyone at every write, and the asymmetry is the point: making one of the two parties non-destructive turns a mutual protocol into a one-sided courtesy.

A last piece falls out of the same design. If the transient party keeps a record of where and whether its overlay is currently applied, then its operations can be stated in terms of the desired end state — be shown here, be hidden — rather than in terms of a change from a state the caller would have to know. Callers stop tracking what is currently displayed, which is exactly the state they are least equipped to track, because the overlay moves for reasons that have nothing to do with them.
