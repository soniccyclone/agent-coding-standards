# Edit the description, not the contents

**Lesson:** When a large body of data must support insertion and deletion at arbitrary points, the obvious representation holds the data and moves it. Every edit then costs in proportion to how much data lies past the edit point, loading costs in proportion to the whole, and the stored form has to be rewritten because it is the thing being edited. The alternative is to introduce one level of indirection and never touch the data at all: represent the body as an ordered sequence of descriptors, each naming a region of some underlying store, and define every edit as an operation on that sequence. Deleting a range becomes splitting two descriptors and unlinking what lies between; inserting becomes splitting one descriptor and splicing in others. The underlying regions are never moved, never copied, and never invalidated.

Three properties fall out together, which is what makes the trade lopsided. Edit cost becomes proportional to the number of descriptors touched rather than to the amount of data affected, so it no longer depends on size. Loading becomes trivial, because an unedited body is described by exactly one descriptor naming the whole thing, and the expensive work is deferred until an edit actually happens. And the stored form is untouched by editing, which means the persistent representation is stable for free rather than by careful arrangement — no small thing, since the stored form is the part that other tools and other eras have to agree with, and it is the part you least want coupled to your editing model.

The method by which this is arrived at is as transferable as the result. Write the properties you want from the representation as an explicit numbered list before choosing one — leanness, closure under the operations, cost of each operation, cost of loading, stability of the stored form — then take a candidate and check off which items it satisfies. You will typically find one that satisfies all but one, and the value of the list is that it makes that one failure precise instead of vague. Then resist the temptation to fix the failure with a better structure. Look first at the access pattern: if the operation that is expensive in general is, in practice, performed once at an arbitrary point and then many times nearby, a single remembered result of the last lookup converts nearly all of them into near-misses, and costs one field. Reserve the more elaborate structure for the case where the pattern does not have that shape, and say out loud that you are accepting a bad worst case in exchange, along with why you believe that case does not arise.

One further move is worth naming, because it recurs. To generalize the scheme to data carrying attributes, the extension was not to add machinery but to add a constraint: require every descriptor to lie within a single region of uniform attributes. A generalization achieved by tightening an invariant rather than by enlarging a structure costs nothing at run time, and the original analysis carries over unchanged — which is exactly what you want to be able to say about a generalization.

**Source:** [Project Oberon](../works/project-oberon.md) — section 5.2's explicit seven-item list of requirements for the internal representation of text (lean data structure, closure under editing, efficient editing, efficient sequential reading, efficient direct positioning, very efficient internalizing, preserved file representations); its adoption of the piece chain, in which a body of text is a sequence of descriptors each naming a file, a starting position and a length, and the editing operations operate on the chain of descriptors rather than on the pieces themselves, with delete and insert expressed as splitting at the boundaries and unlinking or splicing; the section's item-by-item check showing which requirements this meets, including that internalizing is cheap because the list initially consists of a single piece spanning the entire file and that file representations are preserved simply because the operations do not affect them; the generalization to attributed text by requiring the piece chain to subordinate itself to the run structure so that every piece lies within a single run, after which the same ratings still hold; and the resolution of the remaining direct-positioning requirement, where a piece tree and a chain with long-distance links were investigated and a one-entry cache of the most recently computed position-to-piece mapping was chosen instead, justified by the typical scenario of positioning to a remote location once and then to its neighbourhood many times, with the acknowledgement that cache misses remain unsolved but are acute only for extremely long chains that do not occur in ordinary texts and editing sessions.
