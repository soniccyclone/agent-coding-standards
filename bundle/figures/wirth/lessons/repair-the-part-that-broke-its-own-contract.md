# Repair the part that broke its own contract; weakening the requirement instead has to be priced out loud

**Lesson:** When a program is found to be wrong, there are two structurally different responses and they are not equally safe. The first locates the component whose stated obligation was not met and fixes that component, leaving everything downstream to keep depending on what it was promised. The second leaves the faulty component alone and changes the parts that depended on it so that they no longer need the guarantee. The first is the safer, cleaner, more honest route and should be the default, because it gives a degree of immunity from the further consequences of the same oversight — anything else that quietly relied on the same promise is fixed at the same time, without anyone having to enumerate what those things were. The second response leaves the broken promise broken and repays you later, in the form of the next dependency nobody remembered.

That said, the second response is legitimate, and knowing when is a real skill rather than a compromise. It is legitimate when repairing the faulty component turns out to require far-reaching modification while accommodating it is a small local change, and — this is the part that must not be skipped — when you can state what the weakened guarantee costs and show the cost is acceptable. Concretely: does the worst case get worse, and how likely is the bad case in practice? If the worst case is unchanged and the unfavourable distribution is statistically very unlikely, then the efficiency argument against the accommodation collapses and the cheaper fix is defensible. Do that arithmetic explicitly, and record which requirement you have abandoned, because the next person will otherwise read the surviving code as evidence that the requirement still holds. A weakened invariant that is documented is a design decision; the same invariant weakened silently is a trap.

The diagnostic underneath both responses is worth more than either. The fault in such cases is almost never a mistake in the difficult part; it is an unnoticed consequence of an operation everyone regarded as simple. Distributing items alternately between two destinations obviously preserves their number — except that two consecutive groups arriving at the same destination may coalesce into one, so the count of groups can fall, and every later step that assumed equal counts is now wrong. So when hunting a fault, suspect the operations nobody thought worth specifying, and get in the habit of writing down what a simple operation actually guarantees rather than what it was intended to accomplish. The two differ most exactly where the operation is too obvious to examine. And notice the design corollary: an algorithm whose correctness does not depend on a quantity is immune to being wrong about that quantity, so where you can arrange such independence, the whole class of oversight disappears rather than being fixed.

**Source:** [Algorithms and Data Structures](../works/algorithms-and-data-structures.md) — section 2.4.2, where the natural merge program is found to sort incorrectly, the mistake is attributed to an oversight of the consequences of a presumably simple operation (two runs distributed consecutively to the same file merging into one, so the number of output runs can be less than the number of input runs), the two available responses are contrasted with the repair of the faulty distribution named as the safer, cleaner, more honest and generally recommended way, and the alternative fix in the merge procedure is nonetheless adopted after arguing that the worst case is unchanged and highly unequal distribution is statistically very unlikely; together with section 2.4.3's remark that devising the algorithm so its correctness does not depend on the number of runs lets the side effect be safely ignored, and section 2.4.4's contrasting case where exact run counts are needed and the effect therefore cannot be overlooked.
