# Either the parties agree on a rate or they acknowledge each unit

**Lesson:** Any transfer between two parties that do not share a state must resolve one question: how does the receiver know when the next unit is there? There are exactly two answers, and seeing them as a pair rather than as unrelated techniques makes a family of design decisions straightforward. Either both sides agree in advance on a rate and the receiver samples on schedule, or each unit is explicitly announced and explicitly acknowledged so that no schedule is needed at all. The second costs a round trip per unit and buys complete independence from speed: neither side needs to know how fast the other is, either may pause indefinitely, and the transfer is correct at any rate whatsoever. The first costs nothing per unit and buys throughput, at the price of a shared assumption that must actually hold.

The interesting part is what governs the *size* of the unit, because that is where the trade shows up as a real constraint rather than a preference. In the agreed-rate scheme the two sides' notions of the rate drift apart over the length of a unit, so accuracy limits how long a unit may be: a longer unit amortizes the framing overhead better but demands proportionally tighter agreement. That gives a genuine ceiling, and it also gives the escape from it — carry the rate information within the data itself, so that agreement is continuously re-established and the length limit disappears. Whether that escape is taken is a property of the medium's encoding, not of the software, and is exactly the sort of thing to look up rather than assume. And note that the two schemes are less opposed than the vocabulary suggests: the announce-and-acknowledge scheme is usually agreed-rate *within* a unit and independent between units, so the real variable is where the boundary between the two regimes is drawn.

The consequence that reaches the software is the one to plan around. Under an agreed rate, the deadline for producing the next unit is hard and is set by the medium, so producing data and sending it cannot be interleaved — the whole unit must be assembled before the transfer begins, which turns what would have been a stream into a batch and turns the unit size into a memory requirement. Under acknowledgement there is no deadline at all, and the producer may take as long as it likes between units. So the choice of coordination scheme is not merely a performance parameter: it determines whether the code above it can be written as a pipeline or must be written as a buffer-then-send, and discovering that after the fact means restructuring rather than tuning.
