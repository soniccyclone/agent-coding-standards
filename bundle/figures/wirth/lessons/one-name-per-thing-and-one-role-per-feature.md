# One name per thing, one role per feature: mixed levels and merged roles destroy the same guarantee

**Lesson:** Most of what an abstraction gives you is a promise about what cannot happen, and the value of the promise is proportional to how much reasoning it lets you skip. The central one, so basic that it is easy to stop noticing, is that the state can be carved into disjoint named pieces: an assignment changes the piece named on the left and nothing else. Without it you must in principle treat the entire store as the state of the computation at every step, which is the actual reason machine-level programming does not scale. But the promise holds only under a precondition — no piece of storage may be reachable under more than one name — and that precondition is fragile in a way the promise itself is not. It can be broken by an explicit aliasing declaration, or lost without anyone intending it through a general enough parameter-passing rule combined with a general enough scope rule, or handed away deliberately by admitting addresses as ordinary computable values. Once any of those is present, the guarantee is gone globally, not locally: every assignment everywhere is now suspect, even though only a few pieces of code exercise the capability.

The diagnostic worth carrying away is that this kind of failure usually traces to one feature being asked to play two roles. A procedure used as textual abbreviation and a procedure used as a unit of decomposition want opposite things — the abbreviation wants free access to the surrounding context and would happily forgo parameters, the decomposition unit wants all its communication confined to explicit parameters. Project both onto one construct and its rules must be general enough to cover the union, so it loses the discipline that either role alone would have had. This is the general shape: a merged feature is not the intersection of two guarantees but the union of two permissions. When a single mechanism in a design is serving two purposes that would impose different restrictions, the restrictions have already been dropped, and that is where to look when a promise turns out not to hold.

The same analysis applies across levels rather than across roles, which is why the two belong together. Choose the basic abstractions from one level and make sure they are mutually compatible, because a structured construct sitting next to a machine-level escape does not merely coexist with it — the escape can break the structured construct's promise from outside. A counted repetition announces to every reader that its body runs once per value in the sequence, and a jump out of the body silently falsifies that announcement; a jump back in is worse, since it raises problems that force protective rules bolted on afterwards, and rules of that origin complicate the definition, the reader's understanding, and the compiler all at once. The plainest instance is what happens when type distinctions are removed to close the gap between high-level code and assembly: the resulting programs can only be understood by knowing how data is represented in bits and words, so the programmer has been evicted from the abstraction the language was still claiming to offer. A design that offers an abstraction and an unrestricted way around it has offered nothing, and the correct response to a genuine need at the lower level is to serve it without lowering the level of the whole.
