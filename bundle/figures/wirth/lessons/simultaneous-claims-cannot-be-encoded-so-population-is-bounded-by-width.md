# Simultaneous claims cannot be encoded, so the population is bounded by the width

**Lesson:** When several parties may want a shared thing at the same instant and there is no coordinator, the resolution has to happen in a medium where all of them can speak at once and each can still be distinguished. That requirement collapses the design space in a way worth understanding, because it explains an efficiency that looks like waste. A compact encoding is exactly the wrong representation for a simultaneous claim: if two parties each place their identifier on a shared medium at the same moment, what appears is the combination of the two identifiers, which is generally a third valid identifier and tells you nothing about who is present. Encodings are injective on single values and destroyed by superposition. Give each party its own line instead — a representation that is enormously redundant for one speaker and is the only one that survives many — and the combination of several claims is exactly the set of claims, readable by everyone.

Three properties then fall out at once and they are why this arrangement is worth its cost. Nobody has to be in charge, so there is no coordinator to build, to trust, or to lose. Priority comes for free, because the lines are ordered and each contender can see whether a higher-ranked line is asserted and withdraw; no separate priority mechanism, no negotiation. And resolution takes a fixed, known delay rather than a number of rounds proportional to the number of contenders — everyone claims, everyone looks, the losers drop out, once. What you pay is that the number of participants equals the number of lines rather than the number of values those lines could encode, which is the difference between a small population and an enormous one, and it is a hard ceiling rather than a soft cost.

The general principle transfers past hardware buses: any contention scheme in which participants must announce themselves *at the same time* is priced in one unit of shared resource per participant, while any scheme in which participants take turns can use a compact identifier and support far more of them. So the question to settle first is not how to arbitrate but whether the announcements are concurrent or serialized. If concurrent, budget for a per-participant slot and cap the population deliberately. If the population must be large, you have committed to serialization somewhere — a coordinator, a token, a round structure — and the fixed-delay and no-coordinator properties above are what you are giving up. Choosing without noticing this is how a design ends up with an arbitrary participant limit nobody can explain.

**Source:** [Project Oberon](../works/project-oberon.md) — section 9.4's account of the SCSI arbitration phase, introduced by the danger that in a system with several potential initiators two may enter the selection phase at the same instant with unpredictable consequences; the description of arbitration as distributed, requiring no central agency to grant bus access among concurrent competitors, in which a contender asserts the busy signal together with the data line corresponding to its own address, waits an arbitration delay, and proceeds to selection only if no higher-numbered data line has been asserted, otherwise withdrawing in favour of the higher-ranking competitor; and the section's own observation that this is the reason only eight rather than two hundred and fifty-six devices can be connected, since each data line must be associated with exactly one device.
