# Generality can strip out the redundancy you were reasoning with

**Lesson:** There is a seductive program for achieving simplicity: find the fundamental concepts hiding inside the many surface features, extract each from its several incarnations, and present it once in pure form free of arbitrary restrictions on where it may be used. It works, in the sense that it delivers what it promises. Unify the notion of a callable body with the notion of a parameter so that both are just values assignable to variables, and the definition gets shorter while the power increases sharply — you can now swap behavior at runtime, or replace a computed function by a constant, recovering the freedom that previously belonged only to someone modifying their own machine instructions. The trap is what disappears along with the restrictions. Rules constraining which texts are meaningful are also the rules that make a wrong text detectable, and a design with none of them yields programs in which a flaw in the logic is nearly impossible to locate. Maximum generality at the top of a system produces something with the character of a bare universal machine dressed in nicer syntax, which is precisely the thing the high level was supposed to improve upon.

The correction is a different objective, not a retreat to fewer features. Terseness is the wrong target: the value of working above the machine is not that programs come out as short as possible, nor that words and mathematical symbols replace numeric codes, but that the notation supplies abstractions fitted to how people actually hold a computation in mind — and the useful measure of a language's level is how far those abstractions sit from what the machine literally does. Redundancy is therefore not waste to be minimized. It is the surface against which mistakes become visible, and a language incapable of helping you detect an error certainly cannot help you avoid one. What must be minimized instead is the wrong kind of redundancy, the prolix sort that lengthens a text without adding any independently checkable statement of intent.

So the count of primitives is the wrong figure of merit, and two better ones replace it. First, is each included facility simple to understand in all the consequences of its use — not simple to describe, which is a much weaker property. Second, is it free of unexpected interactions when combined with the others, since consequences at the intersections are where the real complexity of a system lives. A design meeting both can afford more constructs than a maximally distilled one and still be easier to think in. This also reframes what syntactic structure is for: it should not be a burden the design carries but a support it rests on, meaning every rule should have a purpose, ambiguities should be prohibited by construction, and a keyword chosen for a concept should be reserved to it so its appearance is unambiguous evidence. Rules that exist to catch you are working for you.

**Source:** [On the Design of Programming Languages](../works/on-the-design-of-programming-languages.md) — the "Simplicity in Generality" section: the generalized-Algol program of extracting fundamental concepts free of restrictive applicability rules, the unification of procedure and parameter into assignable objects with the resulting gain in flexibility, the Euler experience of that premise producing a language where a flaw in a program's logic was almost impossible to detect and the high-level-Turing-machine characterization, and the closing argument that the aim is a framework of abstractions adapted to human capabilities and limitations, that facilities must be free of unexpected interactions when combined, and that syntax must support rather than burden while prolixity is the wrong kind of redundancy.
