# Buy mechanization with a restriction on access, and expect it to redraw the algorithms

**Lesson:** A structure whose size varies during execution normally drags a hard problem in with it: storage has to be acquired as it grows and ideally reclaimed as it shrinks, which is why such structures are harder to provide than the fixed-size ones. There is a way out that is worth generalizing far beyond storage, and it consists of accepting a restriction on how the structure may be used in exchange for being able to mechanize its management completely. Insist that the structure is only ever read by advancing from one element to its immediate successor and only ever written by appending at the end, and the allocation pattern becomes so simple that it can be handled by a facility built once for all users rather than reasoned about by every programmer who touches it. The same restriction is what makes it possible to move data in large blocks between storage of different speeds, because a strictly forward pattern of use is exactly the pattern a mechanically moving device can serve efficiently. Notice the shape of the bargain: you give up the ability to name an arbitrary element, and in return an entire class of decisions leaves your program.

The restriction has to be enforced rather than recommended, and the enforcement mechanism is the interesting part. Supply the structure only through a set of operations that cannot express anything but the permitted pattern, so that the forbidden access is not merely discouraged but unsayable. It follows that the notation used to reason about the structure informally — referring to its i-th element while proving something — may be exactly what the notation available inside a program must refuse. That gap is not a defect; the informal notation serves the reader and the restricted operation set serves the guarantee, and they are allowed to differ.

The consequence people underestimate is that the restriction propagates outward and changes which algorithms exist at all. The same task — putting a collection in order — splits into two nearly disjoint bodies of technique depending on whether every element is individually reachable or only one is visible at a time, and the second body of technique is not a degraded version of the first but a different subject, built on merging rather than on exchanging or inserting, with different cost measures, because the operation that dominates is transferring elements rather than comparing them. So when you impose an access discipline, do not expect the existing algorithms to survive it in reduced form; expect to have to find new ones, and expect the effort of finding them to be repaid by the fact that the resulting programs work at a scale the unrestricted ones could not reach. The reverse also holds and is the more common error: adopting a general-access structure by default forfeits the mechanization you could have had, and the loss shows up much later as a storage-management problem you now own.
