# Prefer the base structure whose shape needs no separate encoding

**Lesson:** A facility that must serve every future client cannot be designed by surveying current clients, because the structures it will be asked to hold have not been invented yet. The only workable criterion is closure under mapping: pick a base structure simple enough that any structure anyone later devises can be projected onto it, and recovered from it, straightforwardly and without loss. That criterion is much weaker than "expressive" and it is what makes the design possible at all. It also inverts the usual instinct that a richer base serves more clients — a richer base commits to distinctions that some future client will find wrong, while a base with almost no structure commits to nothing and forces each client to say what its own shape is, which is precisely the client's job.

The second criterion is the one usually missed, and it decides between candidates that both pass the first. Prefer the structure whose organization is *implicit in the arrangement of the data itself* rather than carried in accompanying descriptors. When order alone determines the shape, nothing about the shape has to be encoded, transmitted, kept in step, or validated on receipt — moving the data anywhere moves the structure with it for free. That property is what makes such a structure survivable across a boundary with hard timing constraints, because the data can be cut at any point into chunks, and a chunk transferred on its own is meaningful without anything else accompanying it. Chunking is what lets the party producing or consuming the data be decoupled from the party meeting the medium's timing requirements, with a buffer between them, and the chunk size becomes a free parameter tuned per medium rather than a property of the abstraction. A structure whose shape lives in separate descriptors admits none of this: the descriptors and the data must be kept together, cutting has to respect their boundaries, and the receiver cannot interpret a fragment.

The cost of the choice should be stated rather than glossed. A structure of this kind is less flexible than a general dynamic one — it can grow but it cannot be reshaped, and inserting into its middle is not among its operations. Naming it honestly, as something between the static and the dynamic, is better than pretending it is fully general, because clients need to know that anything they want in the middle is their problem to arrange above the base. And note where the criterion comes from: this shape is not chosen on aesthetic grounds but because it is the natural abstraction of media that physically move past a head, so the abstraction and the mechanism agree, which is the ordinary reason a foundation stays cheap.

**Source:** [Project Oberon](../works/project-oberon.md) — section 7.1's opening argument that a storage facility cannot accommodate all data types and structures that will be programmed in future and must therefore provide a simple but sufficiently flexible base structure onto which any structure can be mapped and from which it can be recovered straightforwardly and efficiently; the identification of that base structure as a sequence of bytes, with the consequence that anything stored must first be sequentialized; the observation that the sequence is the natural abstraction of physically moving storage media, and that its structural information is inherent and need not be encoded and transmitted alongside the data; the argument that this implicitness is what allows a sequence to be divided into buffered subsequences so the process generating or consuming data is decoupled from the transmission process bound by the medium's timing constraints, with the subsequence size depending on the medium; and the characterization of the sequence as semi-dynamic, able to change its length but not its form since elements can only be appended.
