# Two growing consumers of one resource need no boundary; three do

**Lesson:** When several consumers draw from one finite resource and each can grow, the design question is whether any of them needs a limit of its own. With exactly two, the answer is no, and the reason is worth holding onto because it is a genuine structural fact rather than a trick: place them at opposite ends and let them grow toward each other, and the only constraint that ever binds is the sum, which is the real constraint anyway. Nobody has to guess how much each will need, no configuration parameter has to be chosen, no workload has to be predicted, and a program that uses much of one and little of the other simply works. With three, that arrangement is unavailable — a resource has two ends — and at least one boundary must be fixed at a value chosen without knowing the workload. From then on the system has a limit that is an artifact of its own layout rather than of its capacity, and someone will hit it.

The consequence is that the *count* of independently growing regions is a first-class design variable, and it is worth real effort to keep it at two. Before accepting a third region, ask whether it can be merged into one of the others, or whether it can be made to grow in a way that does not compete — allocated in fixed units from a pool that the others do not draw from, say, or bounded by something already bounded. Whenever you see an arbitrary size limit in a system, this is often where it came from: not from anyone deciding that the limit was right, but from a third region appearing and a number having to be written down.

Where a third region cannot be avoided, the useful move is to notice that the consumers may differ in what they need from the resource. Some require every address they use to be genuinely backed by something physical; others need only an address, and can tolerate large unused gaps. Assign the sparse-tolerant consumer the slack between the addressable space and the physically present space, and the arbitrary limit lands on the region that can afford it — the two demanding consumers are then bounded together by physical capacity, as in the two-region case, and the artificial partition only constrains something that was never scarce. That reasoning generalizes: when a partition is unavoidable, place it where the parties differ in what they actually consume, not where the diagram looks symmetric.

**Source:** [Project Oberon](../works/project-oberon.md) — section 8.1's division of main storage into a block space for module code and static data, a stack for procedure activation records, and a heap for anonymous dynamically allocated variables, followed by the observation that the number of distinct spaces is unfortunately larger than two: that with two, no arbitrary size limitation would be necessary since only the sum of their sizes would be inherently limited by the store, while with three, arbitrarily determined limits are unavoidable; the note that address-mapping hardware alleviates but does not remove the problem by making the virtual space large enough that limits are rarely reached; the argument that stack and heap must be genuinely allocated so that every address within them denotes a physically present location, so there is no point in making their combined virtual space exceed the physical store, with the block space then receiving the difference between the virtual and physical address spaces; and the contrasting layout adopted for the machine lacking a memory management unit, where fixed limits including a stack capped at a specific size were unavoidable.
