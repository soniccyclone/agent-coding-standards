# When a repair forces an expensive access, take everything it offers — and check the opposite repair is its mirror

**Lesson:** Maintaining an occupancy invariant means that some operations push a unit outside its permitted range and have to fix it by reaching to a neighbour. Reaching to a neighbour is the expensive part — it is an access you would not otherwise have made — and once that cost is sunk, the question of how much to take from the neighbour is nearly free to answer generously. Taking the single item that restores legality leaves the unit sitting exactly at the boundary, so the next operation of the same kind pays the same expensive access again. Redistributing evenly between the two costs the same access and leaves both units in the middle of their range, buying a long run of subsequent operations that need no neighbour at all. The general principle is to let the granularity of a repair be set by the cost of initiating it rather than by the size of the violation: when the fixed cost dominates, do the maximum useful work per repair, not the minimum sufficient work.

There is a second, sharper discipline hiding in the same design. Every invariant that can be violated in one direction can usually be violated in the other, and the two repairs should be exact inverses of each other — one splits a full unit and promotes a separator upward, the other absorbs a separator downward and merges two sparse units. Writing them as inverses is not merely elegant; it is the cheapest available check that you have got them right, because any operation the first can produce must be undoable by the second, and a structure reachable by growth but not by shrinking is a bug waiting for the workload that reverses. Build the inverse deliberately and compare, rather than deriving each from scratch and hoping.

The symmetry extends to policy, not just to mechanism, and this is the part usually missed. If the shrinking side has learned to try redistribution before resorting to the destructive repair, the growing side should try redistribution before resorting to its destructive repair too — an overfull unit can hand items to a slack neighbour instead of splitting, exactly as an underfull one can borrow instead of merging. Asymmetric policy across two mirror operations is nearly always an oversight rather than a decision, and the tell is that one path has an optimization the other lacks for no stated reason. When you notice it, either transfer the tactic or write down why the two directions genuinely differ.

**Source:** [Algorithms and Data Structures](../works/algorithms-and-data-structures.md) — section 4.7.1's deletion handling, where reduction below the minimum occupancy is repaired by annexing from a neighbouring page, with the note that fetching that neighbour is a relatively costly operation and one is therefore tempted to annex more than a single item, leading to the even redistribution called page balancing; the fallback when the neighbour is already minimal, merging the two pages together with the middle item from their ancestor and described as exactly the inverse process of page splitting, with merging able to propagate to the root and reduce the tree's height just as splitting can propagate to the root and increase it; and the same section's closing note on the cited variation that page splitting should be delayed in the same way page merging is delayed, by first attempting to balance neighbouring pages.
