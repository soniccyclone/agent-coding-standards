# Restructure the candidate space before you optimize the search through it

**Lesson:** When no analytic solution is known, the honest first formulation of a problem is: enumerate the candidates, test each against the acceptance condition, stop when one passes. That formulation is almost always unusable, and the reason is worth stating precisely — not that testing is slow, but that the space being enumerated is the wrong space. So the design work is to shrink the space rather than to speed up the walk. The first technique is to split the acceptance condition into two parts, one of which can be built into the generator instead of tested by it: generate only candidates already satisfying that part, and test the remainder. A good split is identified by three simultaneous criteria — the restricted space must be dramatically smaller, its members must remain easy to generate, and the residual condition must be cheaper to check than the original. Missing any one of the three makes the split a loss, which is why this is a judgment and not a mechanical transformation.

The second technique is stronger because it changes the representation of a candidate rather than the population of candidates. Express a candidate as a sequence of increments, so that partial candidates exist and are meaningful. The property that makes this pay is a one-way implication: if the whole thing satisfies the condition then so does every prefix of it. Given that, a prefix that already fails can be abandoned with certainty, and an enormous subtree of the space disappears from consideration without being visited. Be exact about the direction of the implication, because the converse does not hold — a prefix that passes may still have no viable completion — and that asymmetry is the entire origin of backtracking. Pruning is sound; progress is not guaranteed; you must be able to shorten a partial candidate and try a different extension. Any incremental construction over a decomposable space has this shape, and recognizing it tells you both what you get for free and what machinery you are obliged to build.

The third element is the one people skip: do the arithmetic before choosing, and do it against the machine you actually have. Multiplying candidate count by cost per candidate turns "obviously too slow" into a number, and the numbers here span from hours of computation down to fractions of a second across the same problem with different formulations. That number is what tells you whether to go looking for another shortcut at all. It also means the answer depends on your circumstances rather than on the problem alone: a formulation that is comfortably fast on a powerful machine is unusable by hand, and being explicit about the cost model is what lets you decide how much cleverness to buy instead of reflexively buying all of it or none. Estimation is not a preliminary to the design decision; it is the design decision.
