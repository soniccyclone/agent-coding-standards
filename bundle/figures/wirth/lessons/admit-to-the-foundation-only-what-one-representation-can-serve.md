# Admit to the foundation only what one representation can serve for nearly everyone

**Lesson:** There is a clean criterion for how abstract a shared foundation may be, and it is not about elegance or about how commonly the concept appears. A concept belongs in the foundation only if a single choice of representation can be made once, by the foundation's implementer, and be acceptable for essentially every use. Numbers pass: the implementer knows the machine's arithmetic, the choice can be made for everyone, and the person writing a program above the foundation is thereby freed from the question — and, importantly, also barred from it, which is the price. Geometric objects fail, and the reason is precise: their proper representation depends on which operations will be performed on them, the frequency and nature of those operations cannot be known to whoever designs a general-purpose foundation, and so any choice made there will be wrong for some real application. The test to apply is therefore not "is this concept general?" but "does the right representation of this concept depend on facts only the caller knows?"

This puts a definite ceiling on the distance a foundation can put between itself and the machine, and it explains why that ceiling is a good thing rather than a failure of ambition. The closer the offered abstractions sit to what the machine does, the easier the representation choice is and the higher the probability that one choice suits everyone. Push further away and you gain expressiveness in the cases you anticipated and lose it in the cases you did not, because the caller who needed a different representation now has to work against yours instead of merely without it. The judgment is a two-sided one: too close to the machine and you have described nothing worth describing, too far and you have frozen decisions that varied.

Two practical consequences. First, when deciding what goes into a shared library, platform, or language, the argument that a concept is ubiquitous is not sufficient — ubiquity across applications with incompatible access patterns is an argument against inclusion, not for it. Second, the same criterion tells you what a foundation owes its users when it does include something: the freedom it takes away has to be compensated by a choice good enough that the user does not need to look, and where that is not achievable the honest move is to supply the pieces from which callers can build their own. Note that this reasoning is also why the right vehicle for describing algorithms is neither a machine-oriented notation nor a purely abstract one: the first leaves nothing to reason with above the hardware, and the second leaves the representation questions wide open, so the useful notation is the one that has already committed to representations that are known to be implementable everywhere.

**Source:** [Algorithms and Data Structures](../works/algorithms-and-data-structures.md) — section 1.1's argument that a programming language embodies an abstract machine whose abstractions free and bar the programmer from representation questions, that the closer the abstractions are to a given computer the higher the probability that a single representation choice suits all applications, that this sets definite limits on the degree of abstraction, and that geometric objects consequently do not belong in a general-purpose language because their representation depends on operations whose nature and frequency the language designer cannot know; together with the same section's rejection of both machine-oriented and purely abstract notations for describing algorithms.
