# Write both admissibility tests first; they tell you what state to carry

**Lesson:** In a search that builds a solution one commitment at a time, the recurring design question is what to hand down to the next step. The tempting approach is to pass the partial solution and let each level work out what it needs, which is correct and wasteful, or to guess at a few running totals, which is fast and arbitrary. There is a way to derive the answer instead. Write, in ordinary words, the condition under which each available move is worth exploring. Then read off the quantities those conditions mention. Those, and only those, are what the recursion must carry as parameters; anything else you were thinking of passing is not earning its place, and anything the conditions mention that you had not planned to pass is a recomputation you were about to hide inside the inner loop.

The step that repays the most attention is noticing that different moves are gated by different kinds of test, and that this is not an accident of the problem but a structural feature. Where a step commits to using something, the question is feasibility: does the commitment fit within the constraints, and if not, is there any point continuing along this branch at all. Where a step declines to use something, feasibility is trivially satisfied and the question is instead about the outcome: given what has been given up, is the best result still reachable from here better than the best result already found. The first is a constraint on the state; the second is a bound on the future. Treating both as "is this move legal" collapses them and loses the second, which is the one that actually prunes, because rejecting a branch on the grounds that it cannot beat the incumbent is what converts an enumeration of every subset into something that finishes.

Two consequences worth carrying to other problems. First, a bound is only usable if it is cheap to maintain incrementally along the path — an amount still achievable, updated by subtraction as each item is passed over, costs nothing per step, whereas a bound requiring a scan of what remains would cost more than the pruning saves. So the bound and the carried state are chosen together, not one after the other. Second, when the choice at each step is binary rather than a selection among many candidates, do not force it into a loop over two values. A loop is the right shape when the alternatives are generated by a rule; when there are exactly two and they are gated by different conditions and update different parts of the state, writing them out as two guarded statements is both shorter and honest about the fact that the two branches are not instances of a common case.

**Source:** [Algorithms and Data Structures](../works/algorithms-and-data-structures.md) — section 3.7's development of the optimal selection program, where the two possible outcomes of considering an object are noted to make a repeat or for statement inappropriate so the two cases are written out explicitly; the derivation of the acceptability condition for inclusion as fitting within the weight allowance and of the condition for exclusion as the still-achievable value exceeding the optimum so far, with the reasoning that if it does not then continuation of the search cannot yield the optimum and further search on that path is fruitless; and the consequent identification of the total weight of the selection and the still-achievable value as exactly the two quantities to be represented as parameters of the recursive procedure, together with the naming of the resulting scheme as branch and bound.
