# A belief that enforces itself needs a counterexample, not an argument

**Lesson:** Some constraints in a field are not facts about the subject but consequences of everyone acting as though they were facts, and the tell is circularity. Choose what to teach on the grounds of what is widely used; what gets taught most widely becomes what is used most widely; so the criterion validates itself and the field stops moving, with each individual choice locally defensible the whole way. The short-term commercial reasoning behind such a choice is genuinely sound, which is exactly what makes the loop durable — no participant is being stupid. The only intervention that works is refusing the criterion in a specific instance and absorbing the local cost, because arguing against the loop from inside it just supplies more evidence for the prevailing choice. Recognizing this pattern is a general skill: when the justification for a practice is that it is the practice, you are looking at a self-reinforcing equilibrium rather than a conclusion, and a decision made against it needs to be defended on different grounds than the ones the equilibrium is offering.

The same structure applies to widely accepted impossibility claims, and the way to handle those is to name them and adopt their negation as an explicit design aim. Two worth having on record: that a usable language must be either slow to compile or slow to execute, so you are obliged to pick which; and that any nontrivial system is bound to contain mistakes forever. Both are stated as commonly accepted notions, and both are stated in order to be dispelled by construction — a one-pass compiler, written in the language it compiles, for a language whose range of applicability was widened rather than narrowed. Notice what makes this a real argument rather than optimism: the claim is refuted by an artifact, not by reasoning about whether the claim ought to be true. An accepted impossibility is usually an observation about the artifacts people have happened to build, generalized past what the observation supports, and it stays unfalsified as long as everyone treats it as a constraint on what to attempt.

The discipline this requires is being able to tell the two kinds of received wisdom apart, because the failure mode in both directions is expensive. Some constraints really are constraints, and attacking them wastes years; some are equilibria, and accepting them wastes a career. The test that discriminates is whether the belief predicts something that could be checked by building the counterexample at a scale you can afford. Where it does, build it: an existence proof settles the question permanently and settles it for everyone, whereas an argument settles it for nobody. And be honest that the burden of proof lands on you and the counterexample has to work — a failed attempt to refute the claim strengthens it.

**Source:** [The Programming Language Pascal](../works/the-programming-language-pascal.md) — the introduction's two stated aims, the second being reliable and efficient implementations that dispel the commonly accepted notion that useful languages must be either slow to compile or slow to execute and the belief that any nontrivial system must contain mistakes forever; and the following paragraph on the objection to teaching in a language that is not widely used, the observation that the language most widely taught thereafter becomes the one most widely used, and the identification of that circularity as the safest recipe for stagnation and therefore worth an effort to break.
