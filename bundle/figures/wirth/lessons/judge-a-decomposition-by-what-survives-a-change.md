# Judge a decomposition by what survives optimization and an unforeseen change of purpose

**Lesson:** A decomposition cannot be evaluated at the moment it is made, because at that moment every plausible split looks defensible. It can be evaluated by two later events, and both are cheap to observe. The first is optimization: after the efficiency work is done, does the program still exhibit the structure of the first version? If it does, the named parts were the real joints of the problem, and the speed came from refining inside them. If the fast version is unrecognizable, then the original names described a story about the problem rather than its structure, and the audit trail from intention to code has been destroyed — which is what makes fast programs unmaintainable far more often than the tricks in them do. Performance work belongs inside the decomposition as further refinement steps, and needing to dissolve the decomposition to go faster is a finding about the decomposition.

The second event is a change of purpose that was not anticipated. Programs that work correctly do not stay unchanged; users discover the results were incomplete or, worse, that the results requested were not what was needed. The measurement to take is which parts have to be touched. When a program is asked to produce every solution rather than one, and the answer is a modified outer control structure over a vocabulary of constituent operations adopted unchanged, the decomposition has been vindicated — and the payoff is counted in reverification effort as much as in edit effort, since the untouched parts keep whatever argument already justified them. This is the practical content of the claim that adaptability to changing objectives and portability to a different environment are both primarily measures of how well a program is structured. They are not separate virtues to be engineered individually; they are the same property observed under two different kinds of pressure.

Two consequences for judgment. Because the same vocabulary of operations can be assembled under different global control — the same algorithm expressed as explicit iteration with an explicit backtrack, or as a recursive procedure where the retreat is the return, performing identically and producing the same results in the same order — the interesting question about a design is rarely which control structure to use. It is whether the vocabulary is right, since that is what both versions share and what a later change will either reuse or discard. And because a decomposition is judged by these downstream properties, correctness of the result is not sufficient grounds for accepting a solution. A working program can be rejected on clarity, regularity, or economy, and being willing to revoke an earlier decision — back to the top of the refinement if that is where the bad decision was — is part of the method rather than an admission of failure. The discipline of making each decision explicit is what makes this possible: you can only revoke a decision you can locate.
