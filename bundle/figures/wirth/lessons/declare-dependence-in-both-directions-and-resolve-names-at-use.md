# Declare dependence in both directions, and resolve names at the point of use once the participant set is open

**Lesson:** A boundary around a component is worth little if it is opaque by default or transparent by default; what makes it useful is that its permeability is stated, in the component's own heading, in both directions at once. One list names everything declared outside that the inside uses. Another names everything declared inside that the outside may use. Anything not on either list is inaccessible in that direction, and the consequence is that a reader learns the component's full coupling from its first few lines without reading its body — the incoming list is its dependency set, the outgoing list is its obligation set, and neither can be inferred wrongly because neither is implicit. Notice which problem this solves that a one-directional facility does not. Hiding internals tells you what a component protects; it does not tell you what it needs. A component whose imports are declared can be moved, tested, or reasoned about against a known environment, because the environment it requires is a written list rather than whatever happened to be in scope.

The interesting design pressure appears when the set of components that will coexist is not known in advance. Inside one program written by one author, a name exported into the surrounding scope is fine: the author can see every other name and avoid collisions. As soon as a component is written to be used alongside others its author has never seen, that argument fails, and the failure is not a small inconvenience — two components independently exporting the same identifier for different things produce a conflict that neither author can fix and that appears only at the moment of assembly. The resolution is to move the decision about how a name is spelled from the point of definition to the point of use: the exporter may insist that its names be written prefixed with its own, so that every reference carries the origin. This costs a little verbosity and buys the property that adding a component to a system can never invalidate the naming of the components already there. Offer the shorter unqualified form as a local override that an importer may request for names it chooses — the caller knows which collisions matter in its own text, and the definer does not.

The generalizable judgment is about who has the information. Global uniqueness of names is a property nobody can maintain unilaterally, so a design that depends on it is depending on coordination that will not happen. Whenever a decision requires knowledge of the whole assembly, either take it at assembly time or give it to the party that can see the assembly, and never let a definer's convenience impose a constraint on a set of participants it cannot enumerate. The same test applies well beyond identifiers: any facility that quietly requires all users to agree is a facility that will fail at the exact moment the system becomes large enough to matter.
