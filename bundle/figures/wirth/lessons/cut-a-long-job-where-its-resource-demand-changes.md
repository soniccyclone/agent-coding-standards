# Cut a long job where its resource demand changes, and let each piece name its successor

**Lesson:** In a system where an activity runs to completion once started, a long activity is not merely slow, it is a denial of service to everything else. The obvious remedies are to abandon run-to-completion or to slice the activity arbitrarily — chop it into equal time quanta and yield between them. The second is better than the first and still crude, because arbitrary cut points force every piece to carry across whatever state happened to be live at that instant, and the amount of carried state is what makes such decompositions unpleasant to write and to read.

There is a much better place to cut, and it is found by asking what each part of the activity actually consumes rather than how long it takes. Long activities are usually not uniform: some stretches need the processor continuously, and others are waits on something else doing the work — a device transferring on its own, a peer responding, a medium draining. Those stretches are radically different in what yielding costs. Suspending during a processor-bound stretch costs a genuine interruption of progress; suspending during a stretch where the processor is idle anyway costs nothing at all and returns the entire duration to everyone else. So cut at the transitions between them. The resulting pieces are not equal in length and should not be — one is short and busy, the next is long and free — and their boundaries fall exactly where the live state is naturally small, because a transition between resources is a point where the activity has just handed off a complete unit of work.

Having cut, the pieces need to be sequenced, and the tidy way is to let each piece nominate what runs next rather than have a controller that tracks where the activity has got to. Each piece is then a complete statement of one stage: do this, then arrange for that stage to follow. Branching — no work available, an error, a repeat of the same stage — becomes a choice of successor, so the whole flow of a long activity is readable as a small graph over named stages, with no separate progress variable to be interpreted and no possibility of the recorded position disagreeing with what is actually installed. What began as an intrusive, uninterruptible job becomes a set of short obligations that interleave with everything else, and the interleaving is safe precisely because each stage ends where nothing is half-done.

**Source:** [Project Oberon](../works/project-oberon.md) — section 11.3's account of the print server, which notes that the printer manages about ten pages a minute so the shortest task takes at least six seconds, that every command in the system is inherently non-interruptible, and that a printing task must therefore be broken into phases with control returned to the scheduler after each; the observation that processing a page consists of reading and interpreting the print stream into a raster, which requires the processor and is typically much the less time-consuming part, followed by transmission of the raster during which the processor is disengaged because the transfer proceeds under printer control by direct memory access, leaving the processor free to accept other requests; and the statement that each of the four phases is a handler procedure, one installed as a scheduled task at any time, with each terminating handler installing its selected successor.
