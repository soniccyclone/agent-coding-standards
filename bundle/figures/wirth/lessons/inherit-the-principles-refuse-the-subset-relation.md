# Inherit a predecessor's principles, refuse to contain it as a subset

**Lesson:** Starting from nothing is a self-indulgence with a cost, so the default should be to build on what exists wherever it already meets your objectives — and the way to make that a real test rather than a gesture is to state the objectives first, then check the candidate against them. Where a predecessor satisfies them better than anything else available, take it: not just its ideas in general but the specific solved forms, the structuring principles and the shape of expressions, and the individual constructs whose design was already settled correctly. Novelty for its own sake is the failure mode on one side, and there is nothing to be gained by reinventing a form that was got right the first time.

The separate question, and the one that gets conflated with it, is whether the new thing must contain the old thing. Compatibility of that strict kind — the predecessor being a subset of the successor — is a much heavier commitment than inheritance, and it is worth refusing when the ancestor's construction principles conflict with what the new features need. The particular collision here is instructive: the old way of writing declarations could not coexist with a natural representation of the new data structuring facilities. Accepting the subset relation would have meant carrying two incompatible declaration schemes forever so that old texts remained legal, which imports the ancestor's design errors into everything that follows and permanently doubles the number of ways to say things. Declining it means old texts are not legal, and that is a bounded, one-time cost paid by a known population.

What makes the refusal defensible is that the cost gets measured rather than waved away. The claim to make is not that compatibility does not matter but that converting existing programs amounts to negligible transcription — and it is stronger still if you can say which discipline in the old programs makes it so, since texts written to a sensible restricted subset of the ancestor convert most easily of all. That is the shape of the argument to reach for whenever this question arises: compare the one-time migration cost against the permanent cost of carrying the incompatible construct, and be specific about who bears each. Migration is a finite expense borne once by the people who have existing artifacts; compatibility is an unbounded expense borne forever by everyone, including everyone who arrives later and never had an old program at all. The asymmetry usually favors the break, and stating it in those terms is what turns a preference into a decision that can be argued.

**Source:** [The Programming Language Pascal](../works/the-programming-language-pascal.md) — the introduction's argument that a new language should not be developed for novelty's sake and that existing languages should be used as a basis wherever they meet the chosen objectives of systematic structure, flexible program and data structuring, and efficient implementability; the adoption of Algol 60's structuring principles and form of expressions on those grounds; the explicit decision not to adopt Algol 60 as a subset because its declaration construction principles would have been incompatible with a natural representation of the new features; the assessment of conversion as a negligible transcription effort, particularly for programs obeying the IFIP subset rules; and the note that the while and case statements and record structures were taken from an earlier Algol successor effort.
