# Improve the dominant term or do not call it an improvement

**Lesson:** Before optimizing, separate the work a method does into its kinds and find out which kind dominates, both in count and in unit cost. Then check that the change you are contemplating touches that kind. The failure is embarrassingly common and hard to see from inside: replace a linear scan for an insertion point with a bisection and the number of comparisons drops from quadratic to logarithmic per step, which sounds decisive and is nearly worthless, because the number of element movements is untouched and still quadratic, and moving an element costs more than comparing two keys. The same trap catches the well-known refinements to the simplest exchange method — remembering whether any exchange occurred, remembering where the last one was, alternating the direction of the sweep. Every one of them reduces redundant checking; not one of them changes the number of exchanges, which is the expensive operation. Clever improvements to the cheap operation produce a much less profound effect than intuition promises.

Worse than negligible is negative, and an improvement should be checked against the input class you actually see rather than the average. Bisecting the search for an insertion point makes the number of comparisons almost independent of the initial order, which sounds like a virtue and destroys a property the naive version had: the naive version was fastest exactly when the data was already nearly in order, and the refined one is slowest there. Since nearly-ordered input is the common case in practice, the refinement is a deterioration on the workload that matters. Characterize a method by what it does on already-ordered, random, and reversed input separately, treat sensitivity to existing order as a first-class property rather than a footnote, and know which of the three your callers will hand you.

Two further guards, both against the same overconfidence. Asymptotic classification is not an ordering of programs: the simple quadratic methods are genuinely faster below some size, so the coefficients and the crossover point are part of the answer, and finding them requires measurement on a real machine because the formulas omit everything that is not a comparison or a move. And sophistication has its own price that must be entered on the same ledger — a refinement combining two separate precomputations, each individually sound, can be rejected on the grounds that the extra table construction and the more complicated inner loop cost more than the improved shifts save, leaving genuine doubt about whether the elaborate version is better at all. The discipline is to make the ledger explicit: what got cheaper, what got more expensive, on which inputs, and at what size. An optimization defended without that ledger is a guess wearing formal clothing.

**Source:** [Algorithms and Data Structures](../works/algorithms-and-data-structures.md) — section 2.2.1's analysis of binary insertion, where the improvement applies only to comparisons while the dominant move term remains quadratic and sorting an already-sorted array becomes slower, with the explicit remark that an obvious improvement may turn out to be a deterioration; section 2.2.3's note that the refinements to the exchange sort reduce only redundant checks and in no way affect the number of exchanges; section 2.3.5 on the need for experimental coefficients because the formulas ignore loop control and other effort, and on straight methods being faster for sufficiently small n; and section 1.9.3's refusal of the combined Boyer-Moore and Knuth-Morris-Pratt strategy because the additional table generation and overhead cast doubt on whether the extension is an improvement or a deterioration.
