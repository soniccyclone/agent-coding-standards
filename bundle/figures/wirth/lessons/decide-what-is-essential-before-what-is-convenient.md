# Sort every design question into essential or ephemeral, and never let an ephemeral one perturb the core

**Lesson:** Design arguments consume attention in proportion to how loudly they are made, not to how much rides on them, so the first discipline is triage: before deciding anything, decide which questions are load-bearing. Whether a language has a coherent, consistent scheme of data types is load-bearing, because everything else is phrased in terms of it. Whether the compiler folds letter case, or which of four spellings of a loop is offered, is not; those questions can be answered any way at all without changing what the system fundamentally is. The same split runs through hardware: addressing modes and a complete, consistent arithmetic set with proper overflow traps are structural, while the details of a prioritized interrupt scheme are peripheral. The point of the sort is not to skip the small questions. It is to keep them from being answered first, because whichever questions get settled first are the ones the rest of the design bends around.

The rule that follows is structural, and it is stronger than "prioritize": the ephemeral must never be permitted to reach into the systematic design of the central facilities. Peripheral conveniences get fitted onto a framework that was already coherent without them. Invert that order — let a special case for some frequent construct dictate the shape of a core mechanism — and you have traded a property that holds everywhere for a benefit that holds once. This is why the honest way to evaluate a proposed feature is against two costs, not one. There is the cost of implementing it, and there is the cost of its mere presence: it enlarges everything that must be described, learned, checked, and kept consistent as the system evolves, whether or not anyone uses it. A feature that is genuinely nice to have occasionally can still be a bad trade, and saying so out loud is the whole skill.

Two forces push against this and both are worth naming, because a force you can name is one you can refuse. The first is the wish to please. Every request comes from someone real, and refusing it feels like a failure of service, so the desire to accommodate quietly displaces the goal of a consistent design. The second is that the costs land on invisible people. The implementer's effort is visible and finite; the memory occupied is visible and cheap. What is invisible is the aggregate labor of everyone who afterwards has to understand the thing well enough to use it correctly, and that is where the real expense sits. Consider the concrete asymmetry between two contemporaneous designs where one shipped a compiler of a few thousand lines years earlier and the other shipped hundreds of thousands of lines years later: the second bought some occasional conveniences and paid in comprehensibility for everyone downstream, permanently. Admitting that your own small system is already at the edge of what you can hold in your head is not modesty; it is the measurement that tells you the budget is spent.
