# Choose passes that preserve each other's work — and that overlap

**Lesson:** Attacking a problem with several coarse-to-fine passes looks wasteful, because every pass touches everything and the last pass alone is a complete solution. It is not wasteful, and the reason tells you how to design such a scheme. Each pass leaves the data in a state that makes the next pass cheap — either because it operates on small independent pieces, or because the pieces it operates on are already nearly in the desired state — so the total work can be far below what one direct pass would cost. The correctness is free: as long as the final pass is the full one, the answer is right no matter what the earlier passes did. That separation is the reason the design is safe to experiment with. Correctness rides on the last pass, and the earlier ones are pure optimization, so a bad choice of schedule costs time and cannot cost correctness.

The property that makes the scheme actually pay is preservation, and it has to be established rather than hoped for. If a later pass can undo the structure an earlier one imposed, each pass starts from scratch and the whole idea collapses into repeated work. What you want is a theorem of the shape "a state already ordered under the earlier criterion remains ordered under it after the later pass runs," and if you cannot argue for something like it, the schedule is not doing what you think. Notice that this is exactly the question people skip when they stack heuristics or cleanup phases in a pipeline: each stage is justified locally and nobody checks whether stage three destroys what stage one achieved.

The second property is overlap, and it is the counterintuitive one. If successive passes partition the data along nested boundaries — each pass merging groups the previous pass had already worked on, and only those — then whole regions of the data never interact until the very end, and the work is concentrated into the final pass instead of spread across the schedule. The fix is to choose the partitions so they cut across each other rather than nest, which is why a schedule whose parameters are multiples of one another performs worse than one whose parameters share no factors. The general form is worth remembering outside sorting: when you decompose work into groups repeatedly, deliberately make consecutive decompositions incommensurable, so information propagates between groups at every stage rather than waiting for a merge. And accept that the optimal schedule may be genuinely unknown — a scheme can be well-founded, obviously correct and clearly better than the alternatives while its exact analysis remains open, which is a reason to test schedules empirically, not a reason to distrust the method.

**Source:** [Algorithms and Data Structures](../works/algorithms-and-data-structures.md) — section 2.3.1's presentation of insertion sort with diminishing increments: the observation that several passes over all items do not introduce more work than they save because each pass either involves few items or finds them already well ordered; the statement that any sequence of increments is acceptable provided the last is unity, since in the worst case the final pass does all the work; the theorem that a sequence already sorted at one increment remains so after being sorted at another; and the finding that increments should not be multiples of each other, since the powers-of-two schedule has each pass combine two chains that previously had no interaction whatsoever, together with the acknowledgement that the analysis poses mathematical problems many of which remain unsolved and that the best choice of increments is not known.
