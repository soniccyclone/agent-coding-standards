# Let the shape of the definition decide recursion or iteration, and prove the depth is small

**Lesson:** The choice between a self-referential formulation and a loop is decidable from the shape of what you are computing, and treating it as a matter of taste is how both camps end up wrong. There is a syntactic test for the cases where self-reference buys nothing: if the self-reference occurs exactly once and sits at the very beginning or the very end of the composition, the definition is a recurrence and translates mechanically into a loop with an accumulator, and the self-referential version has bought only overhead. Teaching the concept with examples of that shape is what produced a generation's apprehension about the technique and the belief that it is inherently inefficient — the examples were the ones where it was pointless. A worse case in the same family: a definition referring to itself twice over overlapping smaller cases recomputes the same values exponentially many times, and rewriting it as a loop carrying two accumulators is not an optimization but a repair.

The converse case is decided by the same test and deserves equal firmness. Where the object being processed or the function being computed is itself defined self-referentially — a structure whose parts are structures of the same kind, a figure composed of rotated half-size copies of itself — the self-referential program is the right one, and its virtue is that correctness can be read off the correspondence between the program's composition and the definition's. Converting such a program to a loop is always possible, since the machine underneath is not self-referential, but it requires managing the pending work explicitly, and the resulting text obscures the essence badly enough that it becomes very difficult to understand. That is a real cost, not an aesthetic complaint: a program whose structure no longer mirrors the definition cannot be checked against the definition.

The obligation that comes with the self-referential form is to bound its depth, and the discipline is to make the bound structural rather than argued. Establish termination the same way as for a loop — exhibit a quantity that decreases at each step and whose exhaustion falsifies the continuation condition — and then, better, give the procedure a parameter that is decremented on each call so termination is guaranteed by the parameter's presence and cannot be broken by later editing. Beyond termination, show the depth is not merely finite but small, since each pending activation costs storage for its own variables and its resumption point. This is a design constraint with teeth, and it can be met by re-examination rather than abandonment: a partitioning method that recurses on both halves can reach depth proportional to the whole input in the worst case, and the fix is not to abandon self-reference but to dispatch the larger part as pending work and continue directly with the smaller, which bounds the depth logarithmically and converts a case that was inappropriate into one that is entirely practical.

**Source:** [Algorithms and Data Structures](../works/algorithms-and-data-structures.md) — section 3.1's schemata for recursive composition and its requirement that the depth be shown finite and quite small because each activation needs storage for its variables and the resumption state; section 3.2's schema identifying the single trailing or leading call as the case to transcribe into iteration, the factorial and Fibonacci examples with the exponential activation count of the latter, and the conclusion that algorithms recursive by nature should be formulated recursively because the explicit recursion stack otherwise obscures the essence; section 3.3's remark that the correctness of the Hilbert and Sierpinski curve programs can be deduced from their structure and that the explicit decreasing level parameter guarantees termination; and section 3.1's reference back to the partition sort, where stacking the longer partition limits the depth from n to log n.
