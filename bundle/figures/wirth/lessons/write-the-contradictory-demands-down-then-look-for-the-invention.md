# Write the contradictory demands down, then look for the invention before settling for the compromise

**Lesson:** Any artifact with more than one constituency accumulates a list of demands that cannot all be met, and the useful first move is to write the whole list out plainly rather than to argue it one item at a time. Easy to learn and safe from misuse; extensible without disturbing what exists; rigorously defined for the logicians and conveniently notated for people already used to something less logical; defined without reference to any particular machine and yet able to exploit the machine you have; a compiler that emits efficient code, is itself small and fast, and contains no rarely-used optimization machinery; a definition that is complete and self-contained and in which a reader can quickly find just what he needs; ready access to whatever else the surrounding system offers; portable to other machines and adaptable to other environments; and all of it built quickly and cheaply. Seeing them together does two things at once. It makes visible which pairs are actually in conflict — and it makes visible that most pairs are not, which is the more useful half, because it stops a real tension from being used to excuse a general shrug. The designer's job is then to decide where the emphasis goes, and that is a decision to be made deliberately and stated, not one to be arrived at by accretion.

This is what makes the activity an engineering discipline rather than a mathematical or an artistic one: the defining work is reconciling conflicting requirements through chosen compromises, exactly as in any other engineering field. Two consequences follow that are unwelcome but worth accepting early. It can be learned only by experience, and the experience that teaches is failure — so a designer with no failures behind them has not yet acquired the thing the job requires. And because the conflicting demands come from different people, all of whom you are nominally serving, unanimous approval is not an available outcome; expecting it will push you toward accommodating everyone, which is how the list's contradictions get resolved by including all of them.

The important qualification is that a stated tension is a hypothesis, not a law, and that the highest-value design work is the invention that dissolves one. Satisfying one criterion does not necessarily mean surrendering another; real progress shows up as a facility that serves several aims which had appeared to be opposed. A restricted loop construct gives more flexibility than the counted form and a stronger reasoning rule than the unrestricted jump, rather than trading one for the other. A structured, densely laid out record with named typed parts serves compactness, checkability, and readability simultaneously, where the previous option had you choose. So the sequence matters: enumerate the conflicts, then spend real effort hunting for the construct that voids one, and only then split the difference. A compromise reached before that search has been made is usually just an unexamined default, and it forecloses the possibility that the conflict was an artifact of the available vocabulary rather than a property of the problem.

**Source:** [On the Design of Programming Languages](../works/on-the-design-of-programming-languages.md) — the "Language Design is Decision Making" section: the enumerated list of frequently encountered demands, the observation that several but not all are contradictory and that the designer must decide where to place emphasis and find a balanced compromise, the framing of language design as a typical engineering discipline masterable only by experience usually gained after failures with no expectation of unanimous approval, and the closing qualification that satisfying one criterion need not sacrifice another because true progress appears through facilities catering to several seemingly contradictory aims, as the three earlier examples demonstrate.
