# If the language can express a facility, the facility does not belong in the language

**Lesson:** There is a clean test for whether a capability should be built into a foundation or supplied on top of it, and it inverts the usual instinct. If the foundation is capable enough to express the capability, then the capability must not be part of it. Input conversion, file handling, storage allocation, process scheduling: these are exactly the things a systems foundation has to be able to express, and that is precisely the argument for their being ordinary components written in it rather than constructs of it. The reasoning is about replaceability. A facility built in has one behavior for everyone forever; the same facility as a component can be substituted by a caller whose needs differ, and — the case people forget — can be left out entirely by a caller who does not need it at all. Accept the consequence honestly: a usable delivery then consists of the foundation plus a standard collection of such components, so the components are not optional in practice. What has changed is not whether they ship but whether they can be displaced, and that is the whole point.

The sharpest form of the argument is choosing the lower-level primitive over the higher-level policy. A predecessor offered processes with a synchronization discipline built in; the successor removes both and offers only the bare transfer of control between coroutines, from which the processes and the synchronization can be built as an ordinary module. This looks like a regression and is not, for two reasons that are worth separating. First, scheduling is policy, policy varies by application, and a programmer who writes his own scheduler gets one fitted to his actual needs rather than one fitted to the average of everyone's. Second, and less obvious, the built-in version cannot be omitted: in the frequent simple case where concurrency appears only as device handling, a program built on the primitive carries no scheduler at all, while a program built on a language with scheduling inside carries it whether it uses it or not. Generalize the pattern: when a mechanism embodies a decision that different users would make differently, push it out of the foundation and expose the primitive it was built from, and check specifically whether the built-in version is escapable by someone who wants none of it.

The judgment this requires is knowing which primitive to expose, and it is a real judgment rather than a mechanical descent. Going lower is not automatically better — the point is to find the level at which the varying policy has been factored out and nothing else has, so that everything above can be built without breaking in. Too high and you have shipped a policy; too low and every client reimplements the same difficult thing, which is a policy decision made by exhaustion. Notice also what this does to the boundary of "the language": it moves complexity from a place where it is frozen and unavoidable to a place where it is visible, readable, and optional. That is the same amount of machinery, held differently, and the difference is entirely in who gets to decide.
