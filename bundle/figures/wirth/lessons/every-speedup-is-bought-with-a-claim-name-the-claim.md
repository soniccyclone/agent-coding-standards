# Every speedup is bought with a claim; name the claim and notice when you stop proving it

**Lesson:** Reducing an optimization problem from unusable to practical usually happens in discrete steps, and each step is purchased with a specific statement about the problem that has to be true. Written out, the ladder is legible and reusable. The first rung is a claim about composition: that an optimal whole is built from optimal parts, so the parts can be solved once each and combined, and the space of whole configurations — which grows explosively — never has to be enumerated. That claim converts an exponential search into a systematic sweep over subproblems, ordered by size, each solved from smaller ones already in hand.

The second rung is a claim about where answers can lie. Having solved neighbouring subproblems, you may be able to prove that the answer to the current one is confined between their answers — that extending a subproblem on one side, or trimming it on the other, cannot move the optimal choice past a known point. Where such a monotonicity holds, the inner search that would scan every candidate becomes a scan of a short interval, and the total cost drops by a whole factor of the problem size. This is the highest-value rung to go looking for, because it costs only an argument and the argument is usually short: ask, of any exhaustive inner loop, whether the answers to adjacent instances constrain this one. If they do, the loop was scanning territory that could have been excluded by reasoning.

The third rung is different in kind and should be recognized as such. When even the reduced algorithm exceeds what you can spend — and note that the binding resource is often memory rather than time, since a table indexed by pairs of positions grows quadratically in space as well — the remaining move is to give up exactness. You locate the answer near some cheaply computed statistic of the input, justify the guess by an argument about likelihood rather than necessity, and search locally around it. This is legitimate, but it is not another optimization; it changes the output from the optimum to something near it, and the honest presentation says so. Note too that the concession need not be total: once the heuristic has cut the problem into pieces small enough for the exact method, the exact method comes back. Mixing an approximate decomposition with an exact base case is a standard and underused shape. What matters is that the boundary between the proved claims and the plausible one is drawn explicitly, because everything downstream that treats the result as optimal is relying on where you drew it.

**Source:** [Algorithms and Data Structures](../works/algorithms-and-data-structures.md) — section 4.6's optimal search tree construction: the observation that the number of configurations grows exponentially, the property that all subtrees of an optimal tree are themselves optimal and the resulting bottom-up construction from single nodes outward, the recurrence for the weighted path length of each subrange, the cited observation that the optimal root of a subrange cannot move left when the range is extended on the right or trimmed on the left and the resulting confinement of the inner search between the roots of the two neighbouring subranges, reducing the count of elementary steps by a factor of the problem size; and the closing discussion that both time and storage are quadratic and therefore unacceptable for large inputs, together with the cited alternative that abandons the optimum for a nearly optimal tree by locating the root near the centroid of the weight distribution, searching locally around it, recursing on the two resulting subtrees, and reverting to the exact algorithm once the subtrees are of manageable size.
