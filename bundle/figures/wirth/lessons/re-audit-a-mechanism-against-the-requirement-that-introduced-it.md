# Re-audit a mechanism against the requirement that introduced it

**Lesson:** Mechanisms are adopted to satisfy a requirement, and they outlive it. The requirement disappears quietly — a resource becomes abundant, a usage pattern goes away, a competing constraint relaxes — while the mechanism stays, because by then it is standard, it is what the components provide, and nobody is assigned to notice. So the audit is worth performing deliberately: for each substantial mechanism in a system, name the requirement it was introduced to meet, and check whether that requirement still exists. If it does not, the mechanism is now paying for nothing, and you are entitled to ask what the system looks like without it rather than treating its presence as given.

Two things make the audit hard, and both are worth naming because they are the reason the mechanism survives. Integration hides cost. Once an elaborate scheme is absorbed into a component and delivered as a single part, its complexity stops being visible to anyone assembling systems from those parts — it is not less complex, it has merely become somebody else's, and its effects are inherited rather than chosen. And the cost that remains is often not throughput but *predictability*: a mechanism whose fast path depends on a cache has timing that varies with history in ways the caller cannot model, which is tolerable for most work and disqualifying for anything that must meet a deadline. A mechanism should be priced in both currencies, and the second is the one that no benchmark shows.

The way to make the audit conclusive rather than argumentative is to build both versions and count. The comparison that settles it is not the code in the path the mechanism accelerates but the *total* — the code that uses the mechanism plus the code that manages the mechanism itself, against the code that does the job without it. The result is frequently the reverse of the assumption: a scheme adopted to simplify a task turns out to make the whole substantially larger once its own administration is included, and what is left of its case is a short list of secondary benefits that can then be weighed honestly. Note that the remaining benefits are usually real but small, and one of them is often a safety property obtainable another way, so the honest conclusion is not that the mechanism is worthless but that it is no longer paying for itself in this setting — which is a claim about the setting, and should be recorded with the setting attached.

**Source:** [Project Oberon](../works/project-oberon.md) — section 8.2's comparison of block allocation with and without an address-mapping unit, reporting sixty-nine instructions for the version without virtual addressing against forty-nine with it, and sixty-nine against one hundred and twenty-four once the routines managing the page tables are counted, so that the version without the mechanism is smaller overall; the enumeration of the remaining advantages as better storage utilization, dismissed as negligible, and the trapping of inadvertent references to unloaded modules; the recollection that address mapping was introduced as a requirement for virtual memory backed by disk with pages moved out and retrieved on demand, and the observation that demand paging has lost its significance given large primary stores; and the concluding assessment that address translation was essential for multi-user operating systems but is dispensable overkill for a single-user workstation, that integrating the whole translation and caching scheme onto one chip led to its considerable complexity being hidden and ignored, and that its essentially unpredictable effects on execution speed make such systems virtually unusable where real-time constraints are tight.
