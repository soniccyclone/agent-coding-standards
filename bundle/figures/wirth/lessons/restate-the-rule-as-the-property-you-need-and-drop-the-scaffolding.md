# Restate the rule as the property you actually need, and drop the concept you arrived by

**Lesson:** A design that was derived from another design inherits that other design's vocabulary, and the inherited vocabulary keeps imposing constraints long after the reason for them is gone. The symptom is a rule that has to be stated with an exception, or an operation that treats two mirror-image situations differently for no reason you can articulate. There may be no proof that anything is wrong — the thing works, the bounds hold — but unexplained asymmetry in a design is evidence of a concept being carried past its usefulness, and it is worth acting on that evidence rather than waiting for a counterexample. Ask what the design is actually required to guarantee, state that requirement directly, and see whether the inherited concept survives the restatement.

Usually it does not, and what replaces it is smaller. The property being protected turns out to be a purely local condition — something that must never happen twice in a row along a path, or must always hold between an element and its immediate neighbour — where the inherited concept was a grouping that enforced the local condition indirectly and clumsily. Stating the local condition as the definition removes the scaffolding, removes the special cases that existed only to preserve it, and removes the asymmetry, because the original asymmetry was an artifact of how the grouping had to be laid out rather than of anything the guarantee required.

Two things follow, and both are worth expecting. The restated rule usually admits strictly more configurations than the old one, since the old one was over-constraining in the service of a concept nobody needs any more, and a larger admissible class means restructuring is triggered less often — while the average cost of an operation on a member of the class rises slightly, because the class now includes shapes the stricter rule excluded. That is a real trade with a legible shape: fewer repairs, slightly worse typical case. Which side wins depends on the ratio of queries to updates in your workload, and it is honest to say that the crossover point is hard to locate in general and depends on implementation details as much as on the algorithms. The second consequence is that the restated design will often turn out to be something already known under another name, arrived at by a different route — which is confirmation that the restatement found the real property rather than a new arbitrary one.

**Source:** [Algorithms and Data Structures](../works/algorithms-and-data-structures.md) — section 4.7.2's observation that the insertion algorithm for binary B-trees reveals a strange asymmetry in handling growth of left and right subtrees, that there is no proof of strangeness yet intuition says the asymmetry should be removed, and the accompanying note that searching cannot distinguish a horizontal from a vertical link so worrying about which side may be horizontal is artificial; the resulting advice to stop adhering to the notion of pages out of which the organization developed, since all that is required is the bound on maximum path length, which needs only the guarantee that two horizontal pointers never occur in succession on a search path; the restated three-property definition of the symmetric form and its derivation of the same bound; the comparison noting that the balanced trees form a subset of the symmetric ones so the larger class has longer average paths but requires rearrangement less frequently, with the preference depending on the retrieval-to-update quotient and on implementation characteristics including densely packed node records; and the closing note that this structure was later rediscovered under another name, differing only in where the distinguishing bit is stored.
