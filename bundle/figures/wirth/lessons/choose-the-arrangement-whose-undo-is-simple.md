# Choose the arrangement whose undo is simple, not the one whose forward move is free

**Lesson:** When a resource is subdivided repeatedly, the tempting discipline is the least constrained one: split anything, anywhere, in any direction. Each individual split is trivially easy and the arrangement can express whatever the user asks for. The trouble arrives only on removal. Give back a region that was created early and has since been surrounded, and there is no canonical way to reabsorb it — the freed space can be given to any of several neighbours, or shared among them, and each choice yields a different arrangement and requires its own case in the code. The forward operation was uniform and the inverse is not, which means the total complexity of the scheme was never visible while the scheme was being chosen.

So evaluate a structuring discipline by its hardest operation, and in a scheme that both creates and destroys, that is nearly always the destruction. A more constrained discipline — subdivide along one axis at the top level, along the other within each part — forbids some arrangements a user might have wanted and in exchange makes removal mechanical: the freed region has exactly one candidate to merge with, and reabsorbing it is the literal reverse of the split that made it. The constraint is not a limitation grudgingly accepted for implementation convenience; it is the thing that makes the operation set closed and the code uniform, and it should be chosen for that reason and defended on those grounds.

There is a second, independent policy question hiding in the same place, and it is worth separating: given the freedom to place something new, which existing occupants should be disturbed? One answer redistributes the resource evenly across all occupants, which is fair, keeps a clean invariant, and forces every existing occupant to move and re-lay-out. The other subdivides the single largest occupant and touches nothing else. The second is worse by the tidiness criterion and better by the criterion that actually matters to someone using the system, which is that what they were already looking at does not change under them. Stability of existing content is a first-class property, and a policy that keeps a global invariant beautiful at the cost of perturbing everything on every insertion is optimizing the wrong thing.

The generalizable pair: prefer the structure whose inverse operation is uniform, and prefer the placement policy whose blast radius is smallest. Both are decided at design time, both are cheap to decide then, and both are extremely expensive to change once every part of the system has come to depend on the arrangement being repairable.

**Source:** [Project Oberon](../works/project-oberon.md) — section 4.1's comparison of screen-structuring strategies, in particular the observation that unconstrained tiling by iterated horizontal or vertical splitting makes reversing an opening straightforward only when the closed region is the most recent, whereas closing an earlier one admits no such simple procedure since the freed area can be shared among several neighbours in more than one way, and the explicit statement that hierarchic tiling was chosen mainly because the algorithm of reusing the area of a closed viewer is simpler and more uniform and no such complicated situations can occur; together with the same section's contrast of placement heuristics, where one system's default invariant divides each track evenly among its viewers so that existing viewers must reduce their size and move when a new one is placed, while the other splits the largest existing viewer into two halves, noted as advantageous because existing contents are kept stable; and section 4.1's listing of the drawbacks of overlapping, including reliance on subordinate management of arbitrary sub-rectangles and clipping because partially overlapped regions must be individually restored, the danger of losing a region by covering it completely, and the absence of canonical heuristics for automatic allocation.
