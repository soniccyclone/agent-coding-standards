# Where you inject randomness matters more than how much: one hidden commitment held consistently beats a fresh coin flip per decision

**Lesson:** Two randomized policies for the same problem sit side by side in this paper, and the gap between them is the most surprising thing in it. The first policy flips a coin at every decision point, picking uniformly among the currently acceptable options. It is the design any engineer would write first, and its guarantee is essentially no better than the guarantee of a policy with no randomness at all. The second policy flips its coins once, before any input arrives, fixing a secret priority order over the resources, and then behaves entirely deterministically forever after: always take the acceptable resource of highest priority. That policy achieves the best guarantee any policy of any kind can achieve. Same problem, comparable quantity of randomness, and the difference is only where the randomness sits.

The reason is worth internalizing because it is counterintuitive. Re-rolling per decision makes each decision individually unbiased but makes the sequence of decisions incoherent — there is no relationship between what you did on the third request and what you do on the ninetieth, so the policy cannot accumulate any structure across time. A single fixed order does the opposite: because it is consulted the same way every time, it behaves consistently, and consistency turns out to be what produces the corrective effect that makes the whole thing work. A resource that keeps being passed over is a resource ranked low, which means the resources that keep being selected are the ones that were ranked high, and the standing order steadily concentrates commitments where they do least damage. Fresh randomness destroys exactly that accumulation. The authors note explicitly that the naive per-decision version fails on inputs where locally reasonable choices are systematically misleading, which is where the standing order earns its keep.

There is a boundary condition that comes with it, and it defines the whole technique's limits. The hidden commitment is valuable only while it stays hidden and stays committed. This paper is careful about it: against an opponent who watches your decisions unfold and adapts the remaining input in response, no randomized policy of any kind does much better than a deterministic one, because a reactive opponent effectively learns your draw by observing its consequences. So the guarantee rests on the input being fixed in ignorance of your coin flips, not merely on the coin flips existing.

For a programmer the transferable habit is to treat the placement of randomness as a design decision with a right answer, not as a detail. When you find yourself reaching for a random pick per event — a random replica, a random shard, a random victim to evict, a random tiebreak — ask whether a single random ordering established at startup and then followed deterministically would serve better. It usually costs less, it is far easier to reproduce and debug because the entire nondeterminism of the system collapses to one seed you can log, and it lets the policy build up the kind of consistent behavior that per-event coin flips structurally cannot. Then ask the boundary question: can whoever generates my load observe how I am behaving and react? If yes, the standing order is exposed and the guarantee is gone.

**Source:** [An Optimal Algorithm for On-line Bipartite Matching](../works/an-optimal-algorithm-for-on-line-bipartite-matching.md) — the contrast the paper draws between the policy that draws a fresh choice at each arrival and the one whose only random act is fixing a priority order during initialization, together with its earlier construction showing what a reactive opponent does to any randomized policy.
