# When a field is full of rival mechanisms, look for the single relation they are all storing, then treat each mechanism as a layout choice for it

**Lesson:** A mature area of practice tends to accumulate a pile of unrelated mechanisms that were each invented for a specific nuisance — a privileged execution mode, bounds registers, handles for things currently in use, per-container permission lists, a login check. One system will contain several of them at once, with no shared vocabulary, and every discussion of which is better is conducted in the wrong terms. The move that dissolves this is to look for the mathematical object all of them are partial encodings of. Here it is a relation over two sets, contexts on one side and protected things on the other, with a set of permitted operations in each cell, plus one bit per operation saying whether the holder may pass it on. That object is trivial to state. Once stated, every mechanism in the pile becomes an answer to a mechanical question: the relation is enormous and almost entirely empty, so how do you store it?

The answers are the mechanisms. Store it by row and each context carries a list of what it may touch. Store it by column and each thing carries a list of who may touch it. Keep it centrally as a set of triples and you get a lookup table nobody can afford to keep resident. That is the whole of the supposed rivalry between the two dominant styles of access control: they are the same relation stored transposed, and the arguments for each are the ordinary arguments about locality — which direction you need to enumerate, whether entries cluster on one axis, whether the frequently consulted part fits in fast memory, whether the substrate you must live with already imposes one layout. Hardware address translation, in particular, has already chosen row-wise storage for you in the one place you cannot override it, which is a constraint on the design and not an argument about it. Real systems then do the obvious hybrid: consult the column-wise structure once, extract a row-wise token, and use the token thereafter. That is what opening something is.

The generalizing insight is that a cell of the relation need not be data at all. Replace the stored entry with a procedure that receives the requester's identity and computes the answer, and you get arbitrarily complex policy without touching the model — the model was never about policy, only about where the answer comes from. The corresponding discipline is that the substrate should attach meaning to almost nothing: it hands out fresh names and knows which context may hand out further rights, and the interpretation of every other attribute belongs to whichever module implements that kind of thing. This is what lets new kinds of protected things be added later without extending the mechanism.

The uncomfortable observation Lampson makes about his own presentation is the part most worth keeping: he orders his models from most general to least, and finds that generality and complexity run in opposite directions, then declines to say whether that is good news. The abstract model is both the most powerful and the simplest thing in the paper. All the complexity arrives with the representations. A programmer who works this way separates the relation from its layout as a matter of routine, so that changing the layout under pressure from the hardware does not require rethinking what the system means, and so that a debate about mechanisms can be settled by measurement rather than taste.
