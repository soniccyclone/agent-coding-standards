# Spend the expensive agreement on who is allowed to decide, not on each decision — and notice that the cheap path then rests on a physical assumption, not a logical one

**Lesson:** A general agreement protocol is the strongest coordination tool available and the wrong one to invoke per operation. Exclusive access held by one process is enormously cheaper, and its only real defect is that it does not survive the holder dying — the resource stays locked forever with nobody to unlock it. Attaching an expiry to the exclusive right repairs exactly that defect and nothing else: the holder may act unilaterally and at full speed while the right is live, and if it vanishes, the right dissolves on its own without anybody having to detect the failure or run a protocol to reclaim it. So the architectural move is to run the expensive mechanism only to grant and renew authority, and to let the authority holder do the actual work alone. Stack that recursively when even the renewals cost too much: one long-lived, deliberately dull holder of coarse authority, handing out finer authority to workers who renew against it without any global coordination at all.

What this buys is a genuinely different cost curve, and what it charges is a shift in the kind of argument that supports your correctness. Agreement protocols are safe on brutally weak assumptions — arbitrary delays, lost and duplicated messages, processes failing and coming back. Time-bounded authority is not. Its safety rests on bounded clock disagreement between the participants, so the holder must treat its right as expiring earlier than its own clock says, by the width of the possible skew. It also rests on an upper bound on how long a write can take to land, because a write issued while the right was live and still in flight after it expired is precisely the case the whole scheme was supposed to exclude. Real devices are exactly where that bound gets violated: storage with weak ordering guarantees and an unpleasant tail on write latency turns an elegant argument into a race. So the honest reading is that this optimization moves part of your correctness proof out of logic and into the physical properties of clocks and I/O paths — which is fine, as long as you know you did it and can name the numbers.

The remaining knob is the expiry interval, and it is a pure trade with no clever escape: short expiry means renewals are frequent and expensive but a dead holder blocks the resource only briefly; long expiry inverts both. There is no setting that is good on both counts, which is why the hierarchy helps — it lets you pick different points on that trade for authority that changes often and authority that almost never does. Note also that the same mechanism is what makes caching legitimate. Holding a right to a piece of state for a bounded time is what licenses keeping a local copy and trusting it, so cached-and-coherent and leased-and-exclusive are the same idea seen from two sides.

A programmer who thinks this way stops reaching for the heavyweight protocol as a general-purpose safety blanket and asks instead what the smallest thing is that everyone needs to agree on — usually "who is in charge of this right now" — then designs the fast path as unilateral action by that party. They also write down, explicitly, the clock-skew and write-latency bounds their fast path depends on, because those bounds are load-bearing and invisible in the code.
