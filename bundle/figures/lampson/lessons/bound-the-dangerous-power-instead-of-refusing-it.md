# When an outer layer's job is to construct the inner layer's objects, self-defense cannot mean withholding the dangerous power — give it the power over a bounded territory instead

**Lesson:** Layered designs usually assume a clean asymmetry: the inner layer guarantees certain invariants, the outer layer consumes them, and the inner layer defends itself by never letting the outer one touch the representations those invariants live in. That assumption quietly breaks the moment the outer layer's actual job is to move the inner layer's objects somewhere the inner layer cannot see — onto slower storage, across a network, into a snapshot. Reconstruction requires exactly the forbidden powers: minting a reference that the inner layer did not create, installing a reference into an already-existing structure, and pointing at something that does not yet exist. The inner layer can refuse, and then the outer layer simply cannot do its job for those object kinds. Or it can grant the power wholesale, and the invariant it exists to protect evaporates. Faced with that choice, designers usually pick a third option that looks like prudence and is actually the worst of the three: they carve out the objects that can be handled and abandon the rest.

The resolution is to stop treating the power as binary. Fabricating a reference is not intrinsically unsafe; fabricating a reference to *anything at all* is. Partition the space of things that can be named into territories, and let holding authority over a territory be what confers the right to mint names within it. Now the outer layer can synthesize freely inside the region it is responsible for, while the region the inner layer keeps for itself is unreachable because no authority over it is ever handed out. The guarantee that survives is narrower than "no one but me may create references" but it is the guarantee that was actually load-bearing, and the design becomes uniform for the first time. Add the two supporting pieces — an object may exist in a state that carries no content at all, and there is an atomic way to exchange a fully built structure with an empty one — and construction can proceed in visible stages while appearing instantaneous to everyone else.

The transferable habit is diagnostic. When a boundary blocks something the far side legitimately needs, resist both reflexes: don't punch a general hole, and don't accept the partial system. Ask instead which specific property the prohibition was protecting, and whether that property can be restated as a scope rather than an absolute. Very often the dangerous operation was already available in a limited, unrecognized form somewhere else in the interface, which is strong evidence that generalizing it costs nothing in real authority. And notice that this pattern is not about one machine: the same relationship — an inner component that must defend itself against a partner whose job is to serialize, ship, and rebuild the inner component's objects, and whose transmissions may be malformed — is the shape of every system that moves live structures between mutually distrustful nodes.

**Source:** [Reflections on an Operating System Design](../works/reflections-on-an-operating-system-design.md) — the layering section, which shows why the capability-list case cannot be moved to secondary storage without violating the kernel's self-defense requirement, and the closing proposal that reaches a single class of objects via an inactive state, an atomic state exchange, and a region-scoped right to create capabilities; the same section notes the analogy to objects migrating between networked machines.
