# Every shared mechanism is a communication channel, so a component's real interface is everything an observer can measure about its execution — and containment becomes a quantity, not a yes or no

**Lesson:** Ask what a piece of code can communicate and the naive answer lists its outputs. The correct answer is: anything an outside observer can measure about it. That includes mechanisms nobody built for communication at all. A lock that prevents two writers from colliding is a shared bit that one party sets and another tests, which is a wire. The rate at which a computation touches memory, or the ratio of its arithmetic to its transfers, perturbs whatever else runs on the machine, which is another wire — a noisy one, but noise only reduces the rate, and any influence above zero can be coded against reliably. Even an accounting record shared with a third party carries bits. The pattern generalizes past security: whenever you convince yourself two components are independent because you did not connect them, you have reasoned about the declared interface and ignored the ambient one, and the ambient one includes every resource they contend for.

This forces an unwelcome epistemology. You cannot establish a property of the form "this cannot leak" by examining the code's stated behavior, because the surface through which it acts is not the surface it declares. You have to enumerate the ways the surrounding system lets one execution be observed, and block each one — and for any system of realistic complexity there is no rigorous way to know the enumeration is complete. Grouping the channels helps: state the system holds on the component's behalf and lets someone else read; outputs the component legitimately produces that reach a third party; and side effects that were never meant to convey anything. The first group is tractable once you can list every kind of state the substrate actually implements, which is harder than it sounds because the substrate implements state in places nobody calls state. The last group is the one that cannot be closed by inspection.

The consequence worth carrying into other domains is that containment stops being a boolean and becomes a bandwidth. If you cannot eliminate a channel, you can measure it and cap it — restrict the shape of the shared output so it carries a handful of bits instead of a document; inject deliberate jitter and spurious activity so the timing signal is drowned; slow the component to a schedule the observer cannot read information out of. Every one of those costs performance, in direct proportion to how much channel capacity you remove, and the honest engineering position is to price the residual leak and decide whether the customer will accept it. That reframing is more useful than the absolute version, because the absolute version is unachievable and therefore gets abandoned entirely, while the quantitative version can actually be engineered and traded against speed.

A programmer who thinks this way stops asking whether a module has been given access to something and starts asking what an adversary co-resident with it can measure. They treat contention itself as coupling. And they hold their own isolation claims at the confidence level the reasoning supports, which is a plausibility argument over an enumeration they believe to be complete, not a proof.

**Source:** [A Note on the Confinement Problem](../works/a-note-on-the-confinement-problem.md) — the catalogue of leakage examples, particularly the use of a file-locking interlock as a shared boolean and the modulation of system load as an information-theoretic channel, and the subsequent grouping of channels into storage, legitimate, and covert, with the closing observation that bounding channel capacity is the affordable alternative to closing it.
