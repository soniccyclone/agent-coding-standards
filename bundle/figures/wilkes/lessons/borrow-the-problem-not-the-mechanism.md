# Borrow the problem from an existing solution, never the mechanism, when the materials differ

**Lesson:** A working system that already solves your problem is enormously attractive as a template, and copying its mechanism is usually a mistake when it is built from different materials under different constraints. Its structure is not a statement about the problem; it is a statement about what was available to whatever process produced it. Transplant that structure into a substrate with different strengths and you inherit accommodations to constraints you do not have while forgoing the ones you do — which is why the early attempts to fly by imitating the mechanism of a bird went badly, and why the eventual answer looked nothing like the model that inspired it.

The discriminating question is which of the two things you are taking. Taking the *problem* is almost always sound: the existing system is strong evidence that a capability is achievable and often the clearest available statement of what the capability has to be. Taking the *mechanism* is sound only if you have checked that the constraints that shaped it are also yours. So the useful reading of an existing solution goes: what task is this accomplishing, what does it have to get right, and what in its structure is there only because of the material it is made of — and that last category is normally most of it.

The failure mode is easiest to spot in the vocabulary a project uses about itself. When the design's ambition is stated in terms of resembling the exemplar rather than in terms of the task, the borrowing has slid from problem to mechanism, and the project has quietly accepted a much harder objective than the one it needs. The prediction that turns out well is usually the unromantic one: that the eventual solution will resemble other artifacts made of your materials, doing your job, rather than the inspiring thing made of something else.

**Source:** [Computers Then and Now](../works/computers-then-and-now.md) — the design-and-assembly section's argument that the artificial-intelligence approach may be the wrong one for automatic assembly because animals and machines are made from different materials on different principles, illustrated by the history of flapping-wing flying machines, and the prediction that handling machines would resemble machine tools rather than hands.
