# Learn to recognize the efficiency objection that is valid and still loses

**Lesson:** Every proposal to raise the level at which people work draws the same objection, and the objection is normally correct on its own terms: the mediated version consumes more of the machine than the hand-built one, in time and in space, so equivalent work requires a bigger machine. What the arithmetic misses is that the resources being compared are not the scarce ones. The abstraction spends a resource that grows cheaply and predictably in exchange for one that does not grow at all — the number of people able to do the work, and the amount any one of them can attempt. When the exchange runs in that direction, the resource comparison is true and non-decisive, and the growth the abstraction unlocks would have been impossible without it.

The practical value of noticing this is that the argument recurs, structurally identical, at every subsequent level: the same objection, the same valid measurements, the same likely outcome. Having watched one round settle, you can classify the next one quickly by asking which resource each side is counting. If the opponents are counting machine resources and the proponents are counting human ones, the historical record says the human count wins — because machine resources are the thing the industry is organized to make cheaper, and human capacity is not.

Two guards keep this from becoming a licence to waste. First, it applies to abstractions that genuinely convert scarce human effort into abundant machine effort, not to overheads that buy nothing. Second, valid-but-not-decisive still means valid: the cost is real, someone pays it, and the honest form of the argument concedes the measurement and disputes its weight rather than denying it. An advocate who insists the abstraction is also free forfeits the credibility needed to win the point that actually matters.

**Source:** [Computers Then and Now](../works/computers-then-and-now.md) — the account of the early-1950s debate over automatic programming, where the objections about running time and memory consumption are described as valid but not decisive, together with the observation that the same objections were then being raised against time sharing and would likely fare the same way.
