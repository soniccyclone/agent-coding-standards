# A user's overreaction to a demonstration is wrong about the solution and right about the need

**Lesson:** When people outside your field see an early demonstration and conclude that their whole problem is now solved, the builders' instinct is to correct them, and the correction is accurate — the demonstration is nowhere near the capability they are imagining. Dismissing the reaction on those grounds throws away the information it carries. An overreaction of that size is a measurement of how badly the existing means of working were serving them, and it is a better measurement than anything they would have said if asked directly, because it was provoked rather than solicited. Two separate findings are bundled inside it: the current interface is inadequate in their judgment, and the particular mode they responded to is central to how they think about their work.

The second finding is the one with teeth, because it forecloses an option. If a mode of expression is how a discipline actually reasons — not a preference but the medium its practitioners think in — then the alternative to supporting it is not asking them to adapt; it is producing a future generation of practitioners conditioned to think differently, which is not a project anyone can undertake. Recognizing which user habits are of that kind, and which are mere familiarity that would yield to a better tool, is one of the higher-value judgments in interface design, and the intensity of an unprompted reaction is among the few available signals.

So the productive response to enthusiasm you know to be premature has two halves held at once. Be honest that the work required is large and mostly not done, since believing the enthusiasm is how a field commits to shipping demonstrations. And take the reaction seriously as evidence about requirements, since discounting it is how a field spends a decade improving something its users were telling it was the wrong thing.

**Source:** [Computers Then and Now](../works/computers-then-and-now.md) — the design-and-assembly section's account of mechanical engineers concluding from early graphical displays that computer-aided design was solved, and the two things the author draws from that reaction: that conventional means of communicating with a computer were inadequate in those engineers' judgment, and that graphical communication is essential to how engineering is practised, leaving no alternative to providing it.
