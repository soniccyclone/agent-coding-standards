# Expect a long, demoralizing gap between demonstrating a principle and exploiting the technology behind it

**Lesson:** A first working instance of a new principle is a laboratory artifact. It exists to show that the principle holds, which means it is deliberately not engineered to the limit of what the available technology could support, and it therefore cannot demonstrate the payoff the principle promises. Getting from there to something fully engineered takes far longer than anyone estimates, and the interval has a characteristic emotional shape: the demonstrations stop being impressive, the promised benefits have not landed, and the questioning turns into recrimination against the principle itself. Knowing that the interval exists, and that its unpleasantness is a normal feature of it, is the difference between reading the trough as evidence of a mistake and reading it as evidence of position.

The reason the estimate is always wrong is structural, not a failure of estimation discipline. What makes the first instance quick is exactly what makes it unrepresentative: it skips the work of exploiting the technology, and that skipped work is most of the remaining effort. It follows that the same lag should be expected every time the pattern recurs at a new level, and that the correct inference during the trough is about *time* rather than about *validity*. A principle vindicated in retrospect looks in retrospect like a short delay; only from inside does it look like failure.

Two things follow for anyone in the middle of one. Do not let the trough be settled by argument, because the arguments available during it are the ones about resource cost, and those are the arguments that were valid and non-decisive in every previous round. And keep the distinction visible between what has been demonstrated and what has been engineered, so that a system's shortcomings can be attributed to the right one — a laboratory instance failing to deliver the benefit says nothing about the principle, and treating it as though it did is how a field talks itself out of something it later takes for granted.

**Source:** [Computers Then and Now](../works/computers-then-and-now.md) — the pioneering-days account of the difficult early-1950s period, where the first stored-program machines were laboratory models that did not exploit the technology of the time and the wait for fully engineered ones brought heart searching and recrimination, together with the explicit application of the same pattern to the state of time sharing at the time of writing.
