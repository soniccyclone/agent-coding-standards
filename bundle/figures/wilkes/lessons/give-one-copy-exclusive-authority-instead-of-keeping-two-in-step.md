# Give one copy exclusive authority for a bounded interval instead of keeping two copies in step

**Lesson:** Two copies of the same datum only create a consistency problem if both are permitted to answer. Grant one of them sole authority for a defined stretch of time and the problem vanishes inside that stretch — not because the copies are being kept in agreement, but because there is no longer anything for agreement to mean. The other copy is stale by design and nobody is allowed to read it. What survives is two events per interval: taking authority and giving it back. That is a strictly better shape than mirroring every update to both places, and the improvement is not about instruction counts. Mirroring spreads a small permanent obligation across every write in the system, and its correctness rests on the claim that no path anywhere ever forgets. Exclusive authority collects the entire obligation into two named moments, and the steady state in between carries no invariant spanning the two copies at all — which means there is no invariant to violate, and no argument to make about interleavings.

The move requires two things, and both are worth checking before you commit. First, an interval with recognizable ends. If the surrounding system already alternates among claimants — a scheduler activating one tenant at a time, a lease, a request lifetime, a partition assignment — the boundaries are handed to you and cost nothing to detect. Where no such alternation exists you would have to invent and enforce one, and if the intervals come out short relative to the handoff cost, or refuse to be made non-overlapping, you are genuinely stuck synchronizing and should stop trying. Second, and more often botched, the authority has to be unbypassable while held. Every access must be forced through the current holder, including the ones that would find it convenient to consult the slow copy directly, and including the reads — a reader that skips the holder gets a stale answer with no indication that it did. One bypass route leaves you paying for both designs and holding the guarantee of neither.

The reason to reach for this deliberately rather than stumble into it is that mirroring is what you get by default. Adding a fast copy beside a slow one and dutifully updating both is the obvious construction, and it looks conservative, so it rarely gets challenged. The question that dislodges it is not "how do I keep these in sync" but "for how long can exactly one of these be the truth, and what event marks each end of that period." The same reframing turns write-through into write-back, shared reads into exclusive ownership, distributed locking into leases, defensive copying into a transfer of the value, and a document edited concurrently into one checked out and returned.
