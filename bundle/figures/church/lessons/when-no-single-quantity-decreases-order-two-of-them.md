# When no single quantity decreases at every step, rank the steps by two quantities in order — and the real work is defining the tie-breaker precisely enough to actually fall

Church's procedure for hoisting quantifiers to the front of a formula is a rewrite system: six rules, each rewriting one part of the formula, applied until none applies. Showing that it always halts is a real obligation, since several of the rules make the formula longer. The obvious quantity to watch is the number of misplaced quantifiers, and it does not work — some steps leave it exactly where it was, moving a quantifier outward through one enclosing connective without eliminating anything.

His argument is a two-level ranking. Every step either reduces the count of misplaced quantifiers, or, holding that count fixed, reduces a second count: the occurrences of certain connectives sitting outside the first misplaced quantifier. Both counts are finite to begin with, so the pair cannot descend forever, and the process halts. The second count is the interesting part, because it is not a natural quantity anyone would name in advance. Church has to define "outside" with a specific carve-out — the symbols that make up the abbreviated existential quantifier are not counted, even though they are of the right kind and are positioned correctly. Without that exclusion the count fails to decrease on some steps and the argument collapses. The whole proof lives in that definition.

The general shape is worth internalizing because it is the standard escape from a stuck termination argument. A single measure that must strictly decrease is a strong demand, and most real processes have steps that do nothing to the headline quantity while clearly making progress in some other sense. The move is not to abandon the argument or to bolt on an iteration cap. It is to name what the stalling steps do accomplish, put it second, and order the pair so that the second only has to fall when the first stands still. Nothing prevents the second measure from being reset upward whenever the first drops, which is exactly why the ordering is the right structure and a sum or product is not.

This is what separates a loop you can reason about from one you hope about. A reconciliation controller that retries until reality matches intent needs a measure, and the usual bug is that some transition leaves the drift count unchanged and the loop oscillates between two states forever. A dependency resolver that backtracks needs the pair of "constraints still unsatisfied" and "candidates remaining at the current decision point." An incremental build that re-runs rules until fixpoint needs to know a rule cannot re-dirty something already clean. A negotiation or backoff protocol needs some strictly shrinking budget or the two parties can trade the same message indefinitely. In every case the useful question when a step makes no headline progress is not "is this rare enough to ignore" but "what did it actually decrease," and if the honest answer is nothing, the process does not terminate and you have found the bug rather than a proof gap.

The practice, then: when you write anything that repeats until a condition holds, write down the measure at the same time. If one number will not do, take two and order them. And expect to spend the effort not on the ranking but on defining the components sharply enough that they truly fall — Church's careful exclusion of the symbols that only look like they belong to the count is the entire content of his proof, and the analogous fiddly clause in your own measure is where you will discover the case your loop does not handle.

**Source:** [Introduction to Mathematical Logic](../works/introduction-to-mathematical-logic.md) — the termination argument for the metatheorem that repeated application of the six reduction steps brings all quantifiers of a formula to initial position: each step either diminishes the number of quantifier occurrences that are not initially placed, or, leaving that number unchanged, diminishes the number of occurrences of implication and negation external to the first such quantifier, where "external" is given a definition that specifically excludes the negation signs forming part of an abbreviated existential quantifier.
