# Assuming something about a parameter revokes your right to instantiate it, and the revocation has to be enforced at the rule, not remembered by the reasoner

Church's propositional system has a rule permitting substitution of any formula for any variable in a theorem. The rule is unrestricted and sound, because a theorem holds for every value of its variables — that is what having no hypotheses means. Then he defines the richer notion of a derivation from hypotheses, and the same rule reappears with a condition welded on: you may substitute only for variables that do not occur in the hypotheses. He does not leave this as a caution. He builds it into clause four of the definition, so a sequence that violates it simply is not a derivation, and he immediately exhibits the failure it prevents — a formula you can obtain from another by substitution, which nevertheless is not derivable from it as a hypothesis.

The reason is short and worth having in the front of your mind. A variable in a theorem is genuinely arbitrary; you are entitled to read it as standing for anything, so replacing it with anything preserves truth. The instant you write that variable into a hypothesis, you have stopped talking about all its values and started talking about the particular unknown one that makes the hypothesis hold. It is the same symbol on the page and a different logical object. Substituting into it is not a slightly risky step, it is a change of subject: you swap out the thing your assumption was about while keeping the assumption. Everything downstream is then about a value nobody assumed anything about.

The other half of the lesson is where the condition lives. Church could have written the substitution rule once, unconditionally, and told the reader to be careful under hypotheses. He does not, and the payoff is visible two pages later in the proof of the deduction theorem: one of the five cases in that proof is discharged in three lines with the remark that the restriction is exactly what makes it go through. A side condition that is part of the rule is a side condition the metatheory can lean on. A side condition that is part of the surrounding advice is one every proof has to re-establish for itself, which in practice means nobody does. There is a related tell in his proof that adding hypotheses cannot destroy derivability: the statement is obvious, the proof is not, and the entire difficulty is that the newly added hypotheses may mention variables the old derivation was busy substituting for. He repairs it by renaming. The obvious monotonicity result costs real work precisely because the freezing is real.

For an engineer the pattern shows up wherever generality and capture meet. A polymorphic type variable can be instantiated at will until it is bound by an enclosing signature that some other part of the code has already constrained; then instantiating it is unsound, and the languages that get this right encode it as a scoping rule rather than as documentation. The same shape appears in a symbolic solver, where a fresh variable can be given any value until a constraint mentions it; in a query planner, where a parameter is substitutable until a filter has already been pushed through it; in a test suite, where a helper written to work for every input stops being reusable at the moment the fixture pins one of its arguments; in configuration, where a value is a knob until something else has been derived under the assumption that it is a specific number. In each case the object that changed is not the code but the quantifier — a thing that was universally quantified silently became a fixed unknown, and the operations licensed by universal quantification silently stopped being licensed.

The practice this argues for is to make the freezing structural. When you introduce an assumption, identify the symbols it captures and arrange for the machinery — the type checker, the scoping rules, the API, the naming convention — to refuse the operations that only made sense while those symbols were arbitrary. Where the tooling cannot do it, at least write the assumption's captured symbols down beside it. What must not happen is the situation where the same substitution operation is available in both regimes, is correct in one, and is prevented from being applied in the other only by the reasoner's memory that a hypothesis is in force somewhere above.

**Source:** [Introduction to Mathematical Logic](../works/introduction-to-mathematical-logic.md) — the section on the deduction theorem, specifically clause (4) of the definition of a proof from hypotheses, which permits inference by substitution only for a variable not occurring in any of the hypotheses, Church's accompanying counterexample of a formula obtainable by substitution but not derivable from the corresponding hypothesis, the use of exactly this restriction to discharge Case 4 of the proof of the deduction theorem, and the renaming argument required to prove that additional hypotheses preserve derivability.
