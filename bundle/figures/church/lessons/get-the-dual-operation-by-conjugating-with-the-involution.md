# When two problems are mirror images under some self-inverse operation, do not write the second procedure — sandwich the first between two applications of the mirror

Church has built an elaborate reduction that puts a formula into a shape with all its existential quantifiers in front, and proved it preserves the property he cares about. He then needs the opposite shape — all universals in front — for the opposite property. He does not build a second reduction. He defines the new normal form as: negate, apply the existing reduction, negate again, and tidy the result back into prefix form. Two properties, validity and satisfiability, are each other's mirror image under negation; his reduction handles one; conjugating it by negation handles the other. The proof of the new metatheorem is correspondingly a four-step citation of results he already had, and no new machinery appears anywhere.

The pattern is worth naming because it is available far more often than it is taken. If you have an operation that solves one problem, and some self-inverse transformation carries that problem to a second one, then applying the transformation, the operation, and the transformation again solves the second. You get the mirrored procedure with no new code, no second proof, and — this is the part that pays over time — no possibility of the two implementations drifting apart, because there is only one implementation. Searching from the end of a sequence is searching from the front with the sequence reversed twice. Descending order is ascending order under a flipped comparison. A maximum is a minimum under negation. Decoding is often encoding conjugated by an inverse alphabet. Undo is redo against an inverted operation log. Reverse-mode iteration over a structure is forward iteration over its mirror. In each case the alternative is a parallel implementation that must be separately tested and separately maintained, and that will eventually differ from its twin in some edge case nobody enumerated.

Two conditions carry the whole weight, and both are checkable. The transformation must genuinely be self-inverse on every input, not merely on typical ones — negation of a floating-point number is the classic failure, since it does not round-trip through NaN and does not distinguish the two zeroes, which is exactly why implementing maximum as negated minimum produces a function that is subtly wrong at the boundary. And the correspondence between the two problems under the transformation must be exact rather than approximate: it is not enough that the mirrored problem is "basically" the original, because the conjugation transports every detail including the ones you were not thinking about. Church states both conditions as theorems rather than assuming them, which is why he gets to spend four lines on the derived result.

The habit to build is noticing the shape before writing the second thing. When a new requirement reads as the opposite, reverse, complement, or dual of something already implemented, stop and ask what operation carries one to the other and whether it is its own inverse. If it is, the new requirement costs a wrapper. If it is nearly its own inverse, you have found the boundary case that will break the naive symmetric implementation you were about to write, which is useful to know before rather than after. And if no such operation exists, that absence is telling you the two problems are less related than the vocabulary suggested, which is also worth knowing.
