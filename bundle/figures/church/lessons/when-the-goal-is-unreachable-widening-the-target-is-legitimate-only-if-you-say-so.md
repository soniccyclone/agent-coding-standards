# When a guarantee is provably unreachable, widening what counts as success is a legitimate move and an illegitimate one, and the difference is whether the wider target was what you wanted

The second-order calculus cannot be complete for the semantics it was built to describe. No set of axioms and rules will ever make its theorems coincide with the formulas true under the intended reading — Gödel settled that, and Church says so plainly. There is nevertheless a completeness theorem for the system, due to Henkin, and it is obtained by changing what counts as a model. Alongside the intended interpretations, one admits smaller ones, in which the ranges of the higher variables are restricted classes rather than everything; the ones that keep the axioms valid and the rules sound are kept, the rest discarded. Against that enlarged class of models, every valid formula is a theorem. The unreachable result becomes reachable because the target moved.

Church's handling of this is the part to steal. He does not present the theorem and let the word "completeness" do the work. He states that whether it is a completeness theorem *at all* turns on a question about intention — namely whether, when the calculus was formulated, the secondary interpretations were meant to be among the things it was about. If they were, the theorem is exactly what it says. If they were not, it is a true theorem about a different subject that happens to share a name with the one you wanted. And he then lists, precisely, what has been lost: for the first-order calculus there is a single interpretation under which theorems and truths coincide, and for the second-order calculus there is no such interpretation at all, so the completeness now holds only against a whole family and never against the reading you care about.

The general move is one of the most common in engineering and one of the least often examined. A guarantee turns out to be unattainable, so the guarantee is redefined until it is attained. Strong consistency is unavailable across partitions, so the system promises eventual consistency. Full coverage is impractical, so coverage is measured over a subset of files. The build cannot be made green, so the persistently failing tests are marked as expected failures. A latency target is missed, so the target becomes a percentile. Every one of these can be entirely correct — the weaker property is often the right thing to want, and pretending otherwise produces systems that promise what they cannot deliver. What separates the honest version from the dishonest one is not the weakening. It is whether the new target is stated as a new target, with the old one's unattainability recorded, and with an explicit account of what the weaker guarantee no longer covers.

The failure mode is that the word survives the redefinition. "Complete," "consistent," "covered," "passing," "verified" all continue to be used after their meanings have been narrowed, and everyone downstream keeps consuming the old meaning. The artifact still says the system is complete; nothing in it says complete-with-respect-to-what. Church's discipline is to attach the qualifier permanently — he coins a separate word for the widened notion and never lets the unqualified term drift onto it — and to say out loud that the original goal is not merely unachieved but impossible, so nobody reads the weaker result as a stepping stone toward the stronger one.

The practical procedure: when you find yourself relaxing a criterion to make a result come out, write down three things next to the result — the original criterion, the proof or argument that it cannot be met, and the specific claims the relaxed criterion no longer supports. The first two protect you from someone re-attempting the impossible. The third is what the people relying on the guarantee actually need, and it is the one that never gets written.

**Source:** [Introduction to Mathematical Logic](../works/introduction-to-mathematical-logic.md) — the section on Henkin's completeness theorem for the pure functional calculus of second order, where the class of interpretations is widened beyond the principal ones to admit sound secondary interpretations over restricted domains, and Church's discussion following the theorem: that it has the meaning of a completeness theorem only if the secondary interpretations were intended in formulating the calculus, that no extension by added rules and axioms can make the theorems coincide with the formulas valid under all principal interpretations, and that unlike the first-order case there is no single interpretation relative to which completeness holds.
