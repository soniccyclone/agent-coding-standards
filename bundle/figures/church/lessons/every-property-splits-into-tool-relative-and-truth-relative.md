# Every property you claim splits into a tool-relative version and a truth-relative version, and the implication runs only one way

Church takes the three properties anyone wants of a set of requirements — that it is consistent, that no part of it is redundant, that it settles everything — and shows that each of them is two properties, not one, because there are two things you can mean. One meaning is about derivation: what the machinery at hand can actually produce as a theorem. The other is about interpretation: what holds in every situation that satisfies the requirements at all. He names both versions of all three properties, keeps them systematically apart for the rest of the section, and then records the relationships. Consistency in the interpretive sense is exactly the existence of some situation satisfying the requirements. A part is redundant in the interpretive sense exactly when there is a situation satisfying everything else and violating it. And the implications between the two families run in one direction only: interpretive consistency gives you derivational consistency, interpretive independence gives you derivational independence, and neither converse is offered.

The asymmetry is the point. The interpretive versions are the strong ones and are largely insensitive to which machinery you happen to be using; the derivational versions are weaker and are hostage to it. That means the property you can actually check by running something is the weak one, and a passing check leaves the strong claim open. Church is explicit that in important cases the derivational version of settling everything is simply unattainable, so a system can be perfectly respectable and still fail the property you were hoping to verify. He also reaches for a third and strictest notion for the settling property — that any two situations satisfying the requirements are structurally the same, related by a correspondence that preserves everything the requirements mention — and observes that this one is the only version fully free of dependence on the surrounding machinery.

Engineers work almost exclusively in the derivational column and talk as though they were in the interpretive one. The type checker accepting a program is a fact about the checker; that the program cannot go wrong is a different and larger claim, and the gap between them is where the escape hatches live. No test failing is a fact about the tests. The linter reporting no violation of a convention is not the convention holding. The model checker exhausting the state space it was given is not the system being safe, because the space it was given came from a hand-written abstraction. A constraint the database enforces is enforced; a constraint the application enforces holds only on paths that go through the application. In each pair, the first item is checkable and weak, the second is what you actually care about and is not directly checkable at all, and the discipline is to say which one you have when you report a result.

The interpretive definitions also hand over two techniques worth using directly. To show a requirement set is satisfiable, exhibit something satisfying it — the smallest concrete instance that meets every stated condition, which is far more informative than an argument that no contradiction has been found. To show a requirement is not redundant, build the instance that meets all the others and breaks that one; if you cannot build it, the requirement is following from the rest and can go. And the strictest notion suggests the question worth asking of any specification that is meant to pin something down: are any two things satisfying this necessarily the same, up to renaming? If the answer is no, the specification admits a family of behaviors, and someone downstream is relying on a member of that family rather than on the specification.

**Source:** [Introduction to Mathematical Logic](../works/introduction-to-mathematical-logic.md) — the section on postulate theory, which distinguishes consistency, independence, and completeness as to provability from the corresponding notions as to consequences, states that a postulate set is consistent as to consequences exactly when it has a model and that a postulate is independent as to consequences exactly when the remaining postulates have a model falsifying it, records that each interpretive notion implies its derivational counterpart without the converse, notes that completeness as to provability is unattainable in important cases, and defines categoricalness — all models isomorphic — as the completeness notion independent of the underlying logic.
