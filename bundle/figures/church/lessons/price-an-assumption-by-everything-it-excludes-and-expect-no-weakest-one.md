# Price an assumption by everything it rules out, not by whether it delivers what you wanted, and do not assume a weakest sufficient assumption exists

Church wants an assumption that forces the universe of discourse to be infinite. He frames the choice in exactly the right currency: adding an axiom is not gaining something, it is *restricting* which interpretations remain admissible, so the question about any candidate is what it excludes. He then works an example that shows why the framing matters. One natural candidate — take the Peano postulates and existentially quantify away their constant — does the job. It also does considerably more: it forces the domain to be not merely infinite but countable, throwing out every uncountable interpretation along with the finite ones. Church rejects it on precisely that ground, saying the extra restriction is more than the purpose of such an axiom calls for, and constructs weaker candidates by using only some of those postulates.

The general failure this guards against is evaluating a constraint solely by whether it delivers the property you asked for. A constraint that delivers it plus four things you did not ask for passes that test and is nonetheless the wrong constraint, because the collateral exclusions are invisible at the moment of adoption and expensive later — they are the interpretations, configurations, deployments, or inputs that some future requirement will need and that are now unreachable without unwinding the assumption. The discipline is to ask, of any assumption, not "does this give me what I want" but "what is now impossible that was possible before," and to keep looking until the answer is close to the target and nothing else.

Church then reports something that complicates the search in an instructive way. Order the candidate axioms by strength — one is weaker than another if the second implies it and not conversely — and there is no weakest. Given any assumption that forces infinity, a strictly weaker one that still forces infinity exists. So the goal "find the minimal assumption sufficient for my purpose" is not always well posed; the descending chain need not terminate, and pursuing it forever buys diminishing generality at increasing cost in intelligibility. What replaces the minimality criterion is judgment about purpose, which is what Church actually exercises: he does not claim his chosen axiom is weakest, he argues it excludes about what he intends to exclude and no more.

Both halves transfer. Engineers add assumptions constantly and almost always price them by sufficiency: requiring a specific database version because it has a feature you need, requiring exactly-once delivery when at-least-once plus idempotence would do, requiring a total ordering on records when a partial one suffices, demanding a schema field be non-null when the code needs it non-null only on one path. Each of those is an assumption whose collateral exclusion is real — a deployment that cannot happen, a transport that cannot be swapped in, a data source that cannot be ingested — and none of the exclusions appear at the moment the requirement is written down.

And the no-weakest result is worth internalizing as a stopping rule. There is usually a strictly weaker assumption that still works, so the search for the truly minimal precondition does not converge, and treating minimality as the objective produces designs so general that nobody can state what they assume. The workable criterion is Church's: name the thing you actually mean to exclude, choose an assumption whose exclusions are approximately that set, and record what it costs — rather than either taking the first sufficient assumption or chasing an infimum that may not exist.

**Source:** [Introduction to Mathematical Logic](../works/introduction-to-mathematical-logic.md) — the section on the axiom of infinity, where Church notes that adjoining such an axiom restricts the interpretation to infinite domains, rejects the axiom derived from the Peano postulates because it would further restrict the domain to be enumerably infinite and so is more severe than the purpose requires, constructs weaker alternatives from subsets of those postulates, and reports the result of Mostowski and Trachtenbrot that for any axiom of infinity there exists a strictly weaker one, so that no weakest axiom of infinity exists.
