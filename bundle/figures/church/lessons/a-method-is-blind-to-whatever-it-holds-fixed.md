# A method can only discriminate along the dimension it varies, so a component that survives everything your technique can change needs a different invariant, not a bigger search

To show that an axiom is not redundant, Church uses a technique that works uniformly and mechanically. Invent an alternative valuation — more than two truth-values, an arbitrary designated subset of them counting as "good", arbitrary tables for the connectives — and look for one under which every rule preserves goodness and every axiom but the suspect one is always good. If such a valuation exists, the suspect axiom cannot follow from the others, since everything derivable from them stays good and it does not. The technique has a large search space and Church exercises it: three values, one designated, a different table for each axiom he wants to isolate.

Then he reaches the rule of substitution, and reports that the technique cannot touch it — not that he failed to find a valuation, but that no valuation whatsoever can work, because substitution preserves goodness under *every* assignment of values and tables. The rule is invariant under the entire space the method searches. Increasing the number of truth-values, choosing exotic tables, designating a different subset: none of it can help, because the property being tested is not sensitive to any of those knobs. So he abandons the method and finds a different invariant entirely, one the method never looked at. Without substitution, every proof step either reproduces an axiom or applies modus ponens, and neither can yield a formula longer than the longest axiom; since the system proves arbitrarily long formulas, substitution is doing work. He notes that this length argument keeps working even if you add finitely many more axioms, which the truth-value method's results would not.

The generalizable content is about the relationship between a technique and its blind spot, and the blind spot is always readable off what the technique varies. Property-based testing varies inputs, so it cannot see a defect that manifests identically on all inputs — a wrong constant, a misnamed metric, a permission check that is uniformly absent. Differential testing against a reference implementation cannot find a bug both implementations share, which is precisely the class of bug that comes from a shared misreading of the spec. A static analyzer that reasons one function at a time cannot see that two functions duplicate an invariant. A benchmark suite that varies load cannot detect a cost that scales with nothing. Load testing in one region cannot exhibit a fault that requires two. In each case people respond to the silence by running more of the same: more inputs, more iterations, more traffic. That is exactly the wrong response, because the method is not underpowered, it is orthogonal.

The practice worth building is to ask, before trusting a negative result, what the method holds fixed — and to treat anything invariant under all of it as unexamined rather than as verified. When you find such a thing, the next move is Church's: look for a different quantity that the suspect component does change. He switched from a semantic invariant to a syntactic one, from truth-values to formula length, because length was the thing substitution manifestly affected and modus ponens did not. That switch is the skill. A component that no amount of variation along your current axis can distinguish is telling you which axis it actually lives on, and the useful question is not how to search harder but what quantity it moves.

**Source:** [Introduction to Mathematical Logic](../works/introduction-to-mathematical-logic.md) — the section on independence, which establishes the independence of the axioms and of modus ponens by generalized truth-tables with three truth-values and one designated value, then observes that the rule of substitution is necessarily tautology-preserving for any system of truth-values and truth-tables so that its independence cannot be established by that method at all, and instead argues it from the fact that without substitution no formula longer than the longest axiom is provable — an argument that continues to hold after adjoining any finite number of further axioms.
