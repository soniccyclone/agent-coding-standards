# When minimality and naturalness both matter, build both formulations and a proved bridge, then prove on the small one and work in the large one

Surveying the many ways the propositional calculus had been set up, Church states the tension plainly and then refuses to resolve it as a preference. If the system is still provisional, or if what you care about is tracking which result rests on which assumption, you want naturalness: primitives that mean something, axioms that are individually simple, and never mind how many there are. If what you care about is proving metatheorems, you want the opposite, because every metatheorem proved by induction over the system costs one case per primitive and one per axiom, and a formulation with a single connective and a single axiom collapses that work — even though the resulting primitive is artificial and the axiom is long enough to be unreadable. Both preferences are correct about their own objective and they point in opposite directions.

The way out is not a compromise, and Church names it in the same breath: a metatheorem proved for one formulation can be carried to another by establishing that the formulations are equivalent. This is why he spends a whole section constructing such a bridge between two of his systems rather than picking one and living with it. The bridge is built once. After that the small formulation is where induction happens and the natural formulation is where anyone actually reads or writes, and neither has to be compromised for the other's benefit.

This is the design of every serious language implementation, though it is rarely described as a response to this trade-off. A core calculus with a handful of forms, plus a desugaring from the surface language into it, plus the type soundness and optimization correctness proofs conducted entirely on the core. A compiler's intermediate representation with far fewer node kinds than the source, so that every analysis pass has few cases rather than hundreds. A normalized data model that everything is projected into before any invariant is checked. A protocol reduced to a small set of message types before the state machine is verified. In each case the small form is not the product and is not meant to be pleasant; it exists so that arguments over it are short and exhaustive, and the translation is what makes those arguments apply to the thing people use.

Two failure modes bracket it and both are common. Skipping the small form means every property must be proved, or more often just believed, against the full surface, where the case analysis is large enough that nobody completes it and coverage silently becomes sampling. Skipping the translation — building the minimal core and then asking people to work in it — produces the system whose semantics are beautiful and whose users route around it, and the routing-around is where the unverified behavior accumulates. The load-bearing artifact is the bridge, and it is the piece most likely to be treated as glue: an under-tested desugarer or lowering pass invalidates every proof conducted downstream of it, because the proofs are about the core and the users are running the surface.

The discipline, then, is to notice when you are being asked to choose between few primitives and expressive ones, and to check whether the choice is real. It usually is not. What is real is that you must be explicit about which formulation each claim was established against, and you must be able to say why the claim transfers — which means the translation is itself an object with properties you owe proofs or tests about, not an implementation detail sitting between two things you care about.

**Source:** [Introduction to Mathematical Logic](../works/introduction-to-mathematical-logic.md) — the section on other formulations of the propositional calculus, which argues that a tentative construction, or one concerned with distinguishing what assumptions each theorem rests on, should prefer naturalness of the primitive connectives and simplicity of the individual axioms over reduction in their number, that the contrary course of reducing the number may simplify the proof of desired metatheorems even at the expense of naturalness, and that a metatheorem once proved for one formulation may be extended to others by establishing equivalence of the formulations — the equivalence construction carried out in the earlier section relating the two systems.
