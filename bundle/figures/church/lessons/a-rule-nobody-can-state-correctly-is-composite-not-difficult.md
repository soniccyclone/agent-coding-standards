# A rule that repeated expert attempts fail to state correctly is composite, not merely hard, and the right primitive decomposes it into pieces that are each obviously right

Church assembles the record on one rule — substituting an expression for a functional variable — and it is a catalogue of failure by people who were not careless. The first published statement, in the standard textbook of the field, was inadequate. Two later statements by two of the most exacting writers in logic were better and still not fully correct. The first correct one arrived six years after the first attempt, and Church could not use it, because it was correct only by leaning on a formation restriction he was unwilling to accept. Elsewhere he notes that a full statement of the analogous rule for a system with binding operators was included in a famous set of lecture notes only because a student suggested it — and that even that statement was not quite right and needed a condition added. Church's own version is a splice of two of the incorrect ones.

He then makes the observation that turns this from anecdote into method. In a system that has an abstraction operator, this rule need not be a primitive at all. It can be replaced by several simpler primitive rules that, taken together, constitute an *analysis* of it. And he draws the conclusion out loud: the repeated difficulty of stating the rule is itself a reason to prefer the systems that have the operator. He is careful to note the countervailing consideration — the calculi without it have been studied more and enjoy an economy of primitives — so the judgment is a trade, not a slogan. But the diagnostic stands: chronic failure to state a rule correctly is evidence about the rule's structure.

The mechanism is not mysterious. A composite rule that has never been factored has to do several unrelated jobs in one statement, and its side conditions are the interference between those jobs written out as special cases. Nobody can enumerate all the interferences, because the enumeration is a proxy for a decomposition nobody has performed. Introduce the missing concept — here, a way to name a function without applying it, which separates forming an abstraction from substituting into one from renaming inside one — and each job becomes its own rule with its own obvious condition. The rules multiply and the difficulty vanishes, which is exactly the trade that a preference for fewest primitives will get wrong if applied blindly.

The engineering signature is recognizable once you know to look for it. A function whose documentation is mostly caveats, whose parameter combinations are half illegal, and whose behavior nobody can state without saying "except when." A configuration key that means different things depending on three other keys. A process step that every senior engineer describes slightly differently and everyone's description is slightly wrong. The standard response is to write more precise documentation, add validation for the illegal combinations, and treat the difficulty as inherent complexity in the domain. That response can be right, and it is wrong often enough to be worth testing: the alternative hypothesis is that the thing is doing two jobs, and that naming the concept it lacks would split it into pieces each of which needs no caveats.

The test is cheap and specific. Take the side conditions and ask, of each one, which job it is protecting. If they partition — some guarding one concern, some another, few or none guarding the interaction — you have found the seam and the decomposition will work. If every condition is about the interaction, the complexity may really be irreducible. And Church's honesty about the cost is part of the lesson: factoring buys correctness and legibility, and it is paid for in primitive count and in departing from what everyone else already knows. Both sides of that go in the ledger.

**Source:** [Introduction to Mathematical Logic](../works/introduction-to-mathematical-logic.md) — the historical notes to the chapter on the pure functional calculus of first order and their footnote on the rule of substitution for functional variables, tracing the inadequate statement in Hilbert and Ackermann, the not-fully-correct statements by Carnap and by Quine, the first correct statement by Hilbert and Bernays, the still-imperfect statement in Gödel's lecture notes, the greater difficulty of stating the rule for systems with abstraction or description operators, and Church's remark that in systems having the abstraction operator the rule can be replaced by simpler primitive rules constituting an analysis of it — grounds, he says, for preferring such systems, against which the operator-free calculi have an economy of primitives in their favour.
