# Define an operation by its value in every case; the word it is read aloud as is a mnemonic that will import promises the definition never made

When Church lays out the two-operand sentence connectives he gives each one three things: the sign he will write, a suggested way to say it out loud, and the complete assignment of a result to every combination of operand values. Only the third is the definition. He states outright that the spoken readings are supplied for convenience and are at best a rough approximation, and that they must not be taken as evidence that the ordinary meaning of the English word is faithfully reproduced by the connective it is attached to. The exhaustive case-by-case assignment is what fixes the operation's character, and it is where the meaning is to be learned with accuracy. Everything else is a handle for humans.

He then demonstrates the gap instead of asserting it, with a set of conditionals whose parts have no relation to one another at all. Under the definition he has given, three of them come out true and one false, and settling which is which requires knowing nothing whatever about the subject matter of the unrelated halves. The move that makes this pedagogy rather than provocation is his handling of the reader who balks: if you find yourself wanting to deny one of these on the grounds that its two halves have nothing to do with each other, that reaction is not an objection to the definition — it is evidence that you are reasoning from a different notion of the word than the one defined. Discomfort locates which definition someone is actually using. It does not adjudicate between them.

The companion move is what he does about the notions he cannot capture. The everyday word has uses that appear to relate the meanings of statements rather than their truth or falsity, and he says plainly that these are hard to pin down and that he is not going to try. So he selects the single use that his machinery can render exactly, names it as a restricted use, and formalizes that. This is the honest form of formalization: take the slice of the informal idea you can make precise, announce which slice it is, and do not let the shared word imply that the rest came along. The alternative — claiming the formal operation captures the natural-language concept — is how a specification acquires obligations nobody wrote down.

Programmers live inside this gap continuously and mostly unknowingly. An equality operator that coerces its arguments, an addition sign that concatenates, a comparison against an absent value that is neither true nor false, a queue described as ordered that is only mostly ordered, a verb documented as idempotent by a service that retries into duplicates, a call named delete that hides a row rather than removing it — in every case the name is doing semantic work the implementation never agreed to, and the disagreement surfaces as a bug filed against behavior that matches its own specification exactly. Two habits follow. Write the definition as coverage of the whole input space, including the cases you consider degenerate, because those are the cases where the name and the behavior diverge and where callers will trust the name. And when someone calls a behavior surprising, treat the surprise as a report about which meaning they inferred from the name, then fix whichever of the two is wrong — sometimes the definition, often the name.
