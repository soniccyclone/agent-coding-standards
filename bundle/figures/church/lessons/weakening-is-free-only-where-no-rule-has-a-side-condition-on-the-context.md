# Adding to a context is only free where no rule has a side condition on the context, and where one does, the repair is to rename before you widen

Church defines what it means to derive a conclusion from a list of hypotheses, and two of the rules available inside such a derivation carry a restriction that mentions the hypotheses themselves: you may generalize on a variable only if that variable is not free in any hypothesis, and you may substitute for a variable only under the same condition. Then he states what looks like a triviality — if a conclusion follows from one list of hypotheses, it follows from any list containing all of them. In most settings that is not worth stating. Here it needs a proof, and the proof is not short. The reason is exactly the side conditions. A derivation built against a small hypothesis list may have generalized on a variable that was free in none of those hypotheses but is free in one of the new ones. Nothing about the derivation changed; the surrounding list changed, and a step that was legal has become illegal.

The repair Church constructs is instructive because it is mechanical and it is the one you would reach for anyway. Collect exactly the variables that are free in the enlarged list but were not free in the original. Choose fresh replacements of matching kind that occur nowhere in the enlarged list or in the given derivation. Rewrite the derivation with those replacements throughout. Now no step's side condition can be tripped by the new hypotheses, because the derivation no longer mentions anything they mention. A short cleanup at the end recovers the intended conclusion. He also remarks that if you actually want to use this as a labor-saving rule rather than admire it as a fact, you have to pin down how the fresh variables get chosen, since a construction that says "pick something unused" is not yet a procedure.

The transferable point is that monotonicity of a context is a property to be checked, not a background assumption. Whenever a rule's applicability is stated in terms of "and X does not appear anywhere in the environment," the environment is an input to that rule, and every enlargement of it is a potential invalidation of work already done. This is the shape of a large family of real defects. A test that passes in isolation and fails in the suite because it asserted on a global nobody else was touching yet. A macro that was hygienic against the identifiers in scope at the time it was written. A generated wrapper whose parameter names were safe until the schema added a field with the same name. A build that worked until a new dependency put a same-named symbol on the link path. A refactor that hoists a helper into a broader scope, where a name that was locally unique now shadows. In each of these, nothing in the artifact changed and the artifact stopped being correct, because its correctness was always conditional on what else was present.

Two habits follow. First, when you write a rule, notice whether its precondition quantifies over the ambient context, and if it does, say so loudly, because you have just made every future addition to that context a breaking change for anyone who relied on the rule. Preconditions that mention only the rule's own inputs cost nothing later; preconditions that mention the world cost forever. Second, when you must widen a context around existing work, do the renaming first rather than testing whether you got away with it. Making a collision impossible by construction is cheap and total; checking for collisions is neither, since it must be redone on every subsequent addition. The general engineering form of Church's move is that freshness bought up front beats collision detection performed repeatedly, and the moment you find yourself asking whether the new thing conflicts with the old thing, you have already accepted the worse of the two.

**Source:** [Introduction to Mathematical Logic](../works/introduction-to-mathematical-logic.md) — the definition of proof from hypotheses in the first-order functional calculus, whose generalization and substitution clauses require that the variable involved not occur free in any hypothesis; and the metatheorem that a deduction from one list of hypotheses yields a deduction from any list containing them, proved by replacing the variables free in the larger list but not the smaller with fresh variables of matching type throughout the given deduction, with the accompanying remark that the choice of fresh variables must be made fully determinate before the metatheorem can serve as a derived rule.
