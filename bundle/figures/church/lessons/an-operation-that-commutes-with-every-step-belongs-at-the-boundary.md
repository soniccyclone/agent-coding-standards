# An operation that commutes with every step can be hoisted to the boundary and deleted from the middle, and checking whether it commutes is the whole design question

Church presents a formulation of the propositional calculus with only one rule of inference, having dropped substitution entirely. What replaces it is a change in how the axioms are given: instead of three specific formulas, three patterns, every instance of which is an axiom. He then proves the two systems have exactly the same theorems, and the proof is the interesting object. Take any derivation and substitute throughout — in every line at once. Every line that was an axiom instance is still an axiom instance, because the axioms are patterns and substituting into a pattern instance yields another. Every line obtained by the one remaining rule is still obtained by that rule, because substituting into an implication gives the implication of the substitutions. So the substituted derivation is a derivation. Substitution never needed to be a step; it could always be pushed outward through the whole proof, which means it could be absorbed into how the leaves are chosen and removed from the interior. He states the sharper form as a separate result: any proof in the original system can be effectively rewritten so that substitution is applied only to axioms.

The reusable content is the criterion, not the conclusion. An operation can be moved out of the middle of a process exactly when it commutes with every step of that process, and the check is finite: for each rule, does applying the operation to the inputs and then the rule agree with applying the rule and then the operation. Two of those checks were all Church needed. When they pass, the operation stops being something the process does and becomes something the process's inputs already satisfy — one fewer rule, one fewer thing to reason about at every point, and every subsequent metatheorem gets a smaller case analysis.

This is one of the most reliably profitable moves in engineering and it is usually made by instinct without the check. Normalizing input at the boundary rather than at every use — case folding, unicode normalization, path canonicalization, timezone conversion — is exactly this hoisting, and it works only if every operation downstream commutes with the normalization. Parsing into a type at the edge so nothing downstream re-validates. Predicate pushdown in a query planner, which is the same identity read the other direction. Resolving configuration once at startup instead of consulting it in every function. Currying dependencies into a constructor so the call sites stop passing them. In each case the payoff is the same: an operation that appeared at every step disappears into the shape of the inputs.

And the failures are all failures of the commuting check, which is why the check deserves to be explicit. Normalize a string at the boundary and then let one downstream operation compare against a raw literal, and the hoisting is invalid for that path. Push a filter below a join that is not equivalence-preserving for outer joins, and you have changed the answer. Resolve config at startup when one consumer expects to see live edits, and the hoist silently freezes behavior. The pattern in every case is that some step did not commute, nobody enumerated the steps, and the transformation was justified by analogy with the steps that did.

So the practice is to write down the steps, check the commuting square for each, and then hoist — and where a step fails the check, treat that as the finding rather than the obstacle. A step that will not let a normalization pass through it is telling you something specific about itself, usually that it is reaching around the abstraction the normalization defines. Fixing that step is generally the better change, and it is the one that lets the whole operation move to the boundary and stop being anybody's concern.

**Source:** [Introduction to Mathematical Logic](../works/introduction-to-mathematical-logic.md) — the section on formulations employing axiom schemata, which presents a system whose axioms are given by three schemata and whose only rule of inference is modus ponens, and proves it has the same theorems as the system with specific axioms plus a substitution rule by observing that substituting throughout every formula of a proof again yields a proof, since the result of substituting in an axiom is again an axiom and substitution distributes over the implication used by modus ponens; together with the corollary that there is an effective process transforming any proof into one where substitution is applied only to axioms.
