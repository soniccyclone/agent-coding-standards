# A mapping that only preserves the answer is free by counting, so the content of an equivalence lives entirely in the structural conditions you also demand

Church spends a section establishing that two formulations of the propositional calculus are equivalent. One has negation as a primitive; the other has a falsehood constant instead. He builds a map from every formula of the first into a representative in the second, proves that a formula is a theorem exactly when its representative is, and states the equivalence in those terms. Then, in a footnote, he takes most of it back. A correspondence carrying theorems to theorems and non-theorems to non-theorems exists between *any* two systems whose theorems and non-theorems are each denumerably infinite — you can even make it one-to-one — purely because the four sets can be put in correspondence by counting. So the existence of such a map is not evidence of anything. What makes his particular map informative is a second set of conditions he states separately: the representative of an implication is the implication of the representatives, and the representative of a negation is built from the representative of what was negated. The map commutes with the constructors. That is where all the content is, and he says so, adding that what equivalence of two systems ought to mean in general is a question he is postponing.

The pattern to extract is that a correspondence proved only on an outcome predicate is almost never the claim someone thinks they are making. If the two sides have the same cardinality and the predicate partitions each into pieces of matching size, a correspondence exists automatically and tells you nothing about either system. It certainly does not tell you the systems are interchangeable, because it need not respect anything you can compute with: two formulas that are built the same way can land arbitrarily far apart, and the map may be uncomputable, or computable and useless. An equivalence is worth having exactly to the extent that it commutes with the operations you care about, and the operations have to be named.

This matters in engineering because equivalence claims are made constantly and almost always in outcome form. "The new pipeline produces the same results on our corpus" partitions inputs into matching accepted and rejected sets and says nothing about whether the two agree on inputs you have not tried — which is the entire question, and which structural agreement would answer and outcome agreement never will. "Our type maps one-to-one onto theirs" is a statement about value sets that says nothing about whether their composition operator agrees with yours, which is what breaks when someone nests one inside another. "This encoding round-trips" is checked on samples and is meaningful only if the encoder commutes with concatenation, with the empty case, with nesting. Two implementations of a set can agree on membership for every element and disagree about union. A shadow deployment showing matching outputs establishes an outcome correspondence over the traffic you happened to receive, which is the weakest thing in this family.

The upgrade is always the same move and it is usually available. State the equivalence as a small number of laws relating the map to the constructors: the image of a composite is the composite of the images, the image of the identity is the identity, the image of the empty case is the empty case. Those laws are finitely many, each is checkable, and together they give agreement on every expression buildable from the pieces — infinitely many cases from finitely much work — which is exactly what sampling outputs cannot do. And when you cannot state such laws, notice what you have: a claim that two things happen to agree pointwise on what you examined, which cardinality alone would have predicted, and which will hold until the first input nobody tried.

**Source:** [Introduction to Mathematical Logic](../works/introduction-to-mathematical-logic.md) — the section relating the two formulations of the propositional calculus, which establishes a many-one correspondence between their formulas carrying theorems to theorems and non-theorems to non-theorems, together with its footnote observing that such a correspondence, even a one-to-one one, exists trivially between any two systems whose theorems and non-theorems are each denumerably infinite, so that its bare existence could hardly constitute a significant equivalence without added conditions such as the stated structural ones on how the representative of an implication and of a negation are formed.
