# Evidence gathered in a setting that already assumes the claim would persuade nobody who actually doubted it, and knowing that is the difference between verification and a consistency check on your own beliefs

The standard way to show that a set of requirements is not self-defeating is to produce something satisfying them, and Church acknowledges the method as well known. Then he refuses to let it pass unqualified in the case that matters. You can indeed demonstrate that the arithmetic postulates are consistent by exhibiting the natural numbers as a thing satisfying them, and he says plainly what such a demonstration is worth: nothing at all to someone who genuinely doubted whether ordinary arithmetic is consistent. The reason he gives is precise and general. Establishing that the exhibited thing exists requires reasoning in a setting where equivalents of the very postulates in question, and of their underlying logic, are already available. The apparatus doing the verifying presupposes what is being verified. He adds a second, quieter verdict: even if your aim is only the smaller one of checking that you formalized correctly a theory you already accept informally, the argument still accomplishes relatively little.

That is a general criterion for evidence and it is worth stating as one. An argument is only as independent as the machinery it is conducted in. If the verifying apparatus contains, or is built from, the thing under test, then the argument establishes coherence between your assumptions and themselves and cannot establish anything about the world. What such an argument does establish — and Church allows it this much — is that you did not make a clerical error while transcribing a belief you already held. That is a real if modest result, and calling it by its right name is what keeps it from being mistaken for validation.

The pattern is endemic in software and usually invisible from inside. A specification checked against the implementation it was reverse-engineered from confirms only the transcription. A parser validated against test cases produced by that parser's own output confirms nothing about the format. A schema migration verified by querying through the same object-relational layer whose assumptions the migration encodes leaves the encoding untested. A model checker run over an abstraction extracted by the code under analysis inherits every mistake in the extraction. Monitoring hosted on the system it monitors reports the system healthy for exactly as long as it can report at all. A simulation whose physics come from the same model the controller was tuned against will show the controller working. A benchmark harness sharing a caching layer with the code it measures measures the cache. In each case the check passes, and the passing carries the information Church allows it: probably no transcription slip.

So the question to ask of any verification is what would have had to be true for it to fail, and whether that thing is independent of the claim. Independent evidence usually costs something specific and identifiable — a second implementation by someone who did not read the first, a property-based generator that does not share code with the parser, an out-of-band health check on separate infrastructure, a reference derived from the requirements rather than from the artifact, a hostile reviewer who is not the author. When independence is unavailable, the honest move is not to strengthen the wording. It is to report what the check actually covered, which is generally the internal consistency of a set of decisions all made by the same mind, and to let that stand alongside the residual doubt rather than in place of it.

**Source:** [Introduction to Mathematical Logic](../works/introduction-to-mathematical-logic.md) — the section on postulate theory, and its qualification of the model-exhibiting consistency proof: for the arithmetic postulates such a proof is possible but of doubtful significance, because establishing the model's existence requires a metalanguage already containing equivalents of the postulates and their underlying logic, so the argument would carry no weight for anyone with real doubts, and even as a check on correct formalization of an informally accepted theory accomplishes relatively little.
