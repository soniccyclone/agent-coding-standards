# Letting a term stand for itself is safe only when the two readings provably cannot collide, and a quoting mark is not a function from values to names

To talk about a formal language Church needs a second language in which to do the talking, and the immediate problem is how the second language names the first one's symbols. He walks through the ordinary situation first: everyday speech routinely uses a word as a name of that very word, and the practice is equivocal — the same string does duty as a designation of a kind of thing and as a designation of itself — yet the equivocation is usually convenient and harmless, and where it is not, extra words or marks disambiguate. He borrows the use-versus-mention vocabulary to make the three cases explicit, including the odd middle case where a word is simultaneously used and mentioned. Then he states his own convention and, crucially, its precondition: the symbols of the studied language will serve as their own names in the language doing the studying, and joining them will mean joining them — permitted only on the condition that no formula of the studied language is also a formula of the studying language in any other capacity. He then closes the remaining hole by forbidding himself a practice he grants would be convenient, that of borrowing formulas from the object language into the metalanguage with their object-language meaning intact.

That is the whole discipline, and it is worth extracting from the logic. A layer may embed another layer's text directly, with no escaping and no delimiters, exactly when the two possible readings of any given string cannot both be available. The condition is a non-collision property of the two grammars, and it has to be established, not assumed. When it cannot be established, you need an explicit mark separating mention from use — and Church catalogues, unsympathetically, how such marks go wrong. They get treated as though they denoted a mapping from things to names of those things, which is not what they are. And a variable enclosed in them yields not a variable of any kind, not even an expression with a hole, but a constant. That is the single most common error in metaprogramming, made by people who would never state it in those words.

Every escaping catastrophe a programmer has met is a violation of Church's precondition. A query assembled by pasting text into text, a shell command built from a filename, a template that renders user-supplied markup, a log line that carries a request body, a serialization format where a bare word is also a boolean, a format string taken from configuration: in each case one layer has embedded another layer's text without the guarantee that only one reading is possible, and the attack or the bug is simply the second reading arriving. Notice the shape of the correct fix, which is also Church's: not more careful escaping applied at each site, but a construction in which the embedded material cannot be read as structure at all — a parameter that is never text, a value passed as an argument vector rather than a command line, a builder that emits nodes rather than characters. Escaping is an attempt to satisfy the non-collision condition after the fact, one string at a time, forever.

The second half — that quoting is not a function and a quoted variable is a constant — is the trap in code generation, macros, and any interface that takes an expression as data. Wrapping something in a quoting construct does not give you a general mechanism that turns arbitrary values into their representations; it gives you one syntactic operation with a specific meaning, and its result is inert. If you need a hole that later gets filled, you need a variable belonging to the outer layer, distinct from anything in the inner one. Church's own notation makes that distinction visible on the page: the letters that range over the studied language's formulas are drawn from a typeface that never appears inside that language, so a reader can see at a glance which level a symbol belongs to. Making the level visible in the artifact is cheaper than remembering it.

**Source:** [Introduction to Mathematical Logic](../works/introduction-to-mathematical-logic.md) — the section on syntax, which sets out use versus mention and the use of a word as a name of itself, adopts the convention that object-language symbols name themselves in the syntax language subject to the condition that no object-language formula is a syntax-language formula in any other capacity, forbids borrowing object-language formulas into the metalanguage with their original meaning, and criticizes the treatment of quotation marks as a name-forming function and the mistaking of a quoted variable for a variable.
