# Test every claim against the observer who thinks your system is a game with no meaning, because intended interpretation is never recoverable from the artifact

To draw the line between the formal and the interpreted parts of a language, Church invents a character: an onlooker who watches people write formulas and assemble derivations, who has learned to apply the rules for recognizing a well-formed expression and a legitimate inference step, and who flatly refuses to believe any of it means anything. For him the whole activity is a puzzle, of the same kind as a chess problem, and a formula is a board position — significant only as something the rules permit you to move on from. Church then uses the character as an instrument. Whatever can be communicated to that observer while he keeps his stance is the formal content; whatever requires him to concede that the expressions mean something belongs to the interpreted content. The test needs no theory to apply, and it partitions any claim about the system in one pass.

He follows it with the deeper structural point. The formal content and the interpreted content are, in a certain technical sense, the same material: properties defined in terms of meaning can be matched by purely structural properties that hold of exactly the same expressions. What distinguishes an interpreted system from a bare calculus is not extra content but the singling out of one reading among the many the calculus admits, and — this is the part worth pausing on — inside the formalism nothing whatever can be said about why that reading is the special one. It can only be postulated as special from outside. He compares this to the way a richer geometry is recovered from a poorer one by designating one particular line and one particular structure on it: same mathematics, plus a decision about what counts as distinguished.

For an engineer this is the sharpest available statement of what a specification cannot do. Every artifact you produce — a schema, a wire format, a type signature, an API, a state machine — admits many readings, and the one you meant is not in there. Take Church's observer to your own work and ask what he could be told. He can be told which requests the validator accepts, which state transitions the code permits, which fields are non-null, which invariants the tests actually check. He cannot be told that a column holds money in minor units, that one of two replicas is authoritative, that a nullable field is only null during migration, that the retry is safe because the downstream call happens to be idempotent, or that a status code means the work was queued rather than done. Everything in that second list is real, load-bearing, and outside the artifact, which means it is enforced by nothing and is preserved only as long as the people who know it stay.

Two consequences follow, and they pull in the same direction. First, the honest response to anything in the second list is to move it into the first: encode the unit in the type, make the illegal state unconstructible, replace the convention with a check, turn the assumption into a property the observer could verify. What cannot be moved must at least be recorded as an assumption rather than believed to be documented by the code. Second, this gap never closes by growing the system. Church notes that a language rich enough to describe its own structure and its own meaning still fails to prove some of the most elementary claims about its own interpretation, so the outer standpoint remains necessary permanently — even after both levels are made fully precise. A system cannot certify its own reading. There is always a level from which the interpretation is fixed, and the discipline is to know which of your beliefs live up there.

**Source:** [Introduction to Mathematical Logic](../works/introduction-to-mathematical-logic.md) — the section on semantics, which introduces the observer who takes formula-manipulation for a game in order to partition claims about a language into formal and interpreted, argues that semantic notions have extensionally coincident syntactic counterparts so that the distinction rests on the significance assigned to one interpretation among many and cannot be stated within the formalism, draws the analogy to recovering metric geometry from projective geometry, and observes that a language able to express its own semantics still has elementary true semantic statements that are not among its theorems.
