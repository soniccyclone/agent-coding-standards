# The step you perform so reflexively that it feels like recognition rather than action is the one your specification will omit

Church traces the history of one rule — substitution, the move that lets you take a proved general statement and use a particular instance of it — across forty years of published logic, and the story is uncomfortable. The man who invented the method of stating rules as operations on syntax used substitution constantly in his first book and never stated it, adding it only fourteen years later for a different system. The first published statement of it for the propositional calculus was inadequate in a specific way: it did not make clear that the material you substitute may itself contain variables, which is precisely the case where the operation gets delicate. Russell stated it properly in one paper and then dropped it from the next. And the most influential logical work of the era took the position that the rule could not be stated at all — that noticing a formula to be an instance of a general theorem was an act of recognition, not something a general rule could capture. It took an outsider, working on a rival system, to write the rule down and then supply it for that work; the authors conceded the omission years later.

Church's diagnosis is that they had abandoned, or never accepted, the idea that inference rules are syntactic operations. That is the mechanism. An operation you perform hundreds of times without deliberation stops presenting itself to you as an operation. It feels like seeing rather than doing, and things you merely see do not go on the list of things the system does. The gap is invisible from inside the practice because the practice is not wrong — everyone performing the substitutions performed them correctly, so nothing ever failed and nothing ever prompted anyone to look. Only someone attempting to state the complete rule set, from outside, with the goal of a machine-checkable account, has any reason to discover that a rule is missing.

Two things follow. The first is a test for whether something is actually specified: can it be described purely in terms of what to do to the artifact, with no appeal to what a competent reader will notice? A rule phrased as "recognize that this is an instance of that" has no content a checker can use, because the whole difficulty lives in the word "instance." The second is that the first attempt to fill such a gap will characteristically undershoot. The partial statement covers the easy case — substitute something closed and self-contained — and misses the case where the substituted material carries its own variables, its own bindings, its own assumptions about scope. That is the case where names collide, where capture happens, where the operation is not what naive reading suggests. A specification that stops short of it looks finished while leaving the hard part unwritten, which is worse than no specification at all, because it forecloses the search.

The engineering shape of this is familiar once you look for it. The convention everyone on a team honors and nobody has written — how identifiers are formed, which fields are safe to mutate, what a nil value in this column implies — produces zero failures until the day someone automates the step or a newcomer joins, at which point it turns out the convention had cases nobody enumerated. Migration scripts, retry logic, deployment sequencing, and the manual verification a senior engineer does by eye before approving a change are all in this category. So is the "obvious" transformation a code generator performs. The move is not to document more, but to ask, of every step in a process, whether it could be handed to something that cannot see: a script, a type checker, an unfamiliar person. Whatever survives that handoff was specified. Whatever needs a knowing human to look at it and recognize something was never a rule; it was a habit that had not yet been noticed.

**Source:** [Introduction to Mathematical Logic](../works/introduction-to-mathematical-logic.md) — the historical notes to the chapter on the propositional calculus, tracing the statement of the rule of substitution from Frege's unstated use in 1879, through Couturat's insufficient 1905 formulation, Russell's 1906 statement and 1908 omission, the claim in Principia Mathematica that recognizing an instance cannot be erected into a general rule, Lewis's supplying of the rule from outside, and Russell's later acknowledgement of the gap.
