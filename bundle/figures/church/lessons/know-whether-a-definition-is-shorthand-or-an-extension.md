# Know whether a definition is eliminable shorthand or a genuine extension, because a definition facility rigorous enough to trust is also one you did not need

Church sets up a large apparatus of abbreviations — dropped brackets, precedence tiers, a dot notation, named shorthands, schemata standing for infinitely many shorthands at once — and is careful to say what none of it is. None of it belongs to the system under study. All of it is a concession to the shortness of human life and patience, of a kind he claims to disdain in principle, and anyone who objects is invited to rewrite the whole book without it, which he characterizes as long but entirely mechanical. That last word is the test. Shorthand is shorthand exactly when its removal is a mechanical operation that leaves no residue, and the invitation to perform the removal is what proves the claim rather than asserting it.

He then separates out the things that get called definitions and are not this. Some explain a notation the language already contains, stated inside that same language. Some are statements in the metalanguage assigning meaning. And some genuinely extend the language by introducing notation that was not there — and these, he insists, are a different animal, because they are part of the object language and so must be governed by rules laid down as precisely as the formation rules themselves. Then comes the observation that makes the whole distinction matter. Once such rules of definition are formulated precisely enough to be trustworthy, they are theoretically superfluous: if you can specify exactly what may be introduced, you could have surveyed the possibilities in advance and simply included them as primitives. So the facility earns nothing in principle. Church's own choice follows — he declines object-language definitions entirely, and notes what the choice buys him: he never has to answer whether the sign used in a definition is the same equality the system already has, or whether such definitions belong to the language or to the discussion of it.

He also disposes of the assumption that an abbreviation must be shorter. Some of his are not, and he keeps them because they make a structure easier to see or throw a particular feature into relief. Brevity is one reason to name something; it is not the reason, and a name that costs characters and buys clarity is a good trade.

For a programmer the sorting question is the one to internalize, because two things wear the same word. Is this construct removable by a mechanical expansion whose result you would recognize, or does it change what the system means? A type alias and a distinct type look alike in a diff and are not remotely the same commitment. Sugar that desugars, and a language feature with its own evaluation rule, differ in exactly this way. A macro that expands to code you could have written, and a macro that introduces a new binding discipline, sit on opposite sides. The test is Church's: perform the elimination, at least in your head, and see whether anything is left over. If nothing is, the construct costs only reading. If something is, you have enlarged the system that every future property has to be proved about, and it needs the same rigor as the primitives — not the informal treatment shorthand deserves.

The corollary is a caution about extension mechanisms in general. When a plugin interface, a rules engine, a configuration language, or a metaprogramming facility grows precise enough that you can state exactly what it admits, ask what it is still buying. Often the answer is that the enumerable set of things it admits could have been provided directly, more simply and with better error messages, and the generality was purchased with a layer nobody wanted to reason through. Generality that is fully specified is generality you can replace with a list.

**Source:** [Introduction to Mathematical Logic](../works/introduction-to-mathematical-logic.md) — the section on definitions, which classes its abbreviation conventions as devices for presentation rather than parts of the system and offers their mechanical elimination as proof, distinguishes abbreviative from explicative, metalinguistic, and language-extending definitions, argues that once rules of definition are made precise they become superfluous because everything they could introduce could have been supplied as primitive notation, gives the resulting reasons for admitting no object-language definitions, and notes that a definition need not shorten anything to be worth making.
