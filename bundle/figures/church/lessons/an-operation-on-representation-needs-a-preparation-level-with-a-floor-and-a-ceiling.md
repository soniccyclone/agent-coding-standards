# A transformation defined on how a thing is written is a function of the writing, so fix the preparation level with both a floor and a ceiling

Church's dualization operation swaps paired symbols wherever they appear: implication against its converse, conjunction against disjunction, universal against existential quantification. The complication is that in his system most of those symbols are not primitive. They are defined abbreviations standing for combinations of a much smaller set. So the same formula can be written several ways, each with different pairs present on the surface, and swapping the surface pairs therefore gives different results depending on which abbreviations were in force when you wrote it. Dualization is not an operation on the formula. It is an operation on a piece of writing that happens to abbreviate the formula, and Church says so outright rather than papering over it.

He then does two separate things, and the separation is the lesson. First, he proves that any two duals of the same formula are provably equivalent to each other. The ambiguity is real but invisible to the relation that downstream results actually consume, so for the purpose of "is this a theorem" the multiplicity costs nothing. Second, where he needs a determinate answer rather than an answer up to equivalence — dualizing schemata, which he wants to cite by name later — he does not appeal to the equivalence. He fixes a preparation: before dualizing, withdraw abbreviations by bracket omission and a named list of definitions, and stop there. Not further. The result is a function of the schema as literally written, and he states that constraint instead of claiming a well-definedness he does not have.

The instruction to stop is the part people miss. Unwinding all the way to primitives would make the operation well-defined and useless, because the pairs it swaps would no longer be present anywhere — conjunction and the existential quantifier would have dissolved into negation and implication, and there would be nothing left to interchange. The transformation lives at a specific level of the representation and requires its input presented at that level: normalized enough that the answer does not wobble, not so normalized that the structure it acts on has been dissolved. A canonicalization step for an operation like this has a floor and a ceiling, and quoting only the floor is how you get a tool that silently does nothing on inputs someone else prepared more thoroughly than you expected.

Nearly every source-level tool is in this position and only some admit it. A formatter, a codemod, a lint rule, an import rewriter, a docstring extractor, a diff, a coverage mapper — all of these are functions of the text, not of the program the text denotes, and each of them has a level at which it works. Desugar too little and the tool sees ten spellings of the same construct and handles three. Desugar too much and the construct it was written to recognize no longer exists: a rule about a language's convenience syntax finds nothing once that syntax has been expanded, and a signature scheme that canonicalizes away a field cannot then protect the field. The bug reports read as inconsistency but the cause is always that the input arrived at a different level than the transformation assumes, and nobody wrote the level down.

So when you build something that rewrites representations, write both bounds into the interface: what must be unwound before the transformation runs, and what must not be. Then separately decide which equivalence downstream consumers actually need, and if it is coarser than literal identity, prove your outputs agree under it and stop trying to make the operation deterministic. Confusing the two obligations produces the usual failure — a tool that is neither canonical enough to be reproducible nor honest enough to declare that its results are only meaningful up to some equivalence, so consumers compare its output byte-for-byte and discover the disagreement in a diff nobody can explain.

**Source:** [Introduction to Mathematical Logic](../works/introduction-to-mathematical-logic.md) — the section on duality for the first-order functional calculus, where the dual of a formula is obtained by interchanging paired connectives and quantifiers in an expression that abbreviates it, so that a formula has many duals; the metatheorem that any two duals of the same formula are provably equivalent; and the accompanying stipulation that in dualizing a theorem or axiom schema, abbreviations by bracket omission and by a specified list of definition schemata are first to be withdrawn, restoration toward unabbreviated form proceeding that far and no farther, making the dual unique for a schema as actually written.
