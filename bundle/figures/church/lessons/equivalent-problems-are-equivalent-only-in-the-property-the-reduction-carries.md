# Two problems shown equivalent are equivalent only in the property the reduction actually carries, and the ones it drops are usually constructivity and cost

Church has two decision problems on the table — deciding whether a formula is provable and deciding whether it is valid — and a theorem, Gödel's completeness result, saying the two predicates coincide. It would be natural to treat that as licensing free translation between them. Church refuses, and the way he refuses is the interesting part. He says a solution to a special case of one problem is a solution to the corresponding special case of the other *in one sense*, and in another sense is not, and he adds that he has not attempted to make the second sense precise. He then names the concrete symptom: the argument that settles validity for a class of formulas supplies no method whatever for producing a proof of a formula that passes its test. The predicates agree; the deliverables do not.

He is equally scrupulous about cost. Several of the special cases are solved by results of the form "if valid in a domain of this size, then valid," where the size is given by an explicit formula. For one of them the formula is a tower of exponentials, and Church says outright that this is a strictly theoretical solution, hardly usable in practice, pointing readers at a different and more practicable procedure derivable from the same papers. He goes further and remarks generally that although most of these cases can be put in the finite-domain form, that form is seldom the most efficient one for actually applying the procedure to a particular formula. The statement optimized for proving a theorem and the statement optimized for running it are different statements, and he does not let the elegance of the first stand in for the availability of the second.

The generalizable claim is that "these are the same problem" is never unqualified. A reduction transports some properties and destroys others, and which ones it transports is a fact about the reduction, not about the problems. Decidability survives almost anything. Constructivity — does a yes answer come with the object? — survives only if the reduction is itself constructive. Complexity survives only if the reduction is cheap and does not blow up the instance. Locality of the answer, the ability to explain *why*, incrementality under small input changes: none of these are carried by default, and a proof of equivalence typically does not even mention them.

This is a routine trap in engineering, where equivalences are usually informal and therefore even less examined. "Our config format is isomorphic to theirs" is true of the value sets and false of the diffs, the error messages, and the round-trip through comments. "This query is equivalent to that one" holds for the result set and not for the plan. "Solving A gives you B" is often true in the sense that a translator exists, and false in the sense that the translator is quadratic, or that it turns a targeted failure into a whole-input failure, or that it loses provenance so nothing downstream can tell you which record was responsible. Rewriting a problem into a form some existing solver handles is a good instinct precisely because such reductions exist; the discipline is to name, before committing, which properties you need the answer to have and to check that the translation preserves each one rather than the single property the equivalence was proved about.

Church's habit is the model: when he notices that a correspondence holds in one sense and not another, he says so even when he cannot yet say precisely what the other sense is. An unformalized reservation, recorded, is worth more than a clean equivalence that quietly overstates itself.

**Source:** [Introduction to Mathematical Logic](../works/introduction-to-mathematical-logic.md) — the section on the decision problem in special cases, where Church distinguishes the decision problem for provability from the decision problems for validity and satisfiability, notes that Gödel's completeness theorem makes solutions correspond in one sense but not in another he does not make precise, since the Bernays–Schönfinkel argument yields no effective method of finding a proof of a formula that passes its test, and where he separately labels Ackermann's finite-domain bound a strictly theoretical solution hardly available for use in practice and observes that the finite-domain statement is seldom the most efficient form for applying a decision procedure.
