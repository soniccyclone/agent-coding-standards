# Proving the more general thing can retire an entire apparatus built for the special case — but check what the apparatus was also giving you before deleting it

Church spends two sections building specialized machinery to prove the completeness theorem: a normal form with a particular quantifier shape, a reduction procedure to reach it, metatheorems relating the original formula to the reduced one, and then an intricate argument that constructs a falsifying interpretation stage by stage. Later, in the course of proving a stronger statement about arbitrary collections of formulas rather than single ones, he obtains the completeness theorem again — this time in four lines, as a corollary — and points out that the new route uses none of the normal form work and none of the earlier proof. The stronger claim was cheaper to establish than the weaker one, and establishing it made an entire chapter's apparatus unnecessary for that purpose.

The counterintuitive part is that generality lowered the cost. It happens more often than instinct suggests, and the mechanism is usually the same: the special case forces you to preserve incidental structure that the general statement is free to discard. Here the single-formula proof had to build a concrete falsifying assignment out of the formula's own parts, which is exactly why it needed the formula in a particular shape. The general proof reasons about consistency of a collection and never looks at the shape of anything. The special case was harder because it was more specific, not despite it.

Anyone who has generalized a function has seen the same thing. Three near-duplicate handlers, each with its own carefully tuned preprocessing, collapse into one that needs none of it, and the preprocessing disappears with them. A cache keyed on a general property retires three special-purpose invalidation paths. A protocol that treats all peers uniformly retires the leader-specific branch. The signal that this is available is a set of transformations that exist only to make inputs fit a narrower assumption; drop the assumption and they have no other reason to exist.

The discipline is in what Church does next, which is nothing — he does not delete the earlier sections. He keeps both proofs and later sets the reader to comparing them on the basis each requires. The reason matters. The specialized route yields a corollary the general one does not: an effective criterion, a concrete test on finite objects that tells you when a formula is provable. The general route runs through a construction he has already flagged as not effective in any sense, so it establishes existence and hands you no procedure. Two proofs of the same statement are not redundant when they rest on different assumptions and deliver different by-products.

So when a generalization makes a pile of specialized code look deletable, separate the claim it subsumes from the properties it does not. The old path may have been giving you a bound the new one only asserts exists, a construction where the new one is an existence argument, an error message that names something the general version has abstracted away, or a performance characteristic that the uniform treatment averages out. Deleting is still usually right — that is the direction the code should move — but the thing to write down is what the specialized version was also delivering, so that when someone later needs it, they find a recorded trade rather than a mystery. And the reverse reading is just as useful: if you cannot state anything the specialized version provided beyond the result itself, that is the strongest possible argument for deleting it today.

**Source:** [Introduction to Mathematical Logic](../works/introduction-to-mathematical-logic.md) — the section on the Löwenheim and Skolem theorems, where after proving that every consistent class of formulas is simultaneously satisfiable in an enumerably infinite domain, Church notes that Gödel's completeness theorem and Löwenheim's theorem follow as corollaries by short arguments making no use of the Skolem normal form or of the preceding section's proof; together with the earlier remark that the maximal-consistent-class construction underlying the general result is not an effective one, and the exercise directing the reader to compare the two completeness proofs as to the strength of the basis each requires.
