# A rule set does not determine its own consequences: the same axioms under a stronger surrounding logic are a different subject entirely

Church opens his treatment of axiom systems by insisting on something usually left tacit. A set of postulates is never the whole specification of a mathematical theory, because the theorems you can reach depend jointly on the postulates and on the logic they are added to, and altering either one changes the result. He then makes the point unmistakable with the cleanest possible example: one fixed set of arithmetic postulates, added to a first-order logic, yields elementary number theory; that same set, unchanged, added to a fourth-order logic yields analysis — rational, real, and complex numbers all become definable, and an entirely different branch of mathematics comes out the other end. Nothing was added to the axioms. What changed was the expressive and inferential apparatus surrounding them.

The consequence for anyone who writes down rules is that the rules are half the specification at most. What the rules imply is a function of the machinery permitted to draw inferences from them, and that machinery is exactly the part that goes unwritten, because it is ambient and therefore invisible to the person immersed in it. Church makes it explicit as a matter of routine practice: each of his systems is presented as postulates plus a named underlying logic, and he notes as a separate fact which properties of the postulates would survive a change of that logic and which would not. Two of his notions — consistency and independence in their derivability sense — depend essentially on the choice; the corresponding model-theoretic notions largely do not. Knowing which of your claims are relative to the surrounding machinery and which are not is the whole skill.

He also shows that this cuts finer than a choice between weak and strong. He remarks that the boundary between what belongs to the underlying logic and what belongs to the special subject is subjective and essentially arbitrary, with no controlling principle available, and illustrates it with the equality relation and with set membership — either can be taken as part of the general framework or introduced as a specific undefined term of the theory, and competent authors have gone both ways. So the framework-versus-domain split is a decision you make, not a fact you discover, and you should expect reasonable people to draw it elsewhere.

For engineers the transfer is almost embarrassingly direct, because we habitually publish the rules and suppress the machinery. A schema is the constraints plus the engine that enforces them: the same foreign keys mean different things under deferred and immediate checking. A concurrency invariant is the invariant plus the memory model, and the same code is correct on one and broken on another. A set of business rules is those rules plus whatever the rules engine can chain, and adding transitive inference to the engine silently adds conclusions nobody wrote. A type-level guarantee is the annotation plus the checker's power; the identical signature promises very different things where one language admits an escape hatch and another does not. An access policy is the policy plus the evaluation semantics for overlapping grants. In every case the artifact under review is the pair, and reviewing only the half that was written down is how a system passes review and still does something nobody intended.

Two habits follow. When you write rules, name the machinery in the same document, with its version and its permitted inferences, because a rule set inherited into a stronger engine acquires consequences without any change to its text. And when you inherit rules from elsewhere — a standard, a prior team, a paper — do not assume the machinery came with them. Reconstruct what they were assuming, since the consequences they proved may not be consequences in your setting, and yours may include things they would have rejected.
