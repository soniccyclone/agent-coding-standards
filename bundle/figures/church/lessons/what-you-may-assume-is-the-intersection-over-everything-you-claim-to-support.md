# What a general system may assume is the intersection over everything it claims to support, so each configuration you add silently deletes leverage rather than breaking anything

Church proves that his first-order system is not complete in any of the senses he defined, and then explains why in a way that is more useful than the proof. Take a formula saying that if a predicate holds of one thing it holds of another. In an interpretation where there is exactly one individual, that formula is true for all values of its variables. It is also true in interpretations where the predicate letters range over only the constantly-true and constantly-false functions. If those were the only readings anyone had in mind, the formula would be an obvious candidate to adopt as an axiom. It cannot be adopted, because the system is also required to be sound for interpretations with more than one individual, and there it is false. The incompleteness is not a defect in the axiomatization. It is the price of the domain being open.

He states the positive form later as the content of the completeness result: the theorems are exactly the formulas that come out true for all values of the free variables, regardless of what the constants are taken to denote, and independently of the nature and the number of the individuals — provided only that there is at least one. Three quantifications, each ranging over a space the system does not get to choose. What the system may assert is precisely the intersection.

That intersection is the thing engineers manage badly, because its cost never presents itself as a failure. Every platform, backend, runtime, tenant, locale, or protocol version you commit to supporting subtracts from the set of facts your shared code is allowed to rely on. Support one more database and you lose the assumption of a specific isolation level. Support a 32-bit target and you lose the assumption about integer width. Support offline clients and you lose the assumption that a read follows the write that preceded it in program order. Add a second tenant with its own identity provider and every claim about token shape leaves the core. In each case nothing breaks on the day the commitment is made. What happens is that some future simplification is no longer available, and the reason will be invisible, because the constraint lives in a support matrix and the code that would have used the assumption was never written.

Two consequences worth acting on. First, when adding a supported configuration, price it as a subtraction from the assumption set rather than as an addition of a code path, and try to name at least one thing the core can no longer take for granted. If you cannot name anything, either the configuration is redundant with one you already support or you have not looked. Second, when you find yourself unable to state a rule that is true in every environment you have actually seen, check whether the environments you have not seen are ones you are genuinely obliged to serve. Church's system is incomplete because the class of interpretations is deliberately wide; had he wanted the extra theorems, the move available to him was to narrow the class and say so. That move is available in engineering too, and it is usually the right one: dropping a supported configuration is how you buy back an assumption, and it is far cheaper than the alternative, which is a core so general that every useful fact about it has to be re-established locally by every caller.

**Source:** [Introduction to Mathematical Logic](../works/introduction-to-mathematical-logic.md) — the section on the consistency of the functional calculus of first order, its metatheorem that the system is not complete with respect to negation and not absolutely complete, and the accompanying semantic explanation that a formula such as one predicate application implying another is valid in interpretations with a single individual and in interpretations restricting the functional variables to constant functions but must not be a theorem for the sake of soundness in interpretations with more individuals — together with the later characterization of the theorems as the formulas true for all values of the free variables, regardless of the denotations assigned to the constants, and independently of the nature and number of the individuals.
