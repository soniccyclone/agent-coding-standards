# Normalize-then-compare decides an equivalence only if the normal form is complete for it; otherwise you have built a one-way test and will read its silence as agreement

Church defines a reduction that drives every quantifier in a formula out to the front, then deletes the ones that bind nothing, and calls the result the prenex normal form. He proves three things about it, carefully kept apart: at every stage exactly one reduction step applies, so the process is deterministic and the output is a function of the input; the process is effective; and each step preserves equivalence, so a formula is always provably equivalent to its normal form. Every property you would want from a canonicalizer appears to be present.

Then he adds the sentence that matters and leaves the counterexamples to the reader: two formulas can be provably equivalent and still have different prenex normal forms. The normalizer is a function on syntax, and the equivalence it respects is coarser than the identity it produces. So the test "same normal form" is sound in one direction and useless in the other. If two things normalize alike they are equivalent; if they normalize differently you have learned nothing at all. The same book shows the contrast a few pages later, in the quantifier-free fragment, where full disjunctive normal form does have the missing property — there, provable equivalence holds exactly when the normal forms agree, and normalize-then-compare really is a decision procedure. Two normalizers, both deterministic, both equivalence-preserving, and only one of them decides anything.

The distinction is soundness versus completeness for the relation you care about, and it is the single most common thing conflated in practice because both properties feel like "the canonicalizer works." Uniqueness of output tells you the function is well defined. Preservation tells you the output means the same as the input. Neither implies that everything meaning the same lands in the same place, and that last property is the only one that licenses comparing normal forms as a test.

Working systems are full of the incomplete version being read as the complete one. Canonical JSON serialization identifies documents that differ only in key order, so unequal bytes still allow equal documents and a cache keyed that way over-misses — harmless. Route the same assumption into a security check and it inverts: an authorization comparison that only detects the identifications the canonicalizer happens to perform will accept two spellings of one identity as two identities. Comparing formatted source to detect a semantic change flags reorderings the formatter does not normalize. Structural diff on ASTs reports a change when a refactor moved a declaration. Query plan fingerprints treat logically identical queries as distinct and duplicate the cache entry. In every one of these the machinery is correct and the reading of its negative answer is not.

So separate the two claims when you build the thing and again when you use it. Say explicitly which relation the normal form is complete for, which is usually a much smaller relation than the one you have in mind — the normalizer identifies exactly the differences somebody wrote a rule for, and nothing else. Then decide what a difference is allowed to trigger. A positive result from an incomplete normalizer is evidence; a negative result is the absence of evidence, and the two must not flow into the same branch. Where you genuinely need a decision procedure, the obligation is to prove the completeness direction, and if it will not go through, the honest move is to expose the operation as "definitely the same" versus "not known to be the same" rather than as equality.

**Source:** [Introduction to Mathematical Logic](../works/introduction-to-mathematical-logic.md) — the section on prenex normal form: the metatheorems establishing that the reduction steps bringing quantifiers to the front terminate, are effective, and yield a uniquely determined result, together with the theorem that a formula is provably equivalent to its prenex normal form; and the immediately following remark that despite uniqueness and equivalence-preservation, two provably equivalent formulas need not share a prenex normal form — contrasted with the exercise on the quantifier-free fragment, where provable equivalence holds precisely when full disjunctive normal forms agree.
