# Promoting a derived shortcut into a primitive buys naturalness and spends the property that every step is locally checkable

The deduction theorem is the formal statement of the thing every mathematician does without thinking: to establish that one statement implies another, assume the first and derive the second. Church presents it as a metatheorem — a result *about* the system, proved once from outside, guaranteeing that any argument conducted in that style can be mechanically expanded into a proof using only the system's actual primitive steps. Others proposed installing it directly as a primitive rule of inference instead. Church notes what that costs, and he is unusually blunt about it: such a rule has a less elementary character than the others, and under the definition of a logistic system he had already committed to, it would not be admissible at all.

The reason is worth stating precisely, because it is the transferable part. Every other rule in the system takes formulas as premises. This one takes a *derivation* as a premise — its applicability depends not on the shape of some finite pieces of text sitting in front of you but on the existence and structure of an entire prior process. Checking an ordinary step means pattern-matching a couple of strings. Checking this step means auditing a whole subargument, including which assumptions were live while it ran. The rule is not wrong and it is not unusable; it simply is not the same kind of object as its neighbours, and a system containing it no longer has the property that verification is a local scan.

Against that, Church grants the honest advantage: naturalness. Taking the deduction theorem as primitive is nothing more than admitting formally what practitioners have always done informally. A formalism whose steps correspond to the moves people actually make is easier to write in, easier to read back, and less likely to be abandoned for an informal argument that nobody checks at all. The trade is not minimality versus bloat. It is whether the granularity of your rule set matches the granularity of human reasoning, or the granularity of a checker.

The engineering shape recurs constantly and is usually made unconsciously. A pattern gets used often enough that someone promotes it: a macro becomes a language keyword, an idiom becomes a framework primitive, a sequence of migrations becomes a single declarative operation, a common code review argument becomes a lint rule. Every such promotion is the same move — a thing that was previously explicable by unfolding it into base operations becomes a base operation. Usability goes up; the population of people who can reason about it from first principles goes down. And the specific hazard is that the new primitive often reads a much larger context than the primitives around it. A framework annotation that behaves differently depending on what else was registered elsewhere, a build rule whose correctness depends on the whole prior sequence of steps, a transaction helper that is only sound if nothing in the enclosing scope did something particular — all of them are deduction-theorem-shaped: convenient, natural, and no longer checkable by looking at the call site.

The discipline Church models is to make the choice explicitly and to know which side of the line the thing lands on. Where a shortcut can be kept as a derived result — proved once, mechanically expandable back into base steps — keep it derived, because then you have the convenience and the locality. Where it must be primitive, say so, and accept that you have redefined what counts as a step and therefore what your tooling can independently verify.

**Source:** [Introduction to Mathematical Logic](../works/introduction-to-mathematical-logic.md) — the historical notes to the chapter on the propositional calculus, on the proposal by Jaśkowski and Gentzen to use the deduction theorem as a primitive rule of inference, Church's observation that such a rule is less elementary than is otherwise usual and would not be admissible under his own definition of a logistic system, and his concession that the disadvantage is partly offset by the naturalness of formally recognizing the ordinary informal practice of proving an implication by assuming the antecedent.
