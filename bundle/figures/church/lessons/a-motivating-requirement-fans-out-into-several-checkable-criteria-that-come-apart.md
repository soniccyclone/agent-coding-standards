# A requirement stated in terms of what you meant fans out into several checkable criteria that come apart, so name the one you are claiming and prove each separately

Church wants to say his propositional system is consistent, and he is explicit that the wish is semantic in origin: nothing logically absurd should be provable. He is equally explicit that this wish, as stated, is not something a system can be checked against, because it mentions meaning and the system does not contain its meaning. So he rebuilds the requirement out of material the system does contain — and it does not come back as one property. It comes back as four. Consistency relative to a chosen transformation of formulas, meaning that no formula and its transform are both theorems. Absolute consistency, meaning that not everything is a theorem. Hilbert's version, meaning that one designated formula is not a theorem. Post's version, meaning that a bare variable is not a theorem. He then does the same thing for completeness and gets three. And he does not wave at the family: he proves the system has each of them, one metatheorem at a time, each with its own argument.

What deserves attention is the shape of the move rather than the logic. A single informal intention was replaced by a set of formal surrogates, none of which is the intention and each of which is checkable. The surrogates are not equivalent in general — they happen to travel together in this particular system, and Church shows this by proving them, not by assuming it. Nothing forces such a family to collapse. In a different system the absolute version can hold while the transformation-relative version fails, which is exactly why he keeps them apart and names them after the people who proposed them.

The engineering situation is identical and is usually handled worse. Everything anyone actually wants from a system is stated the way Church's original wish was stated: it should be correct, it should be available, it should be secure, it should be fast, the data should be consistent. None of those is checkable. Each becomes checkable only by being replaced with surrogates, and the surrogates always fan out. Distributed data consistency splits into linearizable, sequentially consistent, causally consistent, read-your-writes, and eventually consistent, and the industry's most expensive confusions come from a design document saying "consistent" while a system provides one of these and a caller assumes another. Availability splits into uptime of a process, success rate of a request class, and latency under some percentile, which can diverge violently. Correctness splits into type-checks, tests pass, invariants hold under fault injection, and a proof of a specific property. Security splits into a dozen threat-model-relative claims. In every case the informal word is the thing everyone agrees on and the formal criterion is the thing that is actually true, and the two are connected by a choice nobody wrote down.

Two habits follow directly from Church's practice. First, when you make a claim, name the criterion and not the wish. "The store is consistent" is not a claim; "no client observes a write followed by a read that misses it, under single-region operation" is. The named version can be tested, can be falsified, and can be compared against what a caller needs. Second, when several criteria are available and you believe they coincide for your system, that belief is a theorem you owe, not a definition you may assume. Church proves consistency four times because four times is what it costs; the alternative — proving one and calling it consistency — is the move that produces a system correct in the sense nobody was relying on. And there is a diagnostic worth keeping: whenever a requirement is expressed in a word rather than a predicate, someone downstream has already chosen which surrogate it means, and it is probably not the same one you chose.

**Source:** [Introduction to Mathematical Logic](../works/introduction-to-mathematical-logic.md) — the sections on consistency and completeness of the propositional calculus, which observe that both notions are semantical in motivation, set out to modify them so as to be syntactical in character and thus applicable independently of any interpretation, list the resulting several inequivalent syntactic definitions of each (relative to a transformation, absolute, Hilbert's particular-sentence version, and Post's), and then prove the system has each of them as a separate metatheorem.
