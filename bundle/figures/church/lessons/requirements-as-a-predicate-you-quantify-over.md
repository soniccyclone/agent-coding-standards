# Any set of requirements can be a foundation you build inside or a predicate you quantify over, and the second turns every specific result into a general one

Church closes his treatment of axiom systems by distinguishing two entirely different jobs the same list of conditions can do. In the first, the conditions are added to a base as further starting assumptions, and you then work inside the enlarged system; whatever you prove is a fact about that system. In the second, the conditions are turned into a predicate: replace each undefined term by a variable, close everything up, and what you have is a form that is satisfied or not by a candidate structure. You then stay inside the general theory and prove results of the shape *anything satisfying this predicate also satisfies that*. He works the transformation out concretely for the algebraic notion of an integral domain, showing the list of conditions rewritten as a single defining schema over variables, and observes that this is what informal algebra has always been doing when it states axioms for a group or a ring — those are definitions of a kind of structure, not foundations of a subject.

Then he makes the claim that gives the observation its force. This second treatment is always available. Any branch of mathematics could be handled this way if you chose, including arithmetic: rather than deriving number theory from postulates taken as axioms, convert the postulates into the definition of *an arithmetic* and restate every ordinary theorem as a general theorem about anything that is one. It is in exactly this sense, he notes, that mathematics can be said to reduce to logic, and that logic and mathematics look less like different subjects than like the elementary and advanced parts of one.

The trade between the two is worth having precisely. Building inside gives you a fixed setting where the assumptions are simply true and can be used without ceremony; the cost is that every result is a result about that one setting, and nothing transfers. Quantifying over gives you every result at once for every structure meeting the conditions — Church names the economy explicitly as the standard advantage of the axiomatic method — at the cost of needing an ambient theory expressive enough to talk about arbitrary structures, which in his case means climbing to a higher-order logic. And he flags a cost that is easy to miss and exactly right: the general theorems about *an arithmetic* come for free with no additional assumptions, but proving that there exists even one arithmetic requires adding an existence assumption of its own. Results about all satisfiers are cheap; the existence of a satisfier is a separate purchase.

Programmers make this same choice constantly, usually without noticing there are two options. Building a service inside a framework, extending a base class, writing code that assumes a particular database — that is conditions-as-foundation, and it is why the results do not travel. Declaring an interface, a trait, a typeclass, a protocol, or a set of laws and writing algorithms against it — that is conditions-as-predicate, and it is why one implementation of an algorithm serves every conforming type. The functions written against an ordering, a monoid, an iterator, or a transport are exactly the general theorems Church describes: proved once, holding of every satisfier, forever.

His flagged cost lands too, and it is the failure mode of interface-first design. An interface with no implementor is a set of theorems about nothing. The general result is genuinely free; the demonstration that anything satisfies the constraints is separate work, and it is the work that gets skipped when a team writes the abstraction before the second case exists. The right sequencing follows from taking the trade seriously: build inside a concrete setting until you have a second satisfier in hand, then lift the conditions out into a predicate and re-derive what you had as a general result. That ordering pays the existence cost first, when it is cheap, and buys the generality only once there is something for it to generalize over.

**Source:** [Introduction to Mathematical Logic](../works/introduction-to-mathematical-logic.md) — the section on postulate theory, which distinguishes postulates serving as added axioms of a logistic system from postulates treated as propositional functions via their representing forms, works the integral-domain postulates into a definition schema over functional variables, observes that informal abstract algebra uses postulates in this second role and that every branch of mathematics including arithmetic could be so treated, notes the resulting economy of results holding for all interpretations and the consequent sense in which mathematics reduces to logic, and remarks that while the general theorems require no added axioms, asserting that at least one satisfying structure exists requires an axiom of infinity.
