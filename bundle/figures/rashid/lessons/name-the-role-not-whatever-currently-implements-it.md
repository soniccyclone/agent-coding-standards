# Name the role, never the thing currently filling it, and design so a stranger can stand in the middle

**Lesson:** The instinct when wiring components together is to let one hold a name for another: the identity of the process, the object, the address of the thing that answers. This feels like the minimal amount of indirection, and it silently forecloses most of what you will later want. If the name denotes the current implementor, then that implementor cannot be replaced while anyone holds the name, cannot be watched, cannot be moved, and cannot be substituted by something that stands between it and its callers. The stronger discipline is to make the name denote a role — a place where requests for some service accumulate — and to keep the question of who is currently serving that role entirely outside what any holder of the name can see or ask about.

The consequences arrive as a cluster, and they are not independent. If serving a role is a right that can be handed over, then a component's death is survivable: something else takes over the accumulated requests and the callers never learn that anything happened. If the right to serve is distinguishable from the right to be the role's ultimate owner, then takeover on failure has a defined meaning rather than being a race between claimants — and forbidding two servers from draining the same queue is not a limitation but the thing that keeps the semantics coherent when the claimants are on different machines. If the name is opaque, a third party can create names of its own, present them as if they were the originals, and forward everything through itself. That single property is where debuggers, monitors, format translators, and remote-communication relays all come from, and it is why none of them require changes to the layer that defines the naming. A design that permits interposition gets an open-ended family of capabilities for free; a design that leaks identity has to add each one by hand, with privilege.

There is a reasoning dividend as well, and it comes from the names being unforgeable rather than merely opaque. If a component can only be reached by someone who was given the right to reach it, then the set of things that can affect it is knowable and finite, and a claim about its behavior can be made without quantifying over the whole system. The same closure gives the substrate something valuable: because rights cannot be hidden in private memory, the complete graph of who can talk to whom is available to the system itself, which is what makes it possible to notify everyone affected when a role is retired, and to make placement decisions informed by who actually communicates with whom. Global knowledge of the dependency graph is not something you can retrofit onto a system where references are ordinary data.

A programmer who believes this asks, for every reference their design hands out, what would have to change for a different implementation to answer it tomorrow, and whether a component neither end knows about could be dropped into the path without either end noticing. If the answer to either is no, the reference is naming the wrong thing.

**Source:** [Accent: A Communication Oriented Network Operating System Kernel](../works/accent-a-communication-oriented-network-operating-system-kernel.md) — the interprocess-communication section's argument that processes are the wrong basis for a communication facility, the separation of ownership from the right to receive and its use for service takeover after failure, the intermediary-process construction that yields network transparency and debugging as instances of one mechanism, and the closing observations about unforgeable references and complete system knowledge of communication rights.
