# Give the output medium a closed generative basis instead of a catalogue, and make input ride the same transforms backwards

**Lesson:** There are two ways to let people produce output, and they differ by an unbounded amount. One is to ship a catalogue of finished forms — the widgets, the chart types, the layouts — and let people choose among them. The other is to ship a small basis: constructors that turn ordinary data into elementary pieces of the medium, a set of operators that combine and transform pieces, and something that displays a piece within a coordinate frame. The catalogue's reach is whatever the designers thought of. The basis's reach is everything computable in the medium, because a program that can compute the parameters can compute any composition of the primitives, and composition is where the forms come from. The consequence to notice is a leveling: a kit built the second way is exactly as extensible in its presentation as in its arithmetic, and things people normally regard as vendor-supplied furniture — a scroll bar, a bar chart, an animated sequence — become ordinary user constructions rather than requests to the vendor. When presentation can only come from the catalogue, users end up as expressive as the catalogue rather than as expressive as the machine, and nobody experiences that ceiling as the design decision it was.

The property that makes a basis a basis rather than a pile is closure: every operator must take pieces of the medium to pieces of the medium, so the result of a composition is a legal input to the next composition and depth is unbounded. This is what lets an assembly be transformed as a unit — scaled, rotated, merged into a larger one — without anyone having written a rule for the composite case. And it is the test to apply when checking whether you have a basis at all: if some operator only accepts primitives, or produces something that cannot be fed back in, the medium has a floor at which composition stops and the catalogue has crept back in disguised as an operator.

The half of this that gets forgotten is input. An algebra over output alone is only half an algebra, because the moment a person touches the result, coordinates travel the other way and every transform on the outbound path has to be undone on the inbound one. Get this right and sensitivity to input attaches to any composed piece of the medium, not to a privileged class of interactive widgets, so a construction stays interactive through arbitrary scaling and rotation with no work by its author: turn an assembly sideways because its frame is wider than tall, and its hit-testing turns sideways too. Get it wrong and every author reimplements the geometry by hand in the direction the framework declined to handle, which is precisely the bookkeeping the basis existed to abolish. The general rule is that for each transform offered on the way out, the corresponding inverse must exist and be applied automatically on the way in — otherwise composability terminates at the exact point where a human meets the system.

**Source:** [Fabrik: A Visual Programming Environment](../works/fabrik-a-visual-programming-environment.md) — the synthetic-graphics section, with its image-valued objects, its creators mapping numbers and points and style data into elementary images, its generally applicable scale, translate, rotate, invert, clip and merge operators closed over those images, its viewers supplying local coordinate frames, its argument that including primitive image producers confers the ability to synthesize any computable image so that user interfaces become as extensible as numeric and textual manipulation, its sensor components that make any composed image responsive to pointer input, and the scroll-bar example whose display and pointer tracking are both scaled and rotated by the same logic while leaving the diagram uncluttered; plus the closing observation that scores and animations are synthesized the same way.
