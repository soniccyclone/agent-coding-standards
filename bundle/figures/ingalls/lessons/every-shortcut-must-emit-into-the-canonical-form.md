# Make every shortcut and every packaging step a reversible view on the canonical structure, never a one-way door

**Lesson:** Two conveniences that every tool builder is tempted by have the same defect and the same fix. The first is the high-level shortcut: a direct way to produce a common result without assembling it from parts. The second is packaging: a step that turns a working assembly into a finished deliverable by hiding the machinery behind it. Both are worth having, and both go wrong when they operate on a private representation or destroy what they consumed. The discipline that saves them is to require that the shortcut emit exactly the structure a person would have built by hand, and that packaging be a declaration about what is visible rather than a transformation of what exists. Then a person who outgrows the shortcut edits its output instead of starting over, and a person who shipped something can still open it, explain it, and revise it.

The payoff from the first rule is larger than convenience: it makes the shortcut's expressive ceiling stop being the user's ceiling. A drawing surface has no gesture for saying that one shape's corner must follow another's, because a direct-manipulation surface can only express what a hand can do in one motion. But if the drawing gesture lays down the ordinary structure, the relation is expressible one level down, by joining two things in the generated structure that the drawing tool would have left independent. So the correct question about any generator is not how much it can express but what it leaves you holding when you exceed it. A generator whose output is idiomatic hands you the general medium; a generator with a private encoding hands you a rewrite. This also imposes a real obligation on the canonical form, which now has to be legible enough for a human to read and extend after a machine wrote it — a standard that machine-only intermediate representations never have to meet, and which is worth paying for precisely because it collapses two representations into one.

The second rule matters because the derivation of a thing is information about it, and packaging is the moment people habitually throw that information away. Treat the abstraction boundary as an annotation on the full construction — this region is the face, the rest is interior — and hiding becomes a restriction of view that costs nothing and gives up nothing. The interior stays available as documentation of how the thing came to be and as the starting point for the next version, and the same artifact can be a finished deliverable and a work in progress at once, with no export step separating those two states. The general principle is that irreversibility in a tool chain is a design choice rather than a consequence, and it is almost never a choice worth making: every place a tool discards its input, someone later needs the input.
