# Judge an automatic mechanism by how its cost is distributed and how it behaves near exhaustion, then delegate the cases it cannot cover to whoever creates them

**Lesson:** Comparisons between automatic resource-management schemes are usually conducted on the wrong variables. Total overhead and theoretical completeness are the ones people quote; the ones that decide whether a system is pleasant to use are how the overhead is distributed in time and what happens as the resource approaches exhaustion. A scheme that charges a small amount continuously, at the moment of each reference, can beat one with a lower total cost that collects its fee in occasional large instalments, because anything a person or a device is waiting on experiences the instalment as a fault rather than as a cost. The near-empty regime is a second, independent question: a mechanism can be perfectly adequate with plenty of room and degrade sharply when there is little, and since scarcity is exactly the condition under which users meet the mechanism at all, its behavior there deserves as much weight as its behavior in the comfortable case. Both variables are about distribution rather than magnitude, which is why averages hide them.

Choosing on those grounds usually means accepting an incomplete mechanism, and the productive response to incompleteness is not to upgrade the mechanism but to find out who creates the cases it misses and give them the obligation. If the automatic scheme cannot reclaim circular structures, then the kind of thing that introduces a circular link is the natural owner of the knowledge of how to dismantle one, and it can be required to offer an operation that propagates through the structure and breaks it. The manual work then scales with the number of kinds that need it rather than with the number of things in flight, and it lives in the one place a reader already looks for knowledge about that structure. This dissolves the usual false choice between a runtime that handles everything and a programmer who handles everything: the runtime takes the general case and the type that creates an exception carries it.

Delegation is not always available, and the criterion for when it works is worth stating precisely, because it is what keeps this from being an excuse. Delegation works when the uncovered case is identifiable at design time by the party that creates it. If the awkward situation can only arise between parties who do not know about each other, no local obligation can be written that covers it, and the incompleteness is real rather than delegable — at which point you owe the system the complete mechanism after all. And whichever way it comes out, the residual manual work should be named as manual work rather than described as a convenience. Its acceptability rests entirely on being confined to a few declared places, and a residual that is not written down stops being confined the moment someone forgets it is there.

**Source:** [The Smalltalk-76 Programming System: Design and Implementation](../works/the-smalltalk-76-programming-system-design-and-implementation.md) — the storage-management section, which rules out programmer-managed allocation as a certain source of errors and as clutter unrelated to the problem, selects reference counting over collection on the grounds that its computational overhead is more uniform in real time and its behavior with little free storage is better, concedes that cyclic structures fall outside what counting can reclaim, and answers that concession by defining, for each cyclic link field, an unlinking message on the class that propagates through the structure to break the cycle, while stating plainly that this amounts to explicit storage management made tolerable by being well localized.
