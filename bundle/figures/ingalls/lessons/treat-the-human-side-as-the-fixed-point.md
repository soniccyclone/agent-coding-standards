# Treat human cognition as the fixed constraint and the machine as the adjustable side, then design to the whole span between them

**Lesson:** Every interface between a person and a machine has two sides that could bend, and the choice of which one bends is usually made by default rather than on the merits. The argument for bending the machine is not sentimental, it is an argument about timescales: the apparatus people use to think and to communicate has been under refinement for an enormous span and will still be in service long after any particular architecture is scrap, whereas the machine's representations were invented recently and can be changed by a decision. Adapting the durable side to the disposable side is the expensive direction, and it has to be paid for again with every user and every year. This does not license copying human faculties into a machine, which is a different and mostly bad idea; it licenses using them as evidence about which models are sound, because a mechanism that has survived that long is unlikely to be arbitrary.

Taking this seriously enlarges what counts as language design. If the object is to connect the models in someone's head to the models in the hardware, then the design problem covers all four corners at once: what the person is holding internally, what the machine is holding internally, and the external medium that carries traffic in each direction. A grammar is one part of that span, and treating it as the whole is what produces a well-formed notation sitting on top of an alien storage model, reached through an interface nobody designed. The corollary is uncomfortable for anyone who wants clean divisions of labor: the way memory is organized, the way things are named, the way results are displayed, and the way input is sensed are all decisions in the same problem, and they can be got right or wrong relative to each other. A system built this way is genuinely hard to explain to someone holding the narrower definition, because the answer to "which of these things is it" is that the boundary they are assuming does not exist.

The transferable move is to look at any human-facing system and ask which of its awkwardnesses are demanded by the domain and which are the machine's internal convenience leaking outward. Anything in the second category is a design debt, whatever it costs to remove, because the side paying for it is the side that cannot be revised. And the same test explains why the traffic between components should be modeled on the traffic between people: most real communication rides on shared context and refers to it by allusion rather than restating it, so a scheme where each participant publishes what it will respond to and keeps the rest as unspoken background is not an analogy imposed on the machine, it is the arrangement that already works at the scale being imitated.

**Source:** [Design Principles Behind Smalltalk](../works/design-principles-behind-smalltalk.md) — the opening argument that human thought and communication mechanisms are long-engineered and should be respected as sound, with the explicit recommendation to make computer models compatible with the mind rather than the reverse; the Scope principle covering internal models, external media and interaction on both the human and computer sides; the observation that this is why the system resists explanation to people holding a narrower notion of a programming language; and the accompanying account of explicit versus implicit channels in human interaction.
