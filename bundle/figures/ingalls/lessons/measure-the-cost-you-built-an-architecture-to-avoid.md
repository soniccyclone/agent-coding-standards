# Measure the cost your architecture exists to avoid — the avoidance machinery is often the more expensive half, and deleting it deletes a family of problems

**Lesson:** Some structures exist only because an operation was assumed to be expensive: keep pools of reusable pieces sorted by size so the expensive consolidation can be postponed, and accept the accounting that entails. The assumption is usually made once, early, in the abstract, and then never tested — and when it is finally tested, the cost frequently turns out to be proportional not to the size of the system but to how much of it recently changed. If the expensive operation only ever touches the region that has been active, and the things needing repair afterwards are just the survivors in that region plus a set of references you were already recording, then both quantities are small under the empirical fact that most newly made things are discarded quickly. At that point the expensive operation is cheap enough to perform every single time, and the machinery built to avoid it costs more in its own bookkeeping than the thing it was avoiding. The reason to keep auditing assumed costs is that this particular kind of deletion does not remove a module, it removes a problem domain: with the pools gone, fragmentation, coalescing, and size-class policy all stop being questions you need any position on at all.

The same measurement then hands you a latency instrument. When the cost of an operation is proportional to what has accumulated since the last time you performed it, performing it far more often than necessary makes each occurrence proportionally smaller, and the sum stays modest while the worst case collapses. That argues for triggering on accumulated activity rather than on need — count the things made and act at a threshold, rather than waiting until space runs short — which converts an event whose timing is dictated by circumstance into one you schedule. Doing a little work often is not a compromise here; it is the configuration in which both the average and the maximum are acceptable, and it is available only because someone measured what the work is actually proportional to.

Two supporting judgements are worth copying. First, take your latency budget from the periods of the things the system must keep in step with — how often input is sampled, how often an audio buffer must be refilled, how often the display is repainted — rather than from a general aspiration to feel responsive. Those periods are facts about the environment, they tend to be commensurate with one another, and they yield a number you can engineer toward and afterwards verify. Second, a rare expensive fallback can be tolerated if its cost scales with the total size of the artifact and you have separately worked to keep that size small; discipline about footprint elsewhere is what buys the right to keep the policy here simple. That is why a periodic wholesale promotion that clears the decks is a good trade even though it theoretically brings the rare expensive event nearer: rare multiplied by small is not a number worth complicating a design for.

**Source:** [Back to the Future: The Story of Squeak](../works/back-to-the-future-the-story-of-squeak.md) — the storage-management section, which describes an initial strategy of sweeping objects onto size-segregated free lists in order to postpone consolidating compaction, then reports the finding that an incremental reclamation need only repair surviving new objects and the recorded root set, that both are few because most objects die young, that compacting after every incremental reclamation therefore proved cheaper than maintaining the free lists, and that eliminating the free lists removed fragmentation and coalescing as burdens in design, analysis and coding; together with its allocation-counter trigger that forces a small collection even when space is plentiful and yields collections of five to eight milliseconds, its threshold-driven wholesale tenuring that moves the boundary between old and new space to reduce future work while conceding an earlier onset of the rare full collection, its report of that full collection costing about a quarter second in a deliberately lean image, and its identification of ten milliseconds as the significant interactive threshold because pointer polling, sound buffer output and display refresh occur at commensurate rates.
