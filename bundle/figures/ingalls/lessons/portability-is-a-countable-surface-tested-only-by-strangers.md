# Portability is a countable surface, and only a stranger can test it: concentrate the host-specific part, and refuse host facilities to keep behavior identical everywhere

**Lesson:** Nobody can establish that their own system is portable. The proposition only gets tested when someone else moves it to a machine you have never used, without asking you anything, and the way to make that possible is structural rather than aspirational. Gather everything host-specific into one named, countable place and keep the ratio between it and the rest of the system as a figure you can quote — a couple of thousand lines of host interface against a whole engine is a surface a stranger can read in a sitting and finish in a few weeks, and several strangers will then do several machines in parallel without coordinating with you. The alternative failure is not usually a hard dependency but a diffuse one: platform variation scattered through the system as conditionals, host services reached for wherever they were convenient. That surface cannot be counted, so no outsider can estimate the work, so nobody attempts it, so you never learn whether the claim was true. Countability is the property that converts an intention into something falsifiable.

Keeping the surface that small requires refusing host facilities at points where using them would be free. Adopting the host's windows, drawing, and sound gets native behavior at no cost and puts an unbounded portion of your system's semantics outside your control, outside your inspection, and outside your ability to change. Building it yourself all the way down costs the native appearance and whatever acceleration the host had, and buys one artifact that behaves the same everywhere — identical to the point that stored state can be carried between machines unchanged. That last property deserves to be recognized as a verification asset rather than a convenience: a report from an unfamiliar platform reproduces on yours, and any difference in behavior between platforms is a defect to fix instead of a variation to accommodate. Systems built on host facilities give that up and are obliged to treat per-platform divergence as a permanent condition of life.

There is a matching rule for getting the ports back, and it follows the same logic of asking for exactly what you cannot supply yourself. Require that ports and modifications to the shared foundation be returned, and leave everything built on top of it unencumbered. The alignment is with who gains: whoever moved the system to a new machine already has the work in hand and benefits from your continued attention to the core they now depend on, while whoever writes an application on top gains nothing from compulsion and may be deterred by it. Small size serves the same end from the other direction — an artifact small enough to be read is small enough to be ported and changed, so footprint discipline is not merely deployment hygiene but the precondition for anyone outside your group being able to participate at all.

**Source:** [Back to the Future: The Story of Squeak](../works/back-to-the-future-the-story-of-squeak.md) — the overview's claim that every part of the system including graphics primitives and the engine itself can be examined and changed without encountering any other language, and that identical stored images run across the whole portability base; the code-size section's separation of the engine's Smalltalk from the roughly sixteen hundred lines of host-interface C that a port must alter, alongside footprint figures for a complete development environment inside a megabyte; the community section's account of unsolicited ports to a family of Unix platforms and to two Windows variants arriving within weeks of release, from people who had never made contact, and of the licence requiring ports and changes to the base library to be published freely while leaving applications built on top unrestricted; and the closing reflection preferring code identical across platforms to the industry's turn toward native host widgets.
