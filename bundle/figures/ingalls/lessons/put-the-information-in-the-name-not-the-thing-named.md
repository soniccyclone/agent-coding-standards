# When you control how names are handed out, put the information in the name instead of in the thing named

**Lesson:** If your system assigns its own identifiers, the identifier is an unused communication channel and almost everyone leaves it empty. The move is to partition the name space by whatever property you would otherwise have stored alongside each item, so that the property becomes a function of a few bits of the name. Then the per-item field disappears, and so does the table you would have consulted to recover it: the kind of a thing, its size, and its physical location can all be computed arithmetically from the way it was named. What you give up is the freedom to decide those properties later or change them, plus some quantization slack, because a partition scheme has to work in blocks and the blocks will not fit the actual sizes exactly. The trade is decided by counting, not by taste. When the typical item is a couple of dozen words long, one word of header on each is several percent of the entire store, and paying for it forever is a worse deal than the waste from rounding sizes up into coarse buckets.

The reason this is more than a space trick is that a single partition can be made to do several jobs at once. The grouping that lets you derive an item's kind from its name is also a grouping in the name space, and if you then arrange for it to be a grouping in physical storage as well, location becomes computable from the same bits, and the group becomes the natural unit of movement. That last consequence is where the largest win usually hides: freeing space by evicting a whole group means writing many items together and traversing the backing store in its own natural order, rather than chasing scattered individuals and paying a positioning cost for each. The general principle to carry off is that a partition of the name space costs something once and can be made to serve identification, placement, and reclamation together, so it is worth designing the partition around all three rather than adopting the obvious one for the first job that needs it.

Two smaller decisions in the same design deserve to be lifted out. First, the width a name needs is a consequence of how coarse the naming is: name whole aggregates rather than their individual words and the population of names you must distinguish shrinks by the mean aggregate size, which is what lets a modest reference width span a large store. Deciding the granularity of reference is therefore an addressing decision, not just a modelling one. Second, when you need a policy input like which items to discard, prefer the cheapest state that separates the cases you will actually act on. A single bit per item recording whether it has been touched since its group was last swept costs almost nothing and is a serviceable aging signal; tracking exact recency costs per-item storage and per-access work to support a distinction the policy will not use.

**Source:** [The Smalltalk-76 Programming System: Design and Implementation](../works/the-smalltalk-76-programming-system-design-and-implementation.md) — the virtual-memory section, in which references denote whole objects so that a sixteen-bit reference spans over a million words at the observed mean object size; class is recovered by indexing a table with a reference's high-order bits because address space is granted to each class in zones, eliminating a per-object class word; variable-length classes receive one zone per common small length and octave-ranged zones with an explicit length word above that; zones map onto contiguous backing-store locations so page and offset follow from the low-order bits of a reference; space is freed by purging an entire zone, writing many objects to one page and sweeping the disk in page order; and a single per-object bit recording a touch since the last purge of its zone supplies the aging criterion.
