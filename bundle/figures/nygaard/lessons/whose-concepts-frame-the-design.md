# Whoever Supplies The Vocabulary Has Already Decided The Design

**Lesson:** When a technical specialist and the people who will live with a system sit down together, the specialist arrives with a stock of concepts — flows, records, transactions, components, objects — and the others arrive with concepts about colleagues, obligations, and how the day actually goes. Nygaard's observation is that the first party's vocabulary reliably wins, not by argument but because it comes with the techniques, notations, and tools that make the meeting feel productive. Once only one set of terms can be used to state a problem, only the alternatives expressible in those terms remain visible, and the other party's grasp of their own work has been silently reclassified as anecdote.

He pushes this further with a claim that removes the comfortable exit: a development method is never vocabulary-neutral. It embeds assumptions about whether the interests of the parties involved fundamentally align, and those assumptions are almost never written down, so they must be inferred from the method's shape. Declining to declare your assumption does not put you outside the question; it enrolls you in whichever assumption is ambient, which in practice is the one that treats disagreement as a misunderstanding to be cleared up with better explanation. Silence is a position, just an unexamined one. The same corrective applies to the word "users": lumping everyone touched by a system under one label hides that some control its budget and objectives, some are required to operate it hour by hour, some meet it briefly by choice, and some absorb its effects without ever touching it. Statements about satisfying "the users" carry almost no information until you say which of these you mean, because their interests genuinely diverge.

A programmer who believes this changes how they run requirements work. They notice when a conversation has collapsed into their own notation and deliberately re-elicit in the other party's terms, treating the mismatch as data about the domain rather than as imprecision to be corrected. They ask who, specifically, a proposed capability serves, and expect the answer to name conflicting parties rather than a unified customer. And they read their own preferred method as an argument with a hidden premise, then state the premise out loud — because a premise you have named is one a colleague can attack, and one you have not is one that simply operates.

**Source:** [Program Development as a Social Activity](../works/program-development-as-a-social-activity.md) — the perspective-power discussion built on the analyst-and-two-colleagues scenario, the later taxonomy of affected roles and interest groups, and the contrast between the harmony and conflict readings of the same observed situation.
