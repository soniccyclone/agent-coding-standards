# Separate the predicate you reason with from the predicate a component can evaluate

**Lesson:** There are two things one can mean by saying a component knows something, and collapsing them ruins designs in both directions. The first is external, ascribed by whoever is analysing the system: the fact is settled by the component's local information, in the sense that it holds in every global situation consistent with what that component is holding. Nothing computes this. It is a property the analyst states about the system from outside, and it is valuable precisely because it is generous — it lets you prove that no protocol can decide before some round, or that a particular participant is not yet in a position to act, without any reference to what that participant could derive. The second meaning is internal: the component can actually produce the answer, from what it has, within the time it has. Only the second can appear in a branch condition.

The gap between them is not a technicality, and Vardi is unusually careful to name it. The framework hands you the generous notion first, and it comes with an assumption no real reasoner satisfies: that a participant extracts every consequence of its own information, including all consequences of consequences. The puzzle analysis exposes the seam in one sentence. The argument that a participant is in a position to conclude something rests only on the conclusion following from what it can see; a further and separate step is needed for it to announce the conclusion, and that step requires it to become aware of what its information implies. In an analysis that step is free. In a machine it is a search with a cost, and the cost can exceed the deadline the protocol imposes. A specification written in the generous notion can therefore be simultaneously true and unimplementable.

The right response is to keep both notions and label every use, not to discard the generous one. Impossibility results, lower bounds, invariants, and any argument of the form "this design cannot work" belong in the ascribed notion, because weakening the notion only weakens the theorem — if a participant does not know a thing even under the assumption of perfect derivation, no implementation of it will either. Everything a running component tests belongs in the computable notion, and the passage from the first to the second is a real obligation with its own proof rather than a change of typeface. The failure to watch for is a condition that reads like a local test but quietly quantifies over everything the global state permits: the assertion that is really a property of all reachable executions, the guard that would need to know what other nodes have observed, the precondition that is only decidable given information this node does not hold. Each of those is a correct claim in the wrong vocabulary, and the vocabulary is what decides whether it can be compiled.

**Source:** [Reasoning About Knowledge](../works/reasoning-about-knowledge.md) — the overview's explanation of why the multi-agent notion is described as *ascribed*, with its explicit disclaimers that no participant computes its knowledge and none is required to answer questions from it, together with the later statement that this external notion is insufficient once participants must act and that acting requires computing; and the closing remark of the muddy children analysis, which distinguishes a fact following from a child's information from the child being aware of the consequences of that information and able to act on it, and identifies the framework's implicit assumption of perfect derivation as the reason the two come apart.
