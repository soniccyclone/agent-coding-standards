# A law can hold by accident: quantify over interpretations before inferring the structure

**Lesson:** Structural characterizations come in pairs, and the two halves are not equally true. Constrain the shape of a system and a corresponding law becomes valid throughout it: that half is easy and holds. One then wants the converse — a system in which every instance of the law holds must have the shape — and Vardi shows it is false with a counterexample chosen to be as small and as embarrassing as possible. A two-state system whose accessibility relation is not even reflexive nonetheless satisfies every instance of the law that reflexivity is supposed to underwrite, because the vocabulary in play is too poor to expose the defect: the single available proposition happens to take the same value at both states, so no statement can detect that the actual state is being excluded from consideration. The law passes for want of a witness, not because the property is there.

The general shape of the error is worth internalizing, because it is not confined to modal logic. Checking a law against a system that comes with a fixed interpretation checks a conjunction of two things at once, the structure and the data, and a degenerate second factor can carry the whole conjunction on its own. The repair is to quantify the data away: keep only the shape, discard the assignment of meanings to symbols, and then the law holds under every interpretation the shape admits exactly when the shape has the property. That biconditional is the honest form of a structural characterization, and it says something immediately useful about testing. When a check passes, ask whether it would still pass under a richer vocabulary. A suite whose inputs never distinguish two code paths certifies nothing about the difference between them, and the deficiency there is not thin coverage of the input space but thin expressiveness of the input language — no quantity of indistinguishable inputs repairs it.

There is a constructive consolation attached, and it draws the line between what an accidental pass does and does not license. Such a system can be mechanically rebuilt over the same states into one that satisfies the laws honestly, and the rebuilt version is indistinguishable from the original by any statement the language can make. So the accidental pass was not a lie about behaviour, only about mechanism: the observable consequences were right and the reason was wrong. That is precisely the situation where a refactor is free and an inference is not. You may replace the accidental mechanism with the principled one at no observable cost, and you may not conclude from the law's holding that the mechanism was principled already. So whenever someone reports that a property holds everywhere in a system, the question is whether it holds because of the system's shape or because of the values that happen to be flowing through it. Only the first kind survives new data.

**Source:** [Reasoning About Knowledge](../works/reasoning-about-knowledge.md) — chapter three's discussion following the soundness and completeness theorems, with its two-state counterexample showing a structure that models the strongest axiom system while its accessibility relation fails even reflexivity; the observation that the near-converse is recovered at the level of frames, where the relations have the structural property if and only if the corresponding axiom is valid in every structure built on that frame; and the accompanying result that any accidental model can be effectively converted, over the same state space, into a structure in the intended class satisfying the same formulas at every state.
