# Aggregation composes for free along refinements, and for nothing else without a hypothesis

**Lesson:** Kolmogorov establishes three facts about averaging that together say when partial results can be combined. Averaging a quantity given two pieces of information and then averaging that result given only the first piece yields the same thing as averaging given the first piece alone — so a two-stage computation down a chain of increasing information agrees with the one-stage computation, with the unconditional average as the degenerate case where the first piece is nothing. Any factor that is already determined by the information being conditioned on passes straight through the averaging as a constant. And the finite version of the same law, the theorem on total probability, recovers a whole from a weighted combination of its parts. The direction matters: the two stages must be nested, the second coarser than the first. Then he shows the contrast. Exchanging averaging with differentiation, or with integration over a parameter, does *not* come free — each gets its own theorem with its own hypothesis, a uniform bound on the derivative in one case and on the quantity itself in the other, and each proof spends its effort exactly on the region where the bound is needed. Two operations that both look like taking a limit of sums are not therefore interchangeable.

The abstract content is a sharp line between two kinds of composition. Composing an aggregation with a coarsening of the grouping is structural: it holds because the coarser grouping is made of the finer one's groups, and it needs no side conditions, so hierarchical computation of aggregates is always available and always safe. Composing an aggregation with an operation of a different kind is a separate claim requiring a separate proof, and the proof will need a boundedness or uniformity hypothesis that you must actually check. Most reasoning errors about aggregation come from treating the second case as if it were the first — from a general sense that summarizing operations commute with each other. They do not. What licenses the free case is a specific relationship between the groupings, and when that relationship is absent, nothing is licensed.

For systems this is the correctness condition behind every rollup. Per-shard aggregates combine into a global aggregate exactly when the shard boundaries refine the reporting boundaries; per-hour into per-day works because hours nest inside days; a group-by can be computed in two passes when the first pass groups more finely than the second. Get the nesting backwards — precompute per-region and then report per-customer-tier when tiers cut across regions — and no combining function fixes it, because the intermediate result has already discarded the distinctions the final answer needs. The factoring rule is the other half of the same tool: a value that is constant within a group can be lifted out of the group's aggregation, which is what makes partial pre-aggregation and pushing computation to the shard sound rather than merely fast. And the contrast is the standing warning about interchanging a summary with anything else: an average of rates is not the rate of the averages, a median of merged streams is not any function of the per-stream medians, an average of a derivative is the derivative of the average only under conditions someone has to state. When you want to swap two summarizing steps, find the hypothesis that permits it or do not swap them.

**Source:** [Grundbegriffe der Wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung](../works/grundbegriffe-der-wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung.md) — Chapter V, §4, which proves that conditional expectation with respect to a pair of functions, further averaged with respect to one of them, equals conditional expectation with respect to that one alone, that the unconditional expectation is recovered by averaging the conditional one, and that a factor which is a function of the conditioning variable comes outside the conditional expectation; together with Chapter I, §4's theorem on total probability, and Chapter IV, §5's two theorems on interchanging expectation with differentiation and with integration over a parameter, each requiring its own uniform boundedness hypothesis.
