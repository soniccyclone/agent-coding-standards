# Checking every pair is not checking the whole, and the constraint count tells you how much you skipped

**Lesson:** Independence of several experiments is not one condition but a family of them, one for each way of picking outcomes from each experiment, and Kolmogorov does the bookkeeping: he counts how many of those equations are genuinely independent constraints rather than consequences of the others. With that count in hand the standard error becomes obvious. Pairwise independence of more than two events does not give mutual independence, and the counterexample is tiny — four equally likely outcomes, three events, every pair satisfying the product rule exactly, the triple failing it. The pairwise equations are a strict subset of the required ones, so discharging all of them leaves the rest entirely untouched.

The counting is the transferable part, not the counterexample. A genuinely n-ary property over n components imposes constraints indexed by subsets, and verifying only the two-element subsets clears the smallest layer of a structure whose bulk sits above it — and you can compute the ratio, which is usually humbling. What misleads people is that the inference *does* run in the other direction: mutual independence of n experiments really does imply it for any subcollection, so the property is inherited downward. Downward inheritance is not upward reconstruction. The two get confused constantly because the same word names both, and because checking the projections feels like progress toward the whole rather than orthogonal to it. Kolmogorov shows the same trap reappearing one size up: independence of every finite subfamily of an infinite family gives independence of the whole family inside the field generated by finite conditions, but can be lost in a richer field — so even "every finite subset checks out" is a conclusion whose scope depends on the ambient structure you are working in.

In engineering terms: pairwise integration tests between services are not evidence that the ensemble works. Locks that never deadlock two at a time deadlock three at a time. Feature flags that are each individually safe with the baseline are not jointly safe. Branches that each merge cleanly into the trunk do not merge cleanly with each other. When a property is genuinely n-ary the honest options are to test at the arity you actually care about, to find a structural argument that makes the property compositional so the pairwise checks *do* suffice, or to restate the property so that it really is pairwise. What you may not do is present pairwise coverage as support for the joint claim, because the number of constraints you did not examine is both enormous and calculable.

**Source:** [Grundbegriffe der Wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung](../works/grundbegriffe-der-wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung.md) — Chapter I, §5, which counts the independent equations required for mutual independence, proves the property is inherited by any subcollection, and then records that pairwise independence does not imply mutual independence for more than two events, with Bernstein's four-element example; extended in Chapter VI, §§1-2, where mutual independence of every finite group does not in general give independence of the whole family and the property can be lost in more inclusive fields.
