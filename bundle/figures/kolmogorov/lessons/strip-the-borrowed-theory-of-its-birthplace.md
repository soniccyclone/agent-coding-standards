# To found a new field on an existing theory, first strip that theory of the setting it was born in

**Lesson:** A domain that has resisted formalization for centuries usually does not need new machinery invented for it; it needs to be recognized as an instance of machinery that already exists. Probability was in that state — argued about informally while geometry and algebra had long since been axiomatized — and the move that settled it was noticing that an event's probability behaves like a set's measure, that expectation behaves like an integral, and that independence of variables behaves like orthogonality of functions. The whole subject then sits inside the theory of additive set functions, and the peculiar concepts stop being peculiar.

But spotting the correspondence is only half the work, and it is the half everyone does. Measure and integration as they stood could not actually carry probability, because they were still entangled with the geometric material they had been developed for — lengths, areas, points of a space. That entanglement had to be removed first, and only once measure theory had been restated without reference to any particular kind of underlying set could probability be identified with it. The general lesson is that a candidate abstraction is rarely reusable in the state its first application left it in. You have to de-particularize it: delete every mention of the original domain until the definitions speak only of operations and the laws they obey, and the elements they operate on can be anything at all. Kolmogorov is explicit that what the elements of his basic set represent is of no importance to the mathematics — exactly the stance that makes undefined "points" work in Hilbert's geometry and undefined elements work in the definitions of groups and rings.

There is a clean diagnostic for whether the de-particularization succeeded: the borrowed theory's existing results should transfer with no new work. Kolmogorov observes that the standard proofs about integrals, normally written for the case of Lebesgue measure on ordinary space, carry over to the abstract setting essentially word for word and raise no new mathematical problem. When the port is that quiet, you generalized along the right axis. When instead you find yourself reproving everything in the new setting, or discovering that half the old theorems need side conditions, you either generalized along the wrong axis or you carried the original domain's assumptions along without noticing. And the payoff for getting it right is that the abstraction serves cases you never had in mind: an axiom system freed from its motivating interpretation admits unboundedly many others, including applications in fields with no connection to what it was built for. An abstraction that still names its birthplace can only ever serve one.

**Source:** [Grundbegriffe der Wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung](../works/grundbegriffe-der-wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung.md) — the preface, which credits Lebesgue for the analogies between measure and probability and Fréchet for making measure and integration independent of the geometric elements Lebesgue kept in the foreground, together with the opening of Chapter I on developing the theory from axioms in the manner of Hilbert's geometry and abstract algebra, and the note at the start of Chapter IV that the usual integration proofs extend to the abstract case unchanged.
