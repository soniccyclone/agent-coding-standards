# An aggregate is predictable when no single input has much influence, not when it has many inputs

**Lesson:** Kolmogorov states the content shared by every theorem called a law of large numbers, and it is not about averaging or about sample size. It is that a quantity depending on many trials settles down precisely when its dependence on each individual trial is small. He then makes that measurable rather than rhetorical. For each trial, compare the expected value of the aggregate given the outcomes of the trials up to and including it against the expected value given only the trials before it; the mean square of that difference is a number, and it quantifies exactly how much that one trial moves the aggregate. The differences are pairwise uncorrelated, so their contributions add: the aggregate's variance is the sum of the per-trial influences. Convergence follows when that sum stays small relative to the tolerance you demand. Every special case falls out of this — the classical condition on the growth of summed variances, its extension to weakly correlated inputs where a decaying correlation bound is enough, and the observation that uniform bounds on the inputs suffice automatically.

The transferable structure is a decomposition rather than a theorem. To ask whether a quantity computed from many contributions is stable, you do not count the contributions or invoke a limit; you decompose the total deviation into one term per contribution, where each term is the revision the aggregate undergoes when that contribution becomes known, and then you look at the sum. This reframes stability as a statement about sensitivity, which is a local and checkable thing, instead of a statement about scale, which is neither. It also tells you the shape of the failure: a large sum is not a diffuse problem to be fixed by gathering more inputs, it means specific terms dominate, and those terms name the inputs whose behavior the aggregate is really tracking. Adding inputs helps only when the added ones carry little influence; a thousand contributions with one of them decisive is not an average of a thousand things, it is one thing wearing a disguise. Note also which structural assumption is doing the work and which is not: the argument needs only that per-trial revisions do not correlate, not that the trials are independent, which is why the same conclusion survives mild dependence.

The engineering reading is that any figure aggregated from many sources — a fleet-wide latency percentile, a scoring model's output, an availability number, a build's total runtime, a consensus round's outcome — is trustworthy in proportion to the influence bound of its largest single source, and in no proportion at all to the source count. So the diagnostic to run on such a figure is a leave-one-out sensitivity scan rather than a sample-size check: for each contributor, how far does the total move when that contributor's value is revealed or perturbed? Sum those movements and you have both a stability estimate and, ranked by magnitude, the list of things that actually determine your number. The habit pays off twice. It kills the false comfort that a metric averaged over enough machines must be well behaved, and it converts "this dashboard jumps around and we don't know why" into a finite, ordered list of suspects. The same decomposition is what tells you where sharding or replication genuinely buys robustness and where it only spreads a single point of dependence across more hosts.

**Source:** [Grundbegriffe der Wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung](../works/grundbegriffe-der-wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung.md) — Chapter VI, §3, where the general idea behind the law of large numbers is stated as small dependence of the aggregate on each separate trial, the per-trial dependence is defined as the mean square difference of successive conditional expectations, those differences are shown to be uncorrelated so that the aggregate's variance is their sum, and the resulting sufficient condition is derived; together with the same section's Tchebycheff condition on summed variances, its weakening for correlated variables with a decaying correlation bound, and the remark that uniform boundedness of the inputs suffices.
