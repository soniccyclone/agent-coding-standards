# A sufficient condition is unfinished work until you have tried to weaken it and failed on purpose

**Lesson:** Kolmogorov almost never leaves a sufficient condition standing alone. Having given a summable-variance criterion for the strong stability of running means, he adds that the criterion is the best available, and proves it in the strongest possible sense: for any sequence of constants whose sum diverges, one can construct inputs with exactly those variances whose running means fail to be stable. So the condition is not merely one that works, it is the boundary — every relaxation along that axis has a counterexample, and the counterexample is exhibited rather than conjectured. The same reflex appears throughout. His criteria for convergence in probability are stated as sufficient, then immediately upgraded to necessary under added hypotheses, with the hypotheses named. The variance condition for stability is shown to be necessary as well once the deviations are uniformly bounded. His independence criteria are worked into necessary-and-sufficient form, and the count of genuinely independent constraints among them is computed so that nothing redundant is being demanded. Where he cannot close the gap he says so, marking one claim as having no published proof yet.

The habit worth extracting is that a sufficient condition carries no information about how much of it you need, and that missing information is where the errors live. Anyone who later reads the condition has to guess whether it is tight, and will guess whichever way is convenient: treating a loose condition as tight means rejecting cases that were fine, and treating a tight condition as loose means shipping cases that are not. Closing the gap takes one of three forms, all cheap compared to the cost of the ambiguity. Prove the condition necessary. Prove it necessary under a stated extra hypothesis, which localizes the remaining slack. Or, when neither works, construct the adversary at the boundary — the instance that satisfies everything except the last increment of your condition and then fails — because that is what converts an assumption into a demonstrated edge. And when you cannot do any of the three, the honest record is to say the gap is open, which at least stops the next reader from assuming it was closed.

The engineering version is the unexamined precondition. A function documents that it requires a sorted input, a held lock, a warmed cache, a non-empty batch, an idempotent callback — and no one has ever established which of those it actually needs. Callers then contort themselves satisfying requirements that were never load-bearing, while the one requirement that was load-bearing gets dropped by whoever found it inconvenient, because from the outside all preconditions look alike. The corrective is to attack your own guards: for each one, remove it and find the input that breaks, and keep that input as a test. What you end up with is a precondition set where every element is justified by a named failing case, which is simultaneously the tightest contract, the documentation, and the regression suite. The same discipline turns a performance claim from folklore into knowledge — a bound you have only ever confirmed is a bound you do not know the shape of, whereas one you have deliberately tried to break tells you exactly where it stops holding.

**Source:** [Grundbegriffe der Wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung](../works/grundbegriffe-der-wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung.md) — Chapter VI, §5, where the summable-variance condition for normal strong stability of arithmetic means is declared best possible and justified by constructing, for any divergent series of prescribed variances, independent variables realizing them whose means are not strongly stable; together with §3's demonstration that the variance condition becomes necessary under uniform boundedness, Chapter IV §4's convergence criteria stated as sufficient and then shown necessary under named additional hypotheses, Chapter I §5's necessary-and-sufficient independence conditions with the count of genuinely independent equations among them, and the footnote in §5 admitting that one stated result has no published proof.
