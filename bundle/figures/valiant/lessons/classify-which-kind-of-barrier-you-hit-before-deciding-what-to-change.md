# Classify which kind of barrier you hit, because only some kinds can be engineered around

**Lesson:** "This cannot be done" is not one statement, and treating it as one wastes effort in both directions — people grind against walls that will never move, and people abandon problems that a change of encoding would have solved. The obstructions worth distinguishing are at least four. There is the accounting barrier: what you are trying to acquire has more degrees of freedom than the evidence you will ever see can pin down, so no procedure whatever suffices. There is the encoding barrier: the goal is reachable, but not while you insist on describing candidates in this particular way, because doing so forces the procedure to solve a problem believed hard. There is the intrinsic barrier: the family of goals is rich enough that reaching it by *any* description would imply solving a problem believed hard. And there is the channel barrier: the goal is perfectly learnable given the evidence, and the family is not too rich, but the shape of the feedback you have chosen to accept — aggregate scores instead of individual cases — cannot separate the candidates you must separate.

Each diagnosis licenses a different move. The encoding barrier is a genuine invitation to change representation, and only that barrier is. The channel barrier says to change what the process is permitted to observe, and no amount of representation engineering will substitute. The accounting and intrinsic barriers say stop, or redefine the goal, or subdivide it into stages. Getting the diagnosis wrong is the standard failure: a team that reads a channel limitation as an encoding limitation will iterate through representations forever, each attempt failing for the same reason, with nothing in the failures to indicate why.

Cutting across that classification is a second question with equal practical weight: does the barrier rest on an unproven assumption? Two of these obstructions are accounting arguments — the information available is simply insufficient — and they hold unconditionally. The other two are conditional on widely believed but unproven claims about computational hardness. The distinction matters because a conditional barrier is a statement about the state of knowledge as much as about the problem: it tells you that a breakthrough elsewhere would move this wall, and that your negative result has an escape clause someone might one day exercise. An unconditional barrier has no such clause. Recording which kind you have is part of stating the result, and a result whose conditionality is left unstated will be relied on as though it were absolute.

**Source:** [Evolvability](../works/evolvability.md) — the enumeration at the close of section 4 of four impediments to evolvability: the information-theoretic case where the complexity of the mechanism exceeds the number of experiences, the representational case exemplified by the preceding proposition on Boolean threshold functions where learnability by the given representation would imply an unlikely complexity collapse, the intrinsic case where the class is so extensive that learning it by any representation would imply an efficient algorithm for a problem believed hard, and the case exemplified by the parity result where no empirical test of a polynomial number of neighbors can guarantee convergence — together with the explicit note that the first and last of these require no unproven computational assumptions.
