# When a field renames itself every decade, check whether the substance moved at all

**Lesson:** One area of practice carried three successive fashionable names across roughly three decades, each arriving with the energy of a new discipline. The author's flat assessment is that through all of it the concept did not change: use the most capable hardware, the most capable programming systems and the most efficient algorithms available to attack problems in whatever domain has the data. The names turned over; the content did not.

The practical value of noticing this is defensive. Each rename presents itself as a discontinuity that obsoletes what you know and demands fresh study, and reacting to that framing costs real time — relearning vocabulary, re-reading introductions, discounting your own transferable experience. The test to apply is simply to ask what technical claim the new name makes that the old one could not. Where there is a genuine answer, the discontinuity is real. Where the answer is a list of the same activities in new words, you already have the competence, and the appropriate response is to translate the vocabulary and carry on.

There is a sharper version in the same discussion, and it is the more useful half. This field's oldest name was originally *pejorative* — statisticians used it, alongside an even blunter synonym, to accuse someone of extracting conclusions the data did not support. The term was later rehabilitated into a neutral description of respectable work. But the failure mode it was coined to name did not go anywhere; the very next section of the book is devoted to exactly that error and how to avoid it. So a term's change in connotation is not evidence about the underlying risk. When a word that used to mean "you are fooling yourself" becomes a job title, the thing it warned about is still available to do, and it is now harder to talk about because the warning has been absorbed into the label.

Both halves point at the same discipline: read a field's naming history as evidence about fashion and social standing, not as evidence about technical progress, and specifically look for whether a rename quietly retired a criticism that was never actually answered. Vocabulary drifts to become flattering. The error rates do not follow.

**Source:** [Mining of Massive Datasets](../works/mining-of-massive-datasets.md) — chapter 1's opening, which tracks the same concept through "data mining" in the 1990s, "big data" around 2010 and "data science" today while stating that the concept remained the same throughout; and the statistical-modeling subsection, which records that statisticians coined "data mining" (and "data dredging") as a derogatory term for attempts to extract information not supported by the data, that it has since taken on a positive meaning, and that the following section is devoted to the errors made by trying to extract what really is not there.
