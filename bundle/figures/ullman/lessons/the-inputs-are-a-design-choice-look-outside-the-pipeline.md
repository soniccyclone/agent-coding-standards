# The inputs are a design choice — look outside the pipeline

**Lesson:** Once a system has been framed as "given this input, produce that output," almost all subsequent effort goes into the transformation in the middle. The framing is rarely revisited, because it arrived with the problem statement and looks like a fact about the world. It is not; it is a decision somebody made early, usually by picking the most obvious representation. And a signal that is genuinely informative but absent from that representation cannot be recovered by any amount of work on the transformation. The ceiling is set by what came in, and it is often much lower than the ceiling the available information would allow.

So the highest-leverage question is frequently not how to process the input better but what else is knowable about each case. The productive move is to enumerate everything observable about the entity being judged, not just the part the current representation captures — where it came from, who produced it, when, through which path, what accompanied it, what happened afterwards. Some of that will correlate strongly with the answer for reasons that have nothing to do with the content you were staring at, and adding one such signal routinely beats every refinement of the model over the original one. The reflex worth building is: before improving the transformation, ask what it is not being told.

The same reasoning applies to the ground truth, which is the other input people treat as fixed. Authoritative answers seem to require deliberate manual production, which is expensive enough that scarcity is accepted as a constraint. But large quantities of labelling already exist as a byproduct of activity undertaken for entirely different reasons — curated directories, links between pages, ratings attached to reviews, categories people applied for their own convenience. Recognising one of these as a source turns a data-acquisition project into a data-discovery one. The cost is that the labels mean what their original activity meant, not quite what you want them to mean, so the mismatch has to be understood and stated rather than assumed away.

Both halves of this are the same habit: treat the boundary of the system as something you drew, and look over it. The transformation is where the interesting work feels like it is, and the inputs are where the ceiling actually is.

**Source:** [Mining of Massive Datasets](../works/mining-of-massive-datasets.md) — the feature-selection and training-set-creation discussions in the machine-learning architecture section, which observe that the hardest part is often deciding what features to use, suggest adding the originating host or address to a word-based spam representation because spam is generated by particular hosts, and catalogue implicitly labelled data already on the web — directory topic listings, links from a topic's encyclopedia page, and star ratings usable as sentiment labels.
