# When the quantity you care about is unobservable, instrument the field

**Lesson:** The thing you actually want to know about a deployed decision procedure is how well it does on the cases it has not seen, which is precisely the set you have no answers for. Every measurement you can take before deployment is a measurement on a sample chosen in the past, and it is an estimate of future performance only under the assumption that the future resembles that sample. Where the population is generated by people responding to your system — adversaries adapting, users changing behaviour, the world moving on — that assumption is not merely unproven, it is known to be false, and the estimate decays from the day it is taken.

The response is not a better offline measurement but a different kind of measurement: find a signal produced by the deployment itself that correlates with the errors you care about, and track it continuously. Complaints, corrections, reversals, retries, manual overrides, downstream failures — these are all partial and biased views of the error rate, and a biased view that updates is worth more than an unbiased one that is stale. Treat the offline number as a launch gate and the field signal as the operating instrument.

Being explicit about the bias in the field signal keeps it honest. A count of user-reported mistakes measures one direction of error and is silent about the other, and it is filtered through who bothers to report. So it is a trend line rather than an absolute rate: a sudden movement is informative, and the level is not comparable to your offline figure. That is usually enough, since what you need to know is whether the situation is degrading.

The architectural consequence is that a procedure facing a shifting population cannot be built once. Something has to consume the field signal and adjust — retraining on recent examples, reweighting toward recent behaviour, or at minimum alerting a human. That in turn favours designs that can absorb evidence incrementally over designs that require the whole history to be reprocessed, and it makes "how does this get updated" a first-class design question rather than an operational afterthought.

**Source:** [Mining of Massive Datasets](../works/mining-of-massive-datasets.md) — the generalization box in the machine-learning architecture section, which observes that the purpose of a classifier is to classify data whose class is unknown, that we often have no way of knowing whether it does so, and that when the nature of the data changes over time performance must be measured over time — with the rate of reported spam that was not classified as spam given as the example — together with the adjacent argument for on-line learning as the way to adapt as spammers change tactics.
