# The exact shape of the agreement you demand is the biggest lever you have, and its price is discontinuous

**Lesson:** "Everyone agrees" is not one requirement, it is a family, and the members have wildly different prices. Must the agreed values be identical, or merely within a tolerance you choose? Must everyone commit at the same moment, or is it enough that everyone eventually commits to the same thing? Must every honest participant be bound by the agreement, or may those who have already caught the sender in a contradiction be excused? Each of these is a small-sounding edit to the specification, and each moves the cost by a large, discontinuous step rather than by a percentage. Relaxing identical to within-a-tolerance moves the problem from provably unsolvable under asynchrony to solvable by successive averaging. Relaxing same-moment to eventually turns an unavoidable worst-case delay into a delay proportional to the failures that actually occurred. Excusing the participants who already have proof of a contradiction cuts the message cost far below what full unanimity demands.

The reason for the discontinuity is that these requirements are the exact points where impossibility arguments get their grip. Simultaneity forces every participant to know that everyone else is ready, which is a statement about the whole population and cannot be established faster than information propagates. Exactness forces a single point to be picked out of a continuum, which is what the classic asynchronous impossibility exploits. Universality forces conclusions to be shared even with participants holding evidence nobody else has. Weakening one of them does not shave the argument, it removes the hinge the argument turned on. Symmetrically, this is why the strengthenings people add casually are so expensive: asking that all replicas act at the same instant, when the application only needs them to act consistently, buys a worst-case cost that never goes away, even in runs where nothing fails at all.

So the first question in a coordination design is not which protocol to use, it is what the application genuinely requires, stated with this level of precision. A distributed commit needs everyone to reach the same verdict and does not care that they reach it at different times. A group of clocks or sensors needs values close enough for the downstream computation and does not care that they are equal. Actions that must be physically simultaneous do need simultaneity, and then the worst-case bill is real and should be planned for rather than wished away. The discipline is to write the requirement at the weakest point that still serves the application, before shopping for mechanism, because the choice is being made either way and defaulting to the strongest reading is how systems acquire costs nobody asked for.
