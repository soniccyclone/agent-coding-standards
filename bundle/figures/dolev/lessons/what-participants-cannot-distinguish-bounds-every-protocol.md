# What a participant cannot tell apart is the whole argument

**Lesson:** A distributed protocol can only act on what each participant can see, so every claim about it is at bottom a claim about which executions its participants can distinguish. Once that is taken seriously it becomes the organizing tool for both directions of reasoning. To show something is impossible, build a chain of executions in which each neighbouring pair is indistinguishable to at least one still-correct participant, and stretch the chain from an execution that must produce one answer to an execution that must produce the opposite. Every step of the chain is a single, small edit to the record of who sent what, chosen so that nobody who has to remain consistent could have noticed the edit. The contradiction falls out of the chain's existence rather than out of any argument about strategies. To show something is possible, work the same relation from the other side: arrange that any two participants who are behaving correctly hold views close enough to force the same conclusion, so that the space of distinguishable situations collapses to the space of acceptable outcomes.

The technique carries a modelling obligation that is easy to get wrong. An impossibility claim is exactly as broad as the representation it was proved in, and the natural temptation is to represent the protocols one already knows. If the record of an execution is coarse enough that message contents are opaque labels and the only structure is who could have seen what, then a bound proved there also binds protocols using devices the prover never imagined, including cryptographic ones. That coarseness is what let a round-count floor previously known only for plain message exchange be re-established for any exchange whatsoever, and the same representation then supported the finer, fault-count-sensitive bounds on stopping time. Coarse models are not weak models; they are the ones whose theorems survive later invention.

For a programmer the transferable question is not "what is true of the system" but "what can each part tell apart". Debugging a protocol means hunting for two runs a participant confuses; hardening one means adding a source of distinguishing power. Every synchrony assumption, every signature, every disjoint route, every sequence number earns its keep only by splitting cases the participant previously could not split. If a proposed addition does not change anybody's ability to discriminate between executions, it does not change what the protocol can guarantee, whatever it does for confidence.
