# Let the failure budget do the filtering, so no step ever needs to know which inputs were lies

**Lesson:** Given a bound on how many inputs can be corrupt, you can build combining steps whose output is provably sound no matter which of the inputs were the corrupt ones. The budget, not detection, does the work. This turns out to be a small family of moves that recur under different surfaces. Discard extremes by rank: throw away as many largest and as many smallest values as the budget allows, and whatever survives is guaranteed to lie inside the range of the honest inputs, because a liar can only pull the answer outward and there were not enough liars to reach that far. Require corroboration above the budget: refuse to treat a claim as established until more distinct parties assert it than could all be lying, and refuse to treat it as settled until a chain of distinct parties has passed it along. Filter by bounded explanation: instead of naming the culprits, look for a set of suspects no larger than the budget whose removal makes the surviving evidence consistent, and act on the evidence that avoids them.

What unites these is that each converts an epistemic problem into an arithmetic one. The step never asks whether a particular input is trustworthy, a question that has no answer, and it never accuses anyone. It asks only whether the pattern in front of it could have been produced by a bounded adversary, which is a counting question with a definite answer. That property is also what makes such steps composable and provable: the guarantee attaches to the operator itself and holds for any adversary behaviour, so it can be used as a lemma rather than re-argued case by case. And when the pattern in front of you could not have been produced within the budget, you have obtained real knowledge for free, as a by-product of the same arithmetic rather than through a separate detection subsystem.

The habit generalizes well past consensus. Any time you are merging redundant reports from sources you cannot individually validate — replicas, sensors, mirrors, retries, independent estimators — the choice is between building a trust-scoring apparatus that will eventually be wrong about a healthy source, and building a combining rule whose worst-case output is bounded by construction given an assumed corruption count. The second is smaller code, has a proof, and degrades in a way you can state in advance. It also forces the assumption into the open: the rule's parameters are the budget, so nobody can use the system without confronting how many simultaneous liars it was built for.
