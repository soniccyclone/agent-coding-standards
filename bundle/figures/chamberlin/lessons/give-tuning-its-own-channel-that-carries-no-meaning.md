# Give performance tuning its own channel, and admit nothing into it that carries information

**Lesson:** The usual framing pits control against abstraction: either users may name the physical mechanism and thereby become dependent on it, or they may not and lose the ability to tune. The design worked out here splits the difference along a line that turns out to be principled. Users get explicit, first-class statements to create and destroy the auxiliary structures that make access fast, and to say which ordering should govern physical placement — a real tuning interface, exercised freely at runtime. What they do not get is any way to mention those structures in a request. Every question is stated in terms of values and conditions only, and the machine picks which structure to use. The two channels are separate: one channel carries what you mean, the other carries how fast you want it, and no statement in the second can appear in the first.

The line between them is not arbitrary, and the criterion is worth stating as a rule: a structure may be exposed as a tunable knob if and only if it contains no information that is not already derivable from the data itself. Auxiliary orderings and value-matching pointer sets qualify, since they encode nothing new; dropping one loses no facts and changes no answers. That is exactly what makes them safe to create, destroy, and re-choose at will, and it is why the field reports could describe installations tuning a running system by adding and removing access structures without disturbing programs or users. The converse case shows the rule's teeth. A pointer structure whose meaning is supplied by the user rather than derived from values was deliberately rejected, on the grounds that the automatic chooser cannot understand it and therefore cannot use it — admitting such a structure would have quietly required users to navigate it themselves, collapsing the two channels back into one.

The failure mode this guards against is a specific and common one: a performance hint that gradually becomes load-bearing semantics. Once a request can name an index, some request will depend on the order it implies, and now the index cannot be dropped. A programmer who has internalized the split designs every tuning surface — cache configuration, partitioning, placement, parallelism, materialization — so that removing the tuning changes only timing, never results, and treats any tunable whose removal changes an answer as a modeling mistake rather than a knob. The practical test is brutal and quick: delete the tuning declaration and ask whether any behavior other than speed changed.
