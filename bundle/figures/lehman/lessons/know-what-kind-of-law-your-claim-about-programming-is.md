# Know what kind of law your claim about programming is, and hold it with the confidence that kind deserves

**Lesson:** Claims about how software development behaves are not all the same species of claim, and the useful discipline is to place a claim on the spectrum before deciding what it entitles you to. At one end sit statements with the precision of physics. At another sit regularities in living systems, where the constituents are not consciously choosing but the aggregate is still lawful. Further out sit the regularities of economics and social behavior, where the constituents are choosing and the regularity is correspondingly loose. Statements about the evolution of large programs turn out to land, surprisingly, nearer the biological end than the social one — closer to lawful than the fact that every change is a deliberate human act would lead you to expect. Two forces explain that: the machine underneath is utterly literal, so nothing but a correct edit will do and every discrepancy generates real work; and the accumulated mass of code, documentation, habits, and practices supplies inertia that smooths what would otherwise be noise.

Placing a claim on this spectrum tells you how to hold it. Such regularities cannot be predicted at the level of the individual event — no law of this kind tells you what any particular person will decide next week — and their content is entirely about gross behavior over time. That is not a defect; it is the resolution at which the claim exists. Treating a statistical regularity as if it licensed pointwise prediction discredits it; treating it as worthless because it cannot predict pointwise throws away the only guidance available.

The method that produces such claims is also worth adopting directly. Observe the phenomenon and measure it; then look for models and explanations in terms of something more basic than the phenomenon itself; then devise measurements and experiments that could confirm, revise, or reject those explanations, and go around again. Crucially, the absence of a good definition or a settled metric is not grounds for ignoring something you can plainly see happening. Concepts and measures get sharpened by the investigation, not before it — waiting for a rigorous metric before admitting a phenomenon is real is a way of never learning anything.

Finally, this class of claim is reflexive in a way physical law is not. Because the regularities arise from human and organizational behavior, an organization that comes to understand them may change the behavior that generated them, so a law can eventually be invalidated by being learned. The right posture is to treat these regularities as binding on present practice — ignoring them is expensive — while expecting that improved structure, methodology, and habits could one day make some of them obsolete. A programmer who holds claims this way avoids two failure modes: dismissing empirical regularities because they are not theorems, and treating them as immutable when they are actually properties of a practice you might be able to change.
