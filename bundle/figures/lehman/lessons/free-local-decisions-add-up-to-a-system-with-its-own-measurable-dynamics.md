# Free local decisions aggregate into a system with its own measurable dynamics — plan against the measurements, not the intent

**Lesson:** Every choice in a long-lived project looks like a free choice made by a person: this feature next, this deadline, this many people. Yet when you measure the release histories of real systems — sizes, how much of the system each release disturbs, work rates, intervals between releases — the aggregate does not look free at all. It shows trends, invariances, and the ripple of a feedback-stabilized loop. The reason is not mystical. Individually the decisions are local and appear independent, but they are coupled through the code, the organization, the users' habits, and the accumulated weight of earlier decisions, and that web of couplings has dynamics of its own. Early on, the people genuinely steer; as the system ages, the constraints they inherit do most of the steering, and the aggregate behavior belongs to the process rather than to anyone's intention.

Two of the observed invariances are especially uncomfortable. Overall work output tends to hold steady over a project's active life, which implies that beyond some saturation point additional people and money buy no additional output — resources get attracted by the visibility of the commitment, not by their marginal usefulness. And release-to-release content holds roughly steady too, because the effort and time needed to absorb a change grow disproportionately with its size, so the team's capacity to genuinely understand what it is doing bounds how much it can safely do at once. Both say that the safe throughput of an evolving system is a property you measure, not a number you choose.

The practical method that falls out is to treat the process as an empirical object. Keep the history. Derive the system's own envelope from it — typical growth per release, typical fraction of the system disturbed, sustainable change rate, the pattern of intervals. Then read a proposed plan against that envelope. A plan calling for several times the historical growth is not ambitious, it is a claim that the dynamics have changed, and that claim needs evidence before it is believed. In the same spirit, an oddly low estimate of how much existing code a new component will disturb is not good news; against a stable ratio observed across many unrelated changes, it is a signal that the component has been designed as though it were free-standing when the surrounding structure will not permit that.

A programmer or planner who works this way argues about schedules with distributions rather than with conviction, and can say concretely why a plan will produce a poor-quality release and what the recovery will cost. They will also accept the unglamorous recommendation the data implies — split the work, insert a clean-up release first, ship the risky component to a limited audience — because the alternative is not avoiding those costs but paying them later with interest.

**Source:** [Programs, Life Cycles, and Laws of Software Evolution](../works/programs-life-cycles-and-laws-of-software-evolution.md) — the laws concerning self-regulating process dynamics, invariant work rate, and conservation of familiarity, plus the worked release-planning critique of an operating system's twentieth release that applies them to a concrete plan. Also [On Understanding Laws, Evolution, and Conservation in the Large-Program Life Cycle](../works/on-understanding-laws-evolution-and-conservation-in-the-large-program-life-cycle.md) — its commentary on the third and fourth laws, which argues that a system's dynamics are largely fixed during its conception and early life, that arbitrary targets are therefore not ours to set, and that the invariances can be exceeded only temporarily and at a stated cost. Also [Metrics and Laws of Software Evolution - The Nineties View](../works/metrics-and-laws-of-software-evolution-the-nineties-view.md) — which finds the same growth trend and ripple in an unrelated commercial system two decades later, and observes that the trend's character is fixed within the system's first several releases.
