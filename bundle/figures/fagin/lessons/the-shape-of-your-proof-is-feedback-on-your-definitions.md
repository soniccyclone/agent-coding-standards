# The shape of your proof is feedback on your definitions

**Lesson:** Two well-known decomposition theorems in early database theory had been proved by case analysis over the behavior of a mechanical procedure, and Fagin describes the results as having seemed somewhat fortuitous. Once the equivalence between dependencies and a logical fragment is in hand, those theorems drop out as immediate corollaries, and he says plainly that he considers the equivalence a more enlightening statement than the theorems it explains. The original proofs were not wrong; they were symptoms. A result that arrives through enumeration of cases, and lands looking like a coincidence, is usually a shadow of a simpler fact stated at the wrong level.

The same diagnostic shows up in his later work. An earlier existence proof for a certain canonical structure is described as long, and, more importantly, as using a technique that would not extend to the next class of dependencies anyone cared about. Fagin's replacement isolates a single abstract closure property, proves the general result from that property alone, and thereby covers all the previously handled cases plus the ones the old technique could not reach. Fragility under generalization is the tell. If a proof breaks when the hypothesis is widened slightly, the proof is probably leaning on an accident of the specific setting rather than on the reason the theorem is true. The update-semantics work supplies the sharpest version of the diagnostic, running it deliberately rather than in hindsight: faced with two quantities that both ought to be kept small, the authors write down the even-handed definition that minimizes both at once, prove a short lemma showing it forces a degenerate answer, and let that lemma dictate an asymmetric definition ranking one quantity ahead of the other. The negative result is not a setback in the argument, it is the instrument that chooses the definition.

So proofs carry two kinds of information: whether the claim holds, and how well you have carved up the domain. Length, case-bashing, unexpectedness, and refusal to generalize are all evidence about the definitions rather than about the difficulty of the subject. The engineering translation is familiar. When a correctness argument for a piece of code requires walking every branch, when a bug fix feels lucky, when a proof or test suite has to be rewritten from scratch to cover an adjacent case, the right response is to go back and change the decomposition rather than to write the argument more carefully. A programmer who reads proof shape this way uses difficulty as a design signal instead of a measure of their own effort.
