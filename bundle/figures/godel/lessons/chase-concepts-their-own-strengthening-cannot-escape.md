# The concept worth having is the one its own strengthening cannot escape

**Lesson:** Gödel singles out computability as the first notion in this territory that got pinned down without reference to any particular formal apparatus, and he is precise about what makes it special. Almost every neighbouring notion — derivable, definable — can only be fixed relative to a chosen language, and once you fix it, the diagonal construction produces something outside it, forcing a next level, and the next, and so on. Computability does not behave that way. Diagonalizing does not step outside it; there is no need to index it by order or level; a function computable in any system that contains arithmetic is already computable in arithmetic, and even moving to systems of transfinite order adds nothing. The notion is closed under its own strengthening, which is what earns it the description absolute — it names something in the subject matter rather than something about the notation you happened to pick.

Treat this as a test, because that is how Gödel uses it. Given a candidate concept, take the most natural way of making it stronger — add the level above, admit a truth predicate for the current language, apply the diagonal argument that generated the previous level — and ask whether the result lands inside the concept or outside it. Landing outside means you have not defined a concept, you have defined a stage of one, and the honest name for it includes the stage. Landing inside is strong evidence the concept was discovered rather than invented, because it survived a change of the machinery used to state it.

This is directly useful in programming, where the distinction is routinely blurred. A great many notions we treat as fundamental are silently indexed by a formalism: "type-safe" means type-safe in this type system, "verified" means verified against this checker, "well-formed" means accepted by this parser, "reachable" means reachable in this model. Strengthen the formalism and each of these shifts, which is fine as long as everyone knows the index is there. Trouble comes from arguing about them as if they were absolute — from treating "correct" as a property of a program rather than a property of a program relative to a stated model. The other side of the same test tells you which concepts you can build on without fear: computability, cardinality of a data set, the algebraic laws a structure satisfies, the observable behaviour of a process. These do not move when you re-express them, so an argument that rests on them stays valid across changes of language, framework, and toolchain.

The practical instruction is to look for the formalism-independent characterization every time you find yourself with several definitions of the same idea. If they all agree, you have probably found something real and should build on it. If they stratify, name the stage explicitly and stop pretending the concept is the same one across levels.

**Source:** [Remarks before the Princeton Bicentennial Conference on Problems in Mathematics](../works/remarks-before-the-princeton-bicentennial-conference.md) — the opening argument that computability is the first absolute definition of an epistemological notion, contrasted with the level-relative character of demonstrability and definability, and the observation that the diagonal procedure fails to lead outside it. Also [On the Length of Proofs](../works/on-the-length-of-proofs.md) — its remark added in proof, that computability in any of the systems in the tower, or even in one of transfinite order, collapses back to computability in the base, whereas the other metamathematical notions depend essentially on the system chosen. The same test is applied to an argument rather than a concept in [The Consistency of the Axiom of Choice and of the Generalized Continuum-Hypothesis](../works/consistency-of-the-axiom-of-choice-and-generalized-continuum-hypothesis.md), whose closing paragraph notes that the consistency result survives strengthening the base theory with large-cardinal assumptions, and takes that survival as the reason to call it absolute in some as-yet-unspecified sense. A footnote added to [The Consistency of the Continuum Hypothesis](../works/the-consistency-of-the-continuum-hypothesis.md) sharpens the same point into a criterion: the consistency argument extends to stronger axioms of infinity precisely because such axioms imply their own reinterpreted form.
