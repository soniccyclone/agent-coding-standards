# Re-express every notion inside the sub-world, prove one by one which ones survive, and expect all the interesting behaviour from the ones that don't

**Lesson:** Half of Gödel's monograph is a bookkeeping exercise that looks tedious and is the real technical content. Having built a restricted world sitting inside the full one, he takes each concept the theory has defined — membership, inclusion, pairing, function application, ordinal, successor, the orderings, the generating machinery itself — and forms a second version of it whose definition has been rewritten so all its quantifiers and variables range over the restricted world only. Then, for each one, he asks whether the rewritten version picks out the same thing as the original, and proves the answer. Along the way he establishes the compositional rule that makes this affordable: a notion assembled by substituting notions already shown to survive into an operation already shown to survive is itself safe, so the audit proceeds bottom-up over the definition graph rather than restarting for each concept. He is also careful to record that the rewritten version might not even exist before the restricted world is known to satisfy the theory's axioms, and that two definitions that were interchangeable outside can come apart inside.

The moment worth studying is where the audit stops. Several notions cannot be shown to survive, and Gödel says so plainly instead of gliding past it. Those are not embarrassments; they are the mechanism. Because a certain collection-forming operation means something narrower inside the restricted world than outside, that world contains fewer subsets than the ambient universe thinks it should, and it is exactly this shortfall that makes the target statements about sizes come out true there. If every notion had transferred unchanged the sub-world would be indistinguishable from the whole and the proof would establish nothing. So the pattern is: audit everything, expect most things to transfer, and locate your leverage precisely in the few that do not.

Any programmer who has built one system inside another has done a sloppier version of this. Running a language inside an interpreter, a program inside a sandbox, a workload inside a simulator, a query against a materialized subset, a contract inside a virtual machine, a test suite against a fake: in each case some operations mean exactly what they meant outside and some quietly mean something narrower — the clock, the set of visible files, the reachable hosts, the available precision, the set of existing keys, the exceptions that can be raised. Bugs in such systems concentrate almost entirely in the second group, and they are hard to find because the notation for the two groups is identical. The discipline is to enumerate the interface, decide per operation whether it is faithful, do the deciding compositionally so the list stays finite, and keep the unfaithful ones written down. Two payoffs follow. Behaviour that differs between inside and outside has to trace to something on the list, which turns debugging into lookup, and the deliberate uses of the sub-world — its determinism, its bounded resources, its restricted visibility — are exactly the entries on that same list, now being used on purpose rather than tripped over.

**Source:** [The Consistency of the Continuum Hypothesis](../works/the-consistency-of-the-continuum-hypothesis.md) — the chapter defining relativization and absoluteness of notions, operations and variables together with the compositionality rule for absoluteness, the long sequence of individual absoluteness results running through the pairing, ordinal and generating machinery, and the explicit remarks that certain concepts including the universal class and the power-class operation cannot be shown absolute.
