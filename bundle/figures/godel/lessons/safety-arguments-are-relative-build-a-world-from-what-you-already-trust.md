# You cannot show a rule is safe in the absolute; you can only build a world out of what you already trust and check the rule holds there

**Lesson:** Faced with a contested rule that people had argued about on its merits for decades, Gödel does not argue about the rule. He does not try to show it is true, and he does not try to show it is harmless by inspecting what follows from it. He builds a structure using only the machinery already accepted, shows that the contested rule is satisfied inside that structure by construction, and concludes that anyone who trusts the old machinery has no grounds left for fearing the new rule. The claim delivered is deliberately weaker than the one people wanted: not that the rule is safe, but that it is no less safe than what was already in use. That weaker claim is the only kind available, and recognizing this is what lets him actually deliver it.

The pattern is the general shape of a soundness argument for anything added to an existing system: a new type rule, an optimization the compiler is now allowed to perform, a relaxed isolation level, an escape hatch in a memory-safe language, a rewriting rule in an evaluator. The wrong instinct is to reason forward from the addition, enumerating what it now permits and hunting for something bad. That search never terminates and its failure proves nothing. The right instinct is to exhibit an interpretation: a restricted reading of the whole system, built from primitives you already believe in, under which the new rule is automatically valid. If such a reading exists, then any disaster reachable with the new rule was already reachable without it, and you have converted an open-ended search into a finite construction. Notice the direction of the reduction — you are not making the new thing trustworthy, you are pinning its trustworthiness to something whose trustworthiness you have already decided to assume, and being explicit that the assumption is doing the work.

Gödel adds a second condition that is easy to skip and is the part with the most engineering value. His argument is constructive: it does not merely assert that trouble in the extended system implies trouble in the base, it gives a procedure that takes the former and produces the latter. That is the difference between a safety claim you can only believe and one you can debug against. When a failure shows up in the extended system, the argument hands you a specific failure in the trusted core, which means the failure is either genuinely in the core, or your construction was wrong, and you can tell which. A relative-safety argument that lacks this transfer step gives you nothing to do when something breaks. So the discipline is two-part: state precisely what you are assuming, and make the reduction effective enough that a violation on the outside can be mechanically turned into a violation on the inside.
