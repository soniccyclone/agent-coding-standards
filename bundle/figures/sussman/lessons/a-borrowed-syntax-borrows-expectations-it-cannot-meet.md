# A notation that resembles a more powerful one will be used as though it were that one

**Lesson:** Borrowing an existing syntax for a restricted language is usually the right move — it costs nothing to parse, everyone can read it, and the tooling already exists. The bill arrives in the form of expectations. Readers do not learn a notation from its specification; they learn it from what it looks like, and a restricted language dressed in the clothes of a general one will be assumed to have the general one's capabilities. The specific and predictable error is composition: in the host language an operation's result can feed straight into another operation, so someone writes that, and in the restricted language it is not expressible at all because each operation must draw its inputs directly from named storage. Nothing in the appearance of the text signals the restriction. Only the semantics enforce it, and only at the moment somebody tries.

The honest way to state the trade is that syntactic familiarity and semantic honesty pull in opposite directions here. A notation invented for the restricted language would look unfamiliar and would thereby make no promises it could not keep; the borrowed notation looks familiar and makes several. Neither choice is wrong, but the cost of the borrowed one is real and lands on people who did not make the choice, in the form of constructions that seem obviously fine and are illegal for reasons that live somewhere other than the page.

What to do about it follows from where the misunderstanding comes from. The restriction is not discoverable from examples, so it has to be stated where the notation is introduced, in terms of what the constructs may contain rather than in terms of what they mean — the useful sentence is the one saying an operation's inputs may be only stored values and constants. Better still, make the checker reject the illegal composition with a message naming the rule, because in a borrowed notation every rule that is not enforced will be assumed absent.

The general shape of this is worth carrying beyond language design. It applies whenever a familiar container is reused for an unfamiliar purpose: a configuration format hosting a restricted expression language, a general-purpose language hosting a builder whose calls must occur in a particular order, a data format hosting a schema with constraints the format cannot express. In each case, the host's shape advertises the host's freedom, and every restriction you add is invisible until violated. The design question is whether the borrowed familiarity is worth the misplaced confidence, and it often is — but it should be answered rather than assumed, and answering it means writing down the restrictions that the appearance conceals.

**Source:** [Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs](../works/structure-and-interpretation-of-computer-programs.md) — chapter 5 section 5.1.1, the discussion of the disadvantages of the second register-machine notation, which observes that because the controller instructions in a machine definition look like Lisp expressions it is easy to forget that they are not arbitrary Lisp expressions, that they can notate only legal machine operations, and gives as the specific example that operations can operate directly only on constants and the contents of registers and not on the results of other operations; together with the section's other stated disadvantage, that inlining the data-path definitions at each mention makes the description verbose for large machines and obscures the actual data-path structure so that it is not obvious how many registers, operations and buttons there are or how they are interconnected — and the authors' explicit decision to accept both costs because the chapter is more concerned with controllers than with data paths, while noting that data-path design is crucial in designing real machines.
