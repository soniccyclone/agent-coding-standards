# Split a computation along the line where its inputs arrive at different times

**Lesson:** A procedure that takes two things is often really a procedure that takes them at two different times. One argument is fixed and reused — a query, a pattern, a program, a schema, a configuration — while the other varies across many invocations. Written in the obvious way, every invocation redoes the entire computation, including the part that depended only on the argument that never changed. The fix is a change of shape rather than of algorithm: make it a procedure of the stable argument alone, whose result is a procedure of the varying argument. The stable part's work happens once, at the moment the closure is built, and every later call pays only for what genuinely depends on the new input.

The discipline it imposes is the valuable part, because the split forces you to say, for each piece of work, which argument it actually depends on — a question the interleaved version lets you avoid answering. Classifying an expression, extracting its parts, deciding which rule applies: all of that depends on the program alone and belongs before the boundary. Looking up a name's value depends on the environment and must stay after it. Once you are looking for the line, you find work sitting on the wrong side of it that nobody would have noticed otherwise, and each piece moved across is multiplied by every future invocation.

The version of this that people miss is that *control structure* is work too. It is natural to move the analysis of each element of a sequence to the early phase and then, at execution time, loop over the analyzed pieces, calling them in turn. But that loop — testing for the end, taking the next one — depends only on how many pieces there are, which was known early. Compose the pieces into a single chained procedure while you still have them, and the traversal disappears from the runtime entirely; the individual expressions were analyzed either way, but only the second version has analyzed the *sequence*. Generalize: any iteration whose trip count and shape are determined by the stable argument can be unrolled into structure at the boundary, so the per-invocation cost has no loop in it at all. Whenever you have staged a computation and still see a loop over the pre-computed parts, you have not finished staging it.

This is also the honest account of what separates an interpreter from a compiler, and it is worth seeing that the difference is a matter of degree rather than of kind. Push more of the program-dependent work before the boundary, and let the thing produced at the boundary be more specialized and less like a general mechanism, and you slide continuously from one to the other without ever changing the meaning of the language. The same move applies far from language implementation: a regular expression compiled once against many subjects, a prepared statement, a partially applied validator, a plan computed from a schema. The question to ask of any hot path is not how to make it faster but which of its inputs was already known, and how long ago.

**Source:** [Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs](../works/structure-and-interpretation-of-computer-programs.md) - chapter 4 section 4.1.7 on separating syntactic analysis from execution, which observes that the evaluator re-analyzes the syntax of an expression on every execution and that this repeated analysis is expensive and wasteful, splits evaluation into an analysis procedure taking only the expression and returning an execution procedure taking only the environment, notes that analysis of a procedure body happens once even though the resulting procedure may be applied many times while variable lookup must remain in the execution phase because it depends on the environment, and records in a footnote that this technique is an integral part of compilation; together with Exercise 4.23, which contrasts a sequence analyzer that loops at execution time over the analyzed pieces against one that composes them pairwise during analysis, with the explanation that in the looping version the individual expressions have been analyzed but the sequence itself has not, and asks the reader to compare the two for sequences of one and two expressions; and the footnote to the variable case pointing forward to determining a variable's position in the environment structure during analysis so that no search is needed at run time.
