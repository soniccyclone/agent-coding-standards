# Preserving more than is live is always correct, which is exactly why the waste never announces itself

**Lesson:** Any time a shared resource is temporarily repurposed — a register reused by a subcomputation, a buffer handed to a callee, a global reset for a nested operation — there is a question of what must be preserved and put back. The safe rule is to preserve everything, and safety here is genuine: over-preserving cannot produce a wrong answer. That is the trap. A discipline whose errors are one-sided gives you no feedback at all on the side that is merely wasteful, so the waste is not a bug that will surface under test, in review, or in production. It will simply be there, permanently, in proportion to how conservative the original author was feeling.

The correct rule is smaller and requires knowing something the safe rule lets you avoid knowing: preserve exactly what will be consulted after the subcomputation returns. A value that is going to be entirely replaced by the subcomputation's result needs no preservation, because nothing after the return reads the old contents. Working this out is per-site analysis — the same location may need preserving at one call and not at another, depending on what the surrounding code does next — and the analysis is a liveness question, not a property of the location or of the routine being called.

There is a real alternative to preserving that is worth checking before allocating storage: if the new value determines the old one, you can recompute rather than remember. This is genuinely cheaper when it applies, and it applies less often than it seems to — a transformation being easy to invert in one particular instance is not a general licence, because a location's previous contents cannot in general be derived from its current contents. The test is specific and local, and getting it wrong produces a subtle wrong answer rather than a slowdown, so the asymmetry runs the opposite way from over-preservation and deserves correspondingly more care.

The general lesson is about the shape of the feedback rather than about registers. Whenever a discipline's failure mode is one-sided — the conservative choice is always correct and the aggressive one is sometimes wrong — every incentive pushes toward the conservative choice, and the accumulated cost of that choice is invisible because nothing is ever wrong. Defensive copies, over-broad locks, cache invalidation that clears too much, transactions held longer than needed, retries of operations that did not fail: all have this structure. Such places do not get better on their own and are not found by looking for bugs. They are found only by someone deliberately asking, of each conservative act, what would actually break if it were omitted.

**Source:** [Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs](../works/structure-and-interpretation-of-computer-programs.md) — chapter 5 section 5.1.4, the recursive factorial machine, where the argument register and the return-point register are saved before each recursive call and restored on return, but the result register is explicitly not saved because its old contents are not useful after the subroutine returns and only the value produced by the subcomputation is needed; the general statement that the return-point register must always be saved while whether other registers need saving depends on the particular machine, since not all recursive computations need the original values of registers modified during the subproblem; the footnote considering whether the argument register could be restored by inverting the decrement instead of being saved, and concluding that although this works for factorial it cannot work in general because the old value of a register cannot always be computed from the new one; and Exercise 5.6, which observes that the Fibonacci machine's controller sequence contains one save and one restore that can be removed to make a faster machine and asks the reader to locate them. Also section 5.1.3's note that when several subroutines share one return-point register, a subroutine calling another must save that register first or it will not know where to go when it finishes.
