# Design by choosing the weakest formalism that still says what you need, because weakness is what buys mechanical treatment

**Lesson:** When a notation is being designed, the instinct is to ask what it should be able to express and to keep adding until the answer is everything. The better framing is a two-sided constraint: strong enough to describe what you actually need to describe, and weak enough that a machine can be given a controllable, predictable interpretation of it. Those pull against each other, and the design is the point chosen between them rather than the maximum of the first. Expressive power is not the objective; it is one of two things being traded, and it is the one that is easy to want and hard to give back.

The reason weakness pays is that everything mechanical you might want from a notation — deciding things about it, optimizing it, running it in more than one direction, reordering it safely, reasoning about whether it terminates — is a claim about all the things the notation can say. Every construct added expands that set, and a construct that expands it past some threshold does not merely fail to help the analysis, it destroys analyses that were working for everything else. This is why a small addition can feel free and cost enormously: the price is not paid at the construct, it is paid by every guarantee that quantified over the language.

Two habits follow. First, when an extension is proposed, ask what property of the whole formalism it breaks, not what it enables — and be willing to notice that a genuinely useful facility can be too expensive at that price, which is a real and legitimate answer. Second, when a formalism has already lost a property because of a small number of constructs, know precisely which constructs those are, since removing exactly them recovers a well-behaved sublanguage that can be given the stronger treatment. A language honest about which of its constructs are the well-behaved core is far more useful than one that pretends uniformity, because tools can then serve the core and be conservative about the rest.

The framing also disciplines the perpetual argument about how much power a configuration language, a query interface, a policy language, or a template system should have. The argument is usually conducted as though more capability is straightforwardly better and restrictions are failures of ambition. It is the reverse: the restriction is the feature, because it is what lets anything other than the runtime understand the artifact. The only question worth arguing is whether the chosen fragment covers the cases you actually have, and that is an empirical question about your workload rather than a philosophical one about power.

**Source:** [Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs](../works/structure-and-interpretation-of-computer-programs.md) — chapter 4 section 4.4.3, which describes the aim of logic programming as decomposing a computational problem into what is to be computed and how, accomplished by selecting a subset of the statements of mathematical logic powerful enough to describe anything one might want to compute yet weak enough to have a controllable procedural interpretation, so that a program is simultaneously an effective computation whose order of clauses and subgoals the programmer arranges for efficiency and a statement whose result follows from the laws of logic; together with the footnote conceding that the language as implemented fails to be a sound fragment of logic precisely because of two constructs, its negation and its escape into host predicates, that a language consistent with mathematical logic could be obtained by simply removing those two and writing programs with only simple queries and the two positive connectives, that doing so would greatly restrict the expressive power, and that finding ways to achieve more consistency with logic without unduly sacrificing expressive power is a major concern of research in the field.
