# A hierarchy earns its keep by making one path unique, not by classifying things

**Lesson:** Arrange types so each has at most one supertype and at most one subtype, and three separate problems collapse into one mechanism. Converting between distant types needs no direct procedure, only a step to the next level up, repeated. Inheriting an operation you never defined works by moving up a level and trying again until something answers. Simplifying a result to its plainest form works by moving down. All three are the same walk on the same line, and adding a level requires saying only how it relates to its immediate neighbours.

Every one of those depends on a single property, and it is not classification. It is that "up" names exactly one thing. Let a type have two supertypes and nothing above is destroyed as a taxonomy — an isosceles right triangle is genuinely both an isosceles triangle and a right triangle, and the classification is more accurate for saying so. What is destroyed is the uniqueness of the step, and with it every algorithm phrased as "go up until it works." Finding the correct supertype in which to perform an operation becomes a search over the network of relations, and the same happens in reverse when going down, because a type generally has many subtypes. The taxonomy got richer and the machinery got worse.

The lesson to carry is that when you impose a structure to make some traversal cheap, the property you actually depend on is usually far narrower than the structure you named — and the useful question is which property, not which structure. Here it is unique-successor. Elsewhere it is acyclicity, or a total order, or confluence. Naming it lets you notice the moment a modelling change quietly removes it, which is exactly the situation where a design keeps working on the old cases and starts requiring search on the new ones without anybody deciding that.

The authors do not pretend to resolve it. They say the general problem — a useful framework for expressing relations among interrelated types — is intractably difficult, that in the years between editions the main change was that inadequate theories had been embodied in correspondingly inadequate programming languages, and that much of the complexity and the confusing differences among object-oriented languages sit precisely here. Their own response is worth copying: rather than build a weaker general mechanism, the next chapter drops the goal, discusses local state at length, and never mentions classes or inheritance at all. When the general version of a problem is genuinely unsolved, picking the special case that has the property you need — or restructuring so you do not need it — beats a general mechanism that is wrong in ways nobody can enumerate.

**Source:** [Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs](../works/structure-and-interpretation-of-computer-programs.md) - chapter 2 section 2.5.2's subsections on hierarchies of types and inadequacies of hierarchies, which define a tower as a hierarchy where each type has at most one supertype and one subtype, show that raising successively lets a new type be added by relating it only to its neighbours, that operations not defined for a type can be found by crawling up until one answers, and that results can be lowered to the simplest representation; then present the geometric-figures diagram where triangles and quadrilaterals share a supertype and an isosceles right triangle has two, note that multiple supertypes leave no unique way to raise and force searching the type network, and add the footnote observing that general ontology seems intractably difficult, that inadequate theories now sit inside correspondingly inadequate languages, that much of the complexity of object-oriented languages centres on this, and that chapter 3 avoids the issue entirely by discussing local state without classes or inheritance.
