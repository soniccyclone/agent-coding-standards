# The capability you can never have was usually foreclosed by a low-level choice made for an unrelated reason

**Lesson:** When a system persistently cannot do something that similar systems do easily, the explanation is rarely that nobody implemented it. Far more often a decision taken deep in the implementation, for reasons entirely local to that layer, made the capability unreachable — and the connection between the decision and the missing capability is not recorded anywhere, because at the time the decision was made the capability was not under discussion. The example worth internalizing is a storage decision that determines a language-level semantic property: choose to keep a procedure's arguments and local variables on the same stack that holds return addresses, and a call in final position can no longer be turned into a plain transfer, because the caller's storage is entangled with the caller's return information and cannot be released independently. The consequence is that repetition through calling costs unbounded space, so the language must supply separate looping constructs, so programmers must think about two mechanisms instead of one. None of that was intended, argued for, or written down.

Notice why the decision was taken: keeping variables on the stack means their lifetime ends when the call does, which means no reclamation machinery is needed for them, which is attractive precisely to systems that were trying to avoid having any. That is a coherent local argument. The system that put those values in reclaimable storage instead was paying for reclamation for other reasons anyway, and got the calling property as a side effect it did not pay extra for. Both layers were reasoning correctly about their own concerns and the outcomes diverge at a layer neither was looking at.

The lesson for anyone debating a foundational choice is to spend effort on what it forecloses, not on what it costs, because the cost is measurable now and the foreclosure is not. Ask which properties of the layer above depend on this being free to vary. Ask what becomes impossible rather than expensive, since expensive can be revisited and impossible cannot. And treat "everybody's implementation does it this way" as weak evidence, since the real question is whether they all inherited the same constraint from the same forgotten decision.

The reading habit that follows is equally useful. Faced with a limitation that seems arbitrary, do not accept the explanation that it is merely how things are done; trace it downward until you find the decision it rests on, and ask what that decision was buying. Usually it was buying something real, so the limitation is a trade rather than an oversight — but knowing what was bought tells you whether the trade is still worth making, and occasionally it turns out that the thing purchased is no longer needed and the limitation has outlived its reason entirely.

**Source:** [Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs](../works/structure-and-interpretation-of-computer-programs.md) — chapter 5 section 5.5.3, the footnote accompanying the compiler's generation of a direct transfer for a call in final position, which observes that making a compiler generate such code might seem straightforward but that most compilers for common languages do not, and that those languages therefore cannot represent iterative processes in terms of procedure call alone; the stated cause, that their implementations use the stack to store procedure arguments and local variables as well as return addresses, whereas the implementations described in the book store arguments and variables in memory that is reclaimed automatically; the stated motive for the stack choice, that it avoids the need for automatic reclamation in languages that would not otherwise require it and is generally believed to be more efficient; and the qualification that sophisticated implementations can in fact use the stack for arguments without destroying the property, and that whether stack allocation is actually more efficient than automatic reclamation is itself contested and hinges on fine points of architecture.
