# The unit of atomicity belongs to the transaction, not the object, so no object can encapsulate its own concurrency control

**Lesson:** An account that serializes its own deposits and withdrawals is safe, self-contained, and exactly what encapsulation recommends. Then someone writes an operation that exchanges the balances of two accounts, and it is wrong — each individual step is protected, and the composite is not, because another process can change one balance between the moment the difference is computed and the moment it is applied. To fix it, the authors do something they flag as deliberate: they break the modularity of the account by exporting its serializer, so that a caller can hold both accounts' locks across the whole exchange.

That is not a wart in their design; it is the structural consequence of where atomicity lives. What must happen indivisibly is determined by the operation being performed, and an object cannot know which larger operations it will one day be part of. So its self-protection is correct for exactly the transactions that touch it alone, and no others. Every system that grows composite operations over independently locked resources meets this, and each meets it as a surprise, because the individual components are visibly correct and the composition is where the invariant lives.

The bill for exposing the lock arrives immediately and is worth quoting exactly. Once the serializer is handed out, the account stops serializing itself and every caller becomes responsible for doing it — the authors leave the account unserialized rather than doing both. Trying to have both is the natural next suggestion and it does not work: the composite acquires the account's serializer and then the account's own methods try to acquire it again from inside, and the process waits for itself. Protection at two levels of the same lock is not extra safety, it is a hang. Reentrancy, lock ordering, and who-holds-what become the caller's problem, which is precisely the complexity encapsulation was supposed to contain, now redistributed to everyone.

And multiple locks bring their own failure. Two processes exchanging the same pair of accounts in opposite orders each take one lock and wait forever for the other. The standard remedy is to number the resources and always acquire in increasing order, which works — and the authors immediately mark its limit in an exercise: it requires knowing the full set of resources before acquiring any of them. A process that must read one resource to discover which others it needs cannot obey the ordering, and for those cases the choice is more sophisticated avoidance or recovery by backing out and retrying. That is the honest summary of the whole area: encapsulated locking does not compose, exposing locks does not encapsulate, and ordering only works when the future is known in advance.

**Source:** [Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs](../works/structure-and-interpretation-of-computer-programs.md) - chapter 3 section 3.4.2's subsections on the complexity of using multiple shared resources and on deadlock, which show an exchange procedure that is incorrect under concurrency even when each account's deposits and withdrawals are serialized, state that the exchange must lock out other access to both accounts for its entire duration, present make-account-and-serializer while noting that they are deliberately breaking the modularity of the bank-account object by exposing the serializer and that it is now each user's responsibility to manage serialization, pose Exercise 3.45 asking what goes wrong if the account both exports its serializer and continues to serialize its own operations, describe the deadlock arising when two processes exchange the same pair of accounts in opposite orders, give the remedy of numbering accounts and always acquiring the lowest-numbered first, and pose Exercise 3.49 asking for a scenario where that remedy fails, hinting at a process that must access some shared resources before it can know which additional ones it will require.
