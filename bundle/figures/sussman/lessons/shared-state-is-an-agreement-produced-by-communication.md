# Shared state is not a thing that exists but an agreement communication produces, so questions about it between agreements can be meaningless

**Lesson:** Having built serializers and shown how to use them, the authors say the problems go deeper, because from a fundamental point of view it is not always clear what shared state even means. Their example: branches of a bank holding local balances and reconciling periodically. When someone deposits at one branch, has "the balance" changed at the moment the local value changes, or only at reconciliation? The honest answer is that between reconciliations there is no single account balance. There is a value here and a value there, and the phrase that names one number does not refer.

The consequence is a shift in what correctness can even be asked about. If there is no global value, then constraints must be stated over what individual participants observe and over the state immediately following synchronization — and questions about the real balance, or about the order of events between synchronizations, may be not merely unanswerable but meaningless. That is a stronger claim than "hard to determine," and it is the one that matters, because a specification written in terms of a quantity that does not exist cannot be satisfied or violated. The authors note this is how real systems work: international card charges cleared per country, so the balance genuinely differs by country between reconciliations.

Behind it is the general principle, stated plainly: synchronizing processes, establishing shared state, and imposing an order on events all require communication among the processes, so any notion of time in concurrency control is inseparable from communication. Shared state is not discovered, it is manufactured, and its manufacture costs messages. That reframes every design decision in the area as a purchase — each additional guarantee about a common view is bought with coordination, and the systems that scale are the ones that found out how few such guarantees they needed rather than the ones that implemented them efficiently.

The authors point at the physical analogy and do not overclaim it: the speed of light plays the same structural role in relativity, being the fastest signal available for synchronizing events, so time and space are related through it — and they suggest the difficulties in our computational models may mirror a real feature of the universe rather than a defect of our tools. Whether or not one takes the parallel seriously, the practical residue is exact. Stop asking what the value is. Ask which participants must agree, when they must agree, and what you are willing to pay in communication for that agreement.

**Source:** [Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs](../works/structure-and-interpretation-of-computer-programs.md) - chapter 3 section 3.4.2's closing subsection on concurrency, time and communication, which observes that it is not always clear what shared state means, notes that test-and-set style mechanisms require examining a global flag at arbitrary times and are problematic on processors where pipelining and caching mean memory is not consistent at every instant, describes a distributed banking system whose branches hold local balances reconciled periodically and asks when the balance can be said to have changed, argues that only the behaviour observed by individual participants and the state immediately after synchronization may matter and that questions about the real balance or the order of events between synchronizations may be irrelevant or meaningless, cites internationally cleared credit-card charges as a system that works this way, states that synchronizing processes, establishing shared state or imposing an order on events all require communication so any notion of time in concurrency control must be tied to communication, and draws the parallel to the speed of light in relativity as the fastest signal available for synchronizing events.
