# Any obligation that comes due after a transfer of control forces a frame, so undo-on-exit features quietly forbid unbounded looping

**Lesson:** Features get evaluated one at a time, in the category they seem to belong to, and this is where designs acquire defects that nobody can point at afterwards. A rule about how names are resolved looks like it belongs to naming. But consider what it obliges a caller to do: if the caller established something that must be taken back down when the call finishes — a saved previous meaning restored, a handler removed, a resource released — then the moment the callee finishes, control must come back to the caller to discharge that obligation before the caller can finish. The callee therefore cannot be handed the caller's own destination, because something has to happen in between. A record must be kept. And once a record must be kept per call, a computation written as an unbounded chain of calls accumulates records without bound: what was intended as a loop is now a structure that grows with its trip count, and the language cannot express iteration in the style its own semantics suggested.

The escape people reach for is optimization: surely a compiler can see that nothing will ever consult the thing being saved, and skip the save. Sometimes it can, and in general it cannot, because whether anything consults it depends on the behaviour of procedures the compiler may never see — the whole point of the naming rule in question is that an unknown callee may reach back for a name it did not receive. So the impossibility is not an artifact of a weak optimizer; it is a consequence of the feature's own reason for existing. That is the honest way to reach a decision here: not "we prefer this scoping rule" but "we have already committed to calls being transfers that cost nothing, and that commitment excludes any rule requiring work after the transfer." The second commitment is forced by the first.

The general discipline is to audit each feature for what it owes *after* the point of no return, because that is the property that determines whether transfers can be cheap. Scope-exit actions, automatic resource release, unwinding handlers, per-frame instrumentation: each is defended on its own merits, each imposes a post-transfer obligation, and each therefore converts calls in final position from jumps into stack growth. It is entirely legitimate to want the feature and pay the price, but the price should be named up front, since it is not paid in cycles — it is paid in which programs the language can run at all. Two features that were designed by different people, in different categories, on different days, can be individually reasonable and jointly impossible.

**Source:** [Lambda: The Ultimate Declarative](../works/lambda-the-ultimate-declarative.md) — the lexical-and-dynamic-binding section, which shows that under caller-determined name resolution the bindings established on entry must be undone between the callee's return and the caller's, so the callee cannot inherit the caller's return address; observes that a loop written as repeated calls would therefore grow its binding stack without bound; argues that a compiler cannot in general prove the saved values unreferenced because it may not be able to examine every function called; and concludes that adhering to the view of calls as transfers compels lexical scoping rather than merely recommending it.
