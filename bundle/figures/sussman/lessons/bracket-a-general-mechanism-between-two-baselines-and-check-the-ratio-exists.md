# Bracket a general mechanism between what it replaced and what a hand-built special case achieves, and check the ratio is even defined before quoting it

**Lesson:** A general mechanism that improves on the thing it replaced is usually evaluated against exactly that one baseline, and the result is a number that sounds like an achievement and carries no information about how much was left on the table. One baseline can only tell you the mechanism helped. Two baselines tell you where it sits: below, the general thing you replaced; above, an artifact built by hand for this one problem, using every fact about the problem that a general mechanism is not allowed to know. The distance to the lower baseline is what you gained. The distance to the upper one is the part of the win the generality cost you, and it is the only honest estimate of how much better the mechanism could be made. Without it, no one can say whether the remaining gap is a few percent of tuning or a factor that means the approach is wrong.

The second discipline is arithmetic and gets skipped constantly. A ratio between two costs is a stable summary only when both costs grow at the same rate in the parameter you vary. When they do, the ratio settles to a constant as the parameter grows and you have a genuine characterization: this mechanism costs about so many times the hand-built version, for every size. When they do not — when one side's consumption is linear and the other's is not — there is no limiting value, the ratio drifts with the input, and any single number quoted for it is a measurement of the particular input someone happened to try. This is why a benchmark result must always come with the growth class of both sides attached, and why comparisons across different problems are usually meaningless: they are averaging ratios that do not converge.

Together these give a procedure. Pick a parameter to vary. Determine the growth of resource use for the general mechanism, for the thing it replaced, and for a hand-built version, as functions of that parameter rather than as numbers. Where two share a growth class, take the ratio and treat it as a property of the mechanism. Where they do not, say so, and report the growth classes instead — a mechanism that changed a growth class has done something of a completely different kind from one that changed a constant, and collapsing both into "faster" throws away the distinction that matters most.

The habit generalizes past performance work to any evaluation of a general solution: a schema against a purpose-built table, a framework against a hand-rolled implementation, an automated decision against a specialist's. In each case the interesting number is the gap to the specialist, not the gap to the previous general thing, and in each case the gap is only expressible as a ratio when both are scaling the same way.

**Source:** [Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs](../works/structure-and-interpretation-of-computer-programs.md) — chapter 5, Exercise 5.45 and Exercise 5.46. The first sets up a three-way comparison of stack operations for the same computation — the interpreter, the compiled code, and the special-purpose register machine of an earlier section — states that comparing the compiler's optimized stack use to the special-purpose machine's gives an indication of the quality of the compiler, asks for the ratios of pushes and of maximum depth for compiled versus interpreted and for special-purpose versus interpreted, notes that because both quantities are linear in the input these ratios approach constants as the input grows, predicts that the hand-tailored controller will do much better than the rudimentary general-purpose compiler, and then asks what improvements to the compiler would close that gap. The second asks for the same analysis on the tree-recursive Fibonacci procedure against its own special-purpose machine, and states that because the time resource used there is not linear in the input, the ratios of stack operations will not approach a limiting value independent of the input.
