# Give each unit a summary of what it needs and disturbs, and the combinator can optimize what no unit could see

**Lesson:** A decision about whether protective work is necessary is often invisible from inside either party. Whether something must be preserved across a step depends jointly on whether that step disturbs it and whether anything afterwards depends on it, and neither the step nor its successor knows both halves. The usual result is that every producer emits the protection defensively, since being cautious is the only locally safe choice. The alternative is to move the decision to the only place that sees both sides — the operation that joins them — and to give that operation what it needs to decide: a summary, carried by every unit, of what the unit requires beforehand and what it disturbs. The join then compares the second unit's requirements against the first unit's disturbances and emits protection exactly when the two intersect.

Two consequences follow and both are large. The generators stop containing any protective logic at all — not less of it, none — because the concern has been removed from them rather than shared with them. And the protection that does appear is minimal by construction rather than by anyone's judgement, so it stays minimal as the system changes: alter what a unit disturbs, and every join involving it recomputes on its own. This is the difference between an optimization someone performed and an optimization the structure performs, and only the second survives maintenance.

The design detail that makes it practical is that the summary must compose. If the joining operation had to inspect the instructions of its arguments to work out their behaviour, it would be expensive, and it would redo that work every time a composite was itself joined to something else. So the summary is computed once at the leaves, where it is declared alongside the thing it describes, and every combinator derives the composite's summary from its parts' summaries. Analysis cost is then linear in the structure rather than quadratic, and the summaries are available at every level for whatever else wants them. The general form: a compositional annotation is worth far more than a computable one, because it can be consulted anywhere without paying for a traversal.

The precondition is honesty, and it is the whole risk of the approach. A unit that disturbs something its summary does not mention will have its protection omitted, and the resulting fault appears at a distance from the lie, in code that is correct. So the summaries must be either derived mechanically from the unit or checked against it — never maintained by hand as documentation. Where that check is available, this pattern is one of the highest-leverage structures in systems design, and it recurs far beyond compilers: dependency sets that let a build skip work, effect annotations that let a scheduler reorder, declared reads and writes that let a transaction system decide what conflicts. In each case the individual component becomes simpler, the combining operation becomes smarter, and the optimization stops being someone's responsibility.

**Source:** [Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs](../works/structure-and-interpretation-of-computer-programs.md) — chapter 5 section 5.5.1, on instruction sequences and stack usage: the description of the combining procedure that takes a set of registers and two sequences and appends them so that each register's contents survive the first sequence if the second actually needs them, wrapping a save and a restore around the first sequence only when the first modifies the register and the second needs its original contents, with the four possible outputs enumerated for a two-register case; the accompanying statement that this isolates the decision about whether to generate stack operations inside the combining procedure, separating it from the concerns of the individual code generators, so that in fact no save or restore instruction is ever produced by a code generator; and the argument for carrying register-use information rather than deriving it on demand — that representing a sequence as a bare list of instructions would make the combining operation both complex and inefficient, since it would have to analyze each argument even when that argument had itself been built by a previous call whose parts were already analyzed — resolved by having each sequence carry the set of registers that must be initialized before it runs, the set it modifies, and its instructions, with the combining procedures deriving the composite's sets from its components'.
