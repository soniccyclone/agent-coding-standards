# Reify the whole trajectory and improving the convergence becomes an ordinary program

**Lesson:** An iterative approximation is usually written as a loop that carries the current guess and stops when it is good enough. Everything about the run except the final answer is thrown away as it happens. Rewrite the same computation so that it emits the entire succession of guesses as a single value, and something changes that has nothing to do with efficiency: the run itself has become an object other code can take as an argument. The authors are explicit that the acceleration techniques they then apply could have been implemented without this change of representation. The point of the change is not that it enables the technique but that it turns the technique into an ordinary procedure over an ordinary value, so it can be written once and applied to any convergent process whatsoever.

The payoff is at the meta level, where it is easy to miss. A convergence accelerator is a function from a sequence of approximations to a better-converging sequence of approximations to the same limit. It is not a modification to the algorithm that produced the guesses; the producing algorithm does not know it exists and is not touched. That separation is only available because the trajectory was made into a value with a type — a thing of the same kind going in and coming out. A loop that overwrites its state variable has no such interface to offer, so an accelerator over a loop has to be fused into the loop by hand, one fusion per algorithm.

Then take the observation one step further, because this is the part with real transfer. Since the accelerator maps trajectories to trajectories, it can be applied to its own output, and to that output's output, without limit. What you get is a two-dimensional array of successively more aggressive approximation schemes, and reading down its diagonal produces a sequence far better than any fixed number of applications would give. Nothing in that construction required any new idea about numerical analysis. It required only that the transformer be closed under composition with itself, which is a property of the *interface* you chose, not of the mathematics. The general rule: when a transformation's input and output types match, iterating it is free, and free iteration of a modest improvement is often stronger than a cleverer one-shot method. The numbers here are not subtle — a handful of terms of the doubly accelerated sequence match what would otherwise take on the order of ten trillion terms.

The design instruction is to ask, of any process whose intermediate states you are currently discarding, what operators would become expressible if the succession of states were a value. Convergence acceleration is one; comparing states from different moments, running two histories against each other, deciding termination outside the producer rather than inside it, are others the same change unlocks. The cost is that the representation must be lazy or the trajectory is unaffordable, and that cost is real. But the thing bought is not a faster program. It is a place to stand outside the program.

**Source:** [Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs](../works/structure-and-interpretation-of-computer-programs.md) - chapter 3 section 3.5.3's subsection on formulating iterations as stream processes, which rewrites the successive-guesses square-root iteration and the alternating series for pi as streams of successive approximations rather than updated state variables, introduces Euler's transform as a procedure taking a stream of partial sums to a faster-converging stream, builds a tableau by repeatedly applying the transform to its own result and forms the accelerated sequence from the first term of each row, reports that eight terms of the doubly accelerated sequence give pi to fourteen places against the order of ten to the thirteenth terms needed from the original series, and closes by noting that these techniques could have been implemented without streams but that the stream formulation makes the entire sequence of states available as a data structure manipulable by a uniform set of operations.
