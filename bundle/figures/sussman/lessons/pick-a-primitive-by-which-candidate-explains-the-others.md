# Choose among candidate primitives by which one explains the others, since one-way definability is the only asymmetry that carries information

**Lesson:** Arguments about which construct belongs at the bottom of a design usually appeal to sufficiency, and sufficiency almost always comes out a tie: several candidate sets are adequate to express everything, so adequacy separates nothing. A test that does separate them is available and is empirical rather than axiomatic. Take each candidate and try to explain the others in terms of it. Where the explanation runs both ways, you have learned that the candidates are interchangeable and should choose on other grounds. Where it runs one way only — this construct explains those constructs comfortably, and none of them explains it, or explains each other — the asymmetry is the finding. The evidence usually already exists in the literature without having been read as evidence: when the people introducing two elaborate mechanisms both find it natural to explain their mechanism in terms of some simpler one, and neither could plausibly have explained theirs in terms of the other, the simpler one has passed the test in public.

The criteria that make the test meaningful are worth stating, because "minimal" on its own is a trap. A good primitive is not merely universal but easy to describe, simple to implement, and effective as a medium for describing richer constructs — four conditions, of which universality is the weakest and the one most often cited alone. Fail the last three and you get bases that are provably complete and useless for thinking with. The complement is equally important: showing that a construct *can* be removed does not argue that it should be. Choice can be eliminated in favour of repetition and a flag, and the result is more complicated than what it replaced, not less. So an elimination result should be checked for whether it reduced total complexity or merely relocated it, and a reduction that increases the difficulty of describing ordinary programs has argued against itself.

The habit generalizes to any layered system where you must decide what the bottom layer offers. Line the candidates up, attempt each explanation in both directions, and treat the direction that fails as the real content of the comparison; then check the winner against describability and implementability rather than stopping at expressive completeness. This is a cheap procedure that replaces a class of unresolvable arguments, and it also explains why some primitives stay in service for decades despite the periodic arrival of more sophisticated alternatives: they remain the medium in which the alternatives are explained.

**Source:** [Lambda: The Ultimate Declarative](../works/lambda-the-ultimate-declarative.md) — the opening section on primitive operations in programming languages, which sets out that a minimal set should be universal but also easy to describe, simple to implement, and capable of describing more complex constructs straightforwardly, and offers as its worked case the observation that the expositors of two elaborate synchronization mechanisms each went to great lengths to explain theirs in terms of the semaphore while it would be difficult to describe either in terms of the other; together with the accompanying note that the conditional can be eliminated in favour of sequencing plus a loop and a control variable, at the cost of more complexity rather than less.
