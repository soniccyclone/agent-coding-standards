# Every joining operator has two dual implementations, and selectivity decides which one you want

**Lesson:** Whenever two conditions have to hold of the same thing, there are exactly two shapes the implementation can take and they are duals. Either you evaluate the first, and for each partial answer it yields you evaluate the second against the whole collection again; or you evaluate both independently against the collection and then look for compatible pairs among their results. The first pays the cost of the second condition once per surviving partial answer. The second pays the cost of each condition once, and then pays for comparing outputs against each other. Neither is universally better, and which one is better is determined by a quantity neither implementation looks at: how much each condition narrows things down.

Working the arithmetic makes the trade explicit and is worth doing rather than intuiting. If a condition passes some fraction of a collection, the first shape's cost scales with the size of the collection times the number of survivors of the earlier condition; the second shape's cost scales with the product of the two survivor counts. When the conditions are selective, the survivor counts are far smaller than the collection, and the second shape wins by roughly the selectivity factor. When one condition is barely selective at all, its survivor count approaches the collection size and the advantage evaporates. The reason to know this rather than to benchmark it is that the crossover is a property of the data and moves as the data changes, so it is the sort of thing you want expressed as a decision rule rather than baked in.

The second shape has a structural requirement that is easy to overlook: it needs an operation that takes two independently-computed partial answers and either merges them or reports them incompatible. That operation is not free to write, it is closely related to matching two partial descriptions against each other, and it does not exist in a system where partial answers are only ever extended and never combined. So the choice of implementation shape is really a choice made earlier, when the partial-answer representation was designed, and a representation that supports only extension has silently committed to the first shape forever.

The pattern is the same one that separates the two classic ways of joining tables, and it recurs anywhere two independently-computable sets of constraints must be satisfied simultaneously — merging search results, intersecting index lookups, combining filters over a stream. The useful reflex when writing such code is to notice that you are choosing between the two shapes, since the natural way to write it in most languages is the nested one and it will be chosen by default rather than on purpose.

**Source:** [Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs](../works/structure-and-interpretation-of-computer-programs.md) — chapter 4 section 4.4.4, Exercise 4.76, which calls the implementation of conjunction as a series combination elegant but inefficient because processing the second conjunct requires scanning the data base once per frame produced by the first, works out that for a data base of a given size and a typical query yielding a number of output frames proportional to that size divided by a selectivity factor the series approach costs a number of matcher calls quadratic in the size divided by that factor while the independent-evaluation approach costs a number of compatibility checks quadratic in the size divided by the square of that factor, notes this as a saving by exactly the selectivity factor, and asks for an implementation of the alternative — requiring a procedure that takes two frames, checks whether their bindings are compatible, and if so merges them, an operation the exercise itself observes is similar to unification.
