# Where interaction is by name-matching, two copies of a thing are one thing until you rename them

**Lesson:** Suppose components interact by agreeing on shared names, so that anything named by two parties requires both to take part. Then a name is not a label attached to behaviour; it is the wire. Put two identical components side by side and you have not obtained two of them — every name they use is shared, so they are obliged to agree on every step, and the pair is indistinguishable from one. Multiplicity does not come from writing the thing down twice. It comes from making the instances differ, and the only respect in which they can differ is their names. That inverts the usual intuition, in which instantiation is the primary act and naming is bookkeeping laid over it. Here the naming discipline *is* the instantiation mechanism, and nothing else is available to play the part.

Seen that way, the renaming apparatus stops looking cosmetic and turns out to do the work of several familiar language features simultaneously. Prefix every name of a component with a distinguishing tag and you have a local variable: an independent instance, reachable only by a party that knows the tag. Use an index as the tag and you have an array. Apply the operation twice and you have nesting, with compound names reading like qualified paths. Let the tag be chosen from a set at the moment of interaction and you have one instance serving several clients, each identifying itself as it engages. None of these required a new construct — they are one renaming operation used differently — and the reason they compose freely is that renaming distributes through every other operator in the notation, so a renamed assembly is the assembly of the renamed parts.

The condition underwriting all of it is that the renaming be injective, and that condition repays being understood rather than merely satisfied. Distinct names must remain distinct, because collapsing two names collapses two interactions that were separate, converting independent activity into compulsory agreement without any warning. So the soundness of every construction above rests on a property of a function — and precisely the property most easily violated by accident, when names are assembled from strings, or when two subsystems developed apart are brought together and turn out to share a word. The design consequence is blunt: in a system where interaction is by name-matching, name generation must be a designed mechanism carrying a guarantee, not a convention. An accidental collision there is not a tidiness problem, it is an unintended synchronization, and it will surface as a seizure or a mysteriously lost concurrency a long way from wherever the names were picked.
