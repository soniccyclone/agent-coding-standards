# Aim to avoid persistently pessimal states rather than to reach optimal ones, because locally fair rules are globally unfair

**Lesson:** Service a shared pool first-come-first-served and you have a rule that is fair in the only sense the rule can see: equal numbers of grants to equal numbers of requests. Look at the participants instead of the requests and the same rule is destructive. Two consumers drawing through the same pool at wildly different rates will, under equal grants, be pulled to a common rate, which is the slow one — the fast side cannot outrun its supply indefinitely. Worse, the resulting state is self-sustaining: nearly all of the pool ends up held by the slow participant, so recovery takes as long as the slow participant takes to drain it. Equalizing the *service* has equalized the *throughput*, and throughput equalization across unequal capacities is precisely the outcome nobody wanted. The general trap is that a policy expressed over requests cannot see the property you care about, which is a property of participants over time.

The repair is not a cleverer optimum. It is to identify the bad extreme and choose, at each decision, the action that moves away from it: grant the next unit to whichever participant currently holds the least. This is a weak rule with a strong justification — it needs only per-participant occupancy, which is cheap to maintain, it never has to model rates or predict futures, and it steers the system as far as it can from the configuration where one participant has captured everything. The stance generalizes past allocation: never seek to make an optimal decision, merely seek to avoid persistently pessimal ones. Optimal decisions need global knowledge you do not have and time you cannot spend, and their failure mode is unbounded; non-pessimal decisions need almost no knowledge and their failure mode is a bounded loss of efficiency.

Read that way, a set of otherwise unrelated scheduling maxims turns out to be one idea. Keep variance in waiting time low, not just the mean, because a good average with a bad tail is the pessimal state visiting some participant. Avoid fixed priorities and prefer that everyone make reasonably steady progress, because static ranking is a standing licence for the bottom rank to be overtaken forever. Where a policy that reduces average cost admits indefinite overtaking — as any nearest-request-first rule does — bound it by structure, for instance by sweeping in one direction and only changing direction when nothing remains ahead, which sacrifices some of the average gain to remove the unbounded case entirely. And make sure the behavior under overload is stated, since a policy is only ever tested there.

**Source:** [Monitors: An Operating System Structuring Concept](../works/monitors-an-operating-system-structuring-concept.md) — the buffer-allocation example, where first-come-first-served sharing of a free pool between a fast and a slow consumer drags the fast stream down to the slow one's speed and leaves nearly all buffers with the slower stream, and the remedy of allocating each newly freed buffer to the competing stream that currently owns fewest so the system moves as far as possible from that extreme; the disk-head example, where shortest-seek-first is rejected for admitting indefinite overtaking and replaced by a directional sweep; and the concluding list of scheduling principles, which opens by rejecting optimal decisions in favor of avoiding persistently pessimal ones and goes on to low variance, avoidance of fixed priorities, steady progress for every program, and satisfactory behavior under overload.
