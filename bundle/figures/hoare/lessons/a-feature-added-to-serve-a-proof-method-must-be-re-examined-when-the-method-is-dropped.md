# A feature added to make a hoped-for proof method work has to be re-examined the moment that method is abandoned

**Lesson:** Design decisions frequently trace back not to what users need but to what some intended analysis needs. A requirement that every concurrent component eventually finish, for instance, can come entirely from the hope of describing correctness the way it is described for ordinary programs — by a condition asserted to hold once the thing has stopped. If nothing ever stops, that style of statement has nothing to attach to, so the design is bent to make stopping universal. Then the requirement has consequences of its own: something must tell the long-lived helpers that their work is over, which is an obligation their users did not ask for, so a convention is invented to make termination propagate automatically, and the convention needs its own rules about when it fires.

The thing to notice is that the whole chain hangs from a premise that can fail independently. If the proof style never works out — if you end up reasoning about the entire history of a component's interactions rather than about a final state — then the feature at the bottom of the chain is now supporting nothing. It does not announce this. It stays in the language, it stays in the implementation, and it keeps costing what it always cost: intricate to specify, awkward to implement, and, as it turns out, not making the proofs any easier under the method you actually adopted. Left alone, it becomes one of those constructs everyone assumes must have a reason because it is obviously deliberate.

So the discipline is to record, at the time, which features exist to serve which analysis, and to treat the abandonment of an analysis as a trigger to revisit them rather than as an unrelated event. The re-examination is not automatic deletion; it is a fresh justification on current grounds or removal. Two questions do the work. Does this feature still buy anything now that the technique it was for is gone? And is the awkwardness it was introduced to smooth over even a problem under the new technique — because often the constraint that made it necessary is itself gone, and the honest fix is to drop the constraint and handle the genuinely hard cases with specific machinery rather than to keep a general mechanism that exists to make a universal rule bearable. This is the ordinary way languages and systems accumulate weight: not from bad decisions, but from good decisions whose premises quietly expired.

**Source:** [Communicating Sequential Processes](../works/communicating-sequential-processes-book.md) — the automatic-termination part of the discussion chapter's subsection comparing the book with the earlier CSP proposal, which records that the early design expected all processes of a parallel command to terminate in the hope that correctness could be specified by a post-condition as for a conventional program, that the hope was never fulfilled and other proof methods now seem more satisfactory, that the termination obligation forced an ad hoc convention whereby an input-guarded loop ended automatically once all its sources had ended, and that this convention proved complicated to define and implement while making correctness proofs no simpler — leading to the later preference for relaxing the termination restriction on simple subordinate processes and using separate measures for the complicated cases.
