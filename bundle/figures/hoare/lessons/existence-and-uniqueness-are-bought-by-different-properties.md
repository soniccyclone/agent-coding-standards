# Existence and uniqueness of a solution are bought by different properties — know which one you actually need

**Lesson:** A collection of definitions that refer to one another is resolved by starting from the emptiest possible answer and applying the definitions over and over until nothing more accrues. Two questions about that procedure get routinely merged into one, and they have different answers. Does what the procedure converges on actually satisfy the definitions? And is it the only thing that does? The first needs each application to be well behaved with respect to accumulation: applying the definitions to the limit of a growing sequence of partial answers must give the same result as applying them to each partial answer and taking the limit. With that alone, the limit of the iteration is a solution — nothing further is required. The second needs something strictly stronger: each application must be determined by less than it produces, so that the n-th approximation already fixes the first n units of *any* solution at all, leaving a rival nothing to differ in.

Keeping the two apart pays, because a system that needs only existence can accept definitions a system demanding uniqueness must reject, and the difference is not academic. Mutually referring definitions with several solutions are common and usually harmless, provided the procedure canonically delivers the smallest — which is precisely what starting from nothing and only ever adding gives you. Dependency resolution, dataflow analysis, type inference, constraint propagation and recalculation engines all live in that territory, and insisting every definition be uniquely solvable would rule out useful configurations to secure a guarantee nobody was using. The converse case is just as important: if you intend to substitute a name for its definition during reasoning, treating the two as interchangeable, then uniqueness is exactly what you need and the existence of a canonical solution will not do.

Both properties have operational readings that make them checkable by inspection, which is the reason to keep them straight rather than delegating to intuition. Accumulation-friendliness means no rule ever retracts a conclusion an earlier round established; the reliable tell that it has been lost is a rule whose premise is the *absence* of something, since adding facts can then remove conclusions. Determined-by-less means every cycle among the definitions passes through at least one point that commits output before consulting itself. Two further observations. These are properties of the rule forms rather than of any particular set of definitions written with them, so they are worth establishing once when the notation is designed instead of re-checked per program. And a rule system can be impeccable on one and hopeless on the other, which is why "is this well defined" is not a single question and should not be answered as one.
