# Having to quantify over every possible context is a symptom that your primitive observation is too weak; strengthen the instrument instead

**Lesson:** Any notion of two things being the same must be usable for substitution — replace one by the other anywhere and nothing downstream may notice. So the first test of a proposed sameness relation is not whether it matches intuition but whether it is preserved by every way of embedding the things in something larger. Relations that seem obviously right routinely fail this. Declare an internal step invisible, and it follows that a system doing an internal step then behaving as X is the same as X; yet put both in a context offering a choice, and one of them can silently commit to the branch that goes nowhere while the other cannot. The relation was not wrong about direct behaviour. It was wrong about substitution, which is the only thing it was for.

There are two repairs and they cost very differently. The first is to define sameness by brute quantification: two things are the same when, for every context expressible in the language, the results of embedding them are indistinguishable. This works, and establishing a usable set of laws for it is real mathematical labour. But notice what the definition has done — it has moved the difficulty permanently into every subsequent argument, because the definition now mentions all contexts and any proof about it must contend with that. The second repair is to go back to the instrument and ask why direct observation missed the difference. Here the answer is specific: watching only what a system *does* cannot detect a system that has quietly committed to doing nothing, and the fix is to record also what it might *decline* at each point, and to record declinable groups rather than one item at a time, since the interesting failure is being unable to accept any of several offers. Make that change and the naive relation is substitutive as it stands.

The generalizable claim is that ceremony in a theory is diagnostic. When a definition has to range over all environments, all clients, all embeddings, that is usually not depth — it is compensation for an observation too coarse to expose the distinction locally, and the compensation is charged to every user of the theory forever while the fix is a one-time cost paid by its author. The same reading applies well outside semantics: a test suite that can only characterize a component by running the whole system around it, a specification that can only be stated in terms of what every caller would notice, a bug that reproduces only in integration. In each case the diagnosis to consider first is that the component's own interface does not expose enough, and the leverage is in making the local observation sharper rather than in getting better at reasoning about assemblies. A sharper instrument also pays a second dividend: because it distinguishes fewer things than the context-quantified relation would once you also stop caring about what remains invisible, you get a coarser equality and therefore more laws — more permitted rewritings, more admissible optimizations.

**Source:** [Communicating Sequential Processes](../works/communicating-sequential-processes-book.md) — the closing part of the CCS comparison in the discussion chapter's mathematical-models section: the requirement that equal processes stay equal under transformation by the same function, the failure of the natural equivalence identifying a hidden internal step with its continuation once that process is placed in a choice with a deadlocking alternative, Milner's resolution by defining observational congruence as indistinguishability under every context expressible in the language (and the remark that discovering a full set of congruence laws is a significant achievement), followed by Hoare's diagnosis that this extra complexity is due to the inability to make sufficiently penetrating observations of a process without placing it in an environment — which is why the book introduces refusal sets rather than refusals of single events, yielding a weaker equivalence and a more powerful set of algebraic laws.
