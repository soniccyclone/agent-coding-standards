# Splitting a global optimization into independent modules is a hypothesis you must earn, not a structural default

**Lesson:** Modular decomposition is usually presented as a free win, but for anything that allocates or arbitrates it is a substantive bet, and the argument runs against you on the merits. A single component holding all the tables can see the whole state and therefore can, in principle, decide better than several components each seeing a slice; and several components deciding independently can, again in principle, cycle into a mutual wait that no one of them could have detected. So the monolithic arbiter is the theoretically stronger design, and the case for splitting cannot be "modularity is good." It has to be that each part can be given a policy which behaves acceptably on its own slice and which does not interact destructively with the others' policies. That is a claim about specific algorithms, and until you have them, the decomposition is unproven.

What makes the claim dischargeable is giving up on optimality — the two things are the same decision seen from two sides. A part that is only trying to avoid persistently bad outcomes needs almost no information about the rest of the system, so it can be written independently and its behavior does not depend on what the other parts are doing; a part that is trying to be optimal needs global state, and needing global state is what forces the monolith. The corollaries follow in the same direction: prefer policies you can reverse by preemption over commitments you cannot; prefer mechanisms that give participants provable independence from each other's strategies over mechanisms that couple them; and refuse the temptation to present clients with an abstraction more accommodating than the underlying hardware, because every such improvement is a promise whose cost is coordination with parts you were trying to stay independent of.

There is a companion split that makes the whole arrangement affordable, and it is worth stating separately: separate deciding from policing. The shared arbiter should be allowed to assume its clients follow the rules, with violations caught by a checking layer that is replicated per client, local to it, and ideally enforced by hardware. Centralizing the checks would put every client's misbehavior on the critical path of the one component all clients queue behind, growing the very thing that must stay small and fast; distributing them keeps enforcement proportional to the number of clients rather than concentrated. Stating rules for correct use and assuming compliance is only safe because the enforcement exists elsewhere — the assumption and the local envelope are two halves of one design, and taking the first without the second is how a shared component acquires defensive checks it can never remove.

**Source:** [Monitors: An Operating System Structuring Concept](../works/monitors-an-operating-system-structuring-concept.md) — the conclusion's discussion of disjointness, which concedes that more knowledge of the whole system should in principle allow decisions nearer optimality and that independent scheduling of different resources can lead to deadly embrace, hence that the monolithic monitor with large shared tables is the design those considerations point to; states that there is no a priori reason the split into isolated disjoint monitors should succeed and that it can be made to succeed only by discovering good scheduling algorithms for each; and lists the enabling principles, including preemption in preference to non-preemption, methods securing independence of scheduling strategies, refusal to present a virtual machine better than the actual hardware, and the placement of checking in a per-process user envelope rather than in a central shared monitor.
