# Deadlock is a shape in the state space — a concavity in the forbidden region facing the start — and the cure is to enlarge the forbidden region

**Lesson:** Take two participants each acquiring and releasing the same two exclusive resources, and plot progress on two axes, one per participant, starting at the origin. Every step by one moves the point along its own axis. Mutual exclusion carves out rectangular regions the point may never enter — the region where both hold one particular resource is simply unreachable. A trajectory meeting the boundary of such a region can only slide along it, which is what waiting looks like geometrically. Now the whole phenomenon becomes visible in one glance: doom is the region enclosed by a concavity in the forbidden area that faces the origin, because any trajectory entering it can move only towards the corner and cannot leave. Concavities facing the other way are entirely harmless. Deadlock is not an event that happens; it is a shape, and the moment of entering the doomed region is long before anyone appears to be stuck.

Two consequences follow directly from the picture and neither is obvious without it. The doomed zone is entered before anything looks wrong, so the observable seizure is not where the mistake was made — which is exactly why after-the-fact debugging of these failures is so unrewarding. And the only reliable remedy is to enlarge the forbidden area until the concavity is gone, since anything short of that leaves a trajectory that reaches the trap. Two ways of enlarging it are worth having. One is to introduce an additional artificial resource that must be held before either real one and released only after both, which fills the concavity directly. The other, cheaper and more general, is a fixed order over all resources that every participant acquires in — the concavity cannot form because the two participants can no longer approach the shared corner from opposite sides.

The ordering discipline repays a close reading because it is looser than the folklore version and the looseness is usable. Only acquisition order is constrained; release order is free, and resources should be released the moment they are finished with rather than held to the end. A participant may even acquire out of order, provided that at the moment of acquisition it holds nothing that comes later in the standard order. And crucially the whole discipline is checkable by reading the text of each participant separately, with no reference to any other — which is the property that makes it enforceable in a large system where no one person knows all the participants, and which no amount of testing can substitute for.

**Source:** [Communicating Sequential Processes](../works/communicating-sequential-processes-book.md) — the sharing-by-interleaving section of the shared resources chapter: the two cooks who share a pot and a pan and acquire them in opposite orders; the two-dimensional plot of their interleaved lives with the rectangular regions excluded by mutual exclusion, the trajectory that can only follow an edge while one cook waits, and the argument that danger arises solely from a concavity in the forbidden region facing the origin while other concavities are safe; the conclusion that the only sure prevention is to extend the forbidden region over the danger zone, whether by an artificial resource acquired before both and released after both — as with the footman in the dining philosophers — or by insisting on a fixed acquisition order; and the following paragraph generalizing the ordering rule to any number of users and resources, permitting free release order and out-of-order acquisition provided later resources have been released, and noting that observance can often be checked by a visual scan of the user processes' text.
