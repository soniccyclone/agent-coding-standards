# Nested expressions are the model of good structure, and their narrow interface is the source of both their power and their limits

**Lesson:** Ask why writing a compound arithmetic expression is such an obvious advance over sequencing machine instructions and the usual answer — mathematicians already write that way — explains nothing. The real answer is that the construct happens to satisfy, simultaneously, every property one wants from decomposition, and it is worth naming those properties because they generalize far past arithmetic. The meaning of the whole is determined by the meanings of its parts. A part has no purpose beyond its contribution to the whole. The parts can be understood without reference to each other. The same decomposition principle applies again inside any part, without modification. What passes between a part and its context is small, definite, and controlled. And the decomposition is legible in the written form — you can see which pieces are pieces by looking. Any candidate structuring mechanism can be scored against these six, and the interesting mechanisms are the ones that fail two or three of them in ways their advocates have not noticed.

The instructive part is the coupling between the last two properties and the mechanism's reach. The interface here is one value, which is why the properties are achievable together and achievable *efficiently* — a small result fits where computation happens, and no part of the machine outside that value can be involved. That same narrowness is the limitation: a mechanism this clean cannot carry large aggregates. Widening it so that whole collections flow between parts buys genuine expressive reach, and it buys it at a price that must be paid consciously. Cost stops being negligible, because an aggregate must be materialized somewhere rather than held where computation happens. Representation becomes the tool builder's decision rather than the user's, and one fixed representation is right for some sizes and shapes of data and badly wrong for others — a choice that ideally would differ between programs, or even between phases of one program. And a construct that hides where a result is stored takes control away from the person who knows which data are large.

The honest resolution is not to refuse the wider interface but to admit that when interfaces widen, two things have to be given back to the user: notation for the in-place, updating form of an operation, so that the space of an argument can serve as the space of the result when that is what the situation needs; and the ability to define representations and the operations over them, rather than receiving one representation as a fixed gift. Note the discipline required for the second to work — an extension mechanism succeeds only on a base that is already efficient, only if it extends the meaning of existing operators rather than growing new syntax, and only if the tool refuses to insert automatic conversions or defaults that the user did not write. Extension mechanisms usually fail because their designers want the reach without accepting those three restrictions.
