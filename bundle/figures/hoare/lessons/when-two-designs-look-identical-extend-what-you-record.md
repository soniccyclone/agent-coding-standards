# When two designs you must distinguish look identical, find the discriminating situation and let it name the missing observable

**Lesson:** You have a record of everything a system can do, and you find two systems with identical records that you nevertheless must tell apart — one lets its partner choose, the other decides for itself and merely happens to have the same repertoire. The wrong responses are to declare them equal because your instrument says so, or to bolt on a marker distinguishing them by fiat. The right one is to find a situation in which they demonstrably behave differently, and let that situation dictate what the record was missing. Here the situation is being placed beside a partner prepared to do only some of what is on offer: one system may then seize up and the other cannot. The observable that captures it is what the system might *decline* — the collections of offers it could refuse, at each point of its history. The discriminating experiment names the missing observable; you do not have to guess at it.

Two things about the extension are worth generalizing. First, it is genuinely more expensive — you are now carrying families of sets where before you carried sequences — and that expense is not a failure of ingenuity to be optimized away. If a distinction is real and the cheap vocabulary cannot express it, the vocabulary has to get dearer, and pretending otherwise means shipping a model that certifies two different things as the same. Second, when two candidate extensions carry the same information, choose between them by their algebra rather than by which sounds more natural. Recording what may be refused and recording what may be accepted are interconvertible, but one of them satisfies simple closure laws — refusing a set entails being able to refuse any part of it, and refusing nothing is always possible — while the other's laws come out awkward. Take the one with the simpler laws; every proof you ever write afterwards is charged for that decision.

The dividend is that a word everyone had been using loosely acquires a definition. With the richer vocabulary, deterministic stops being an adjective and becomes a checkable property: a system is deterministic exactly when, at every point of every history, it never refuses anything it is capable of doing. Nondeterministic means there exists a moment where it could and might not. Notice the shape of that definition — it mentions no randomness, no internal state, no implementation, only the relation between what a thing can do and what it is willing to do — which is why it applies to systems nobody had a theory of, and why the added complexity of the observation vocabulary paid for itself.

**Source:** [Communicating Sequential Processes](../works/communicating-sequential-processes-book.md) — the refusals section of the nondeterminism chapter, which observes that internal and external choice cannot be told apart by their traces, exhibits an environment in which one may deadlock at its first step and the other cannot, defines a refusal as a set of events initially offered by the environment on which the process may deadlock, remarks that the resulting families of sets are an unavoidable complexity and that refusals are preferred to ready sets because their laws are simpler, and then defines a deterministic process as one that can never refuse any event in which it can engage, at any point after any trace.
