# Pooling interchangeable units buys capacity and spends addressability, in one transaction

**Lesson:** Place two copies of a thing side by side so that each request is taken by whichever can serve it, and you have built something neither copy was: an assembly whose behaviour turns on a decision nobody makes explicitly. When only one copy could have handled a request, nothing is ambiguous. When both could, which one did is recorded nowhere — the history of what happened does not say — and from that point the assembly's future depends on a division of the past that was never observed. Capacity went up and the ability to say which unit is in which condition went away, and those are not two effects but one, because interchangeability is exactly what allowed either to take the work. You cannot buy the first without spending the second.

What this breaks in practice is the assumption that a pool is simply more of a unit. Guarantees a single unit could make about its own sequencing — that a request arriving after another sees the effect of that other — need not hold across the pool, since the two may have been taken by different units and nothing in the interface distinguishes that case from the other. Reasoning about the assembly therefore has to range over every division of the history consistent with what was observed, which is strictly weaker than reasoning about one unit, and the weakening is structural rather than an artifact of a lazy implementation. No amount of engineering inside the pool recovers a distinction that was destroyed at the moment the units were made mutually substitutable.

Two responses are available and both are respectable; drifting between them is not. Keep the units interchangeable and stop making per-unit claims: state the assembly's guarantees purely in aggregate terms and arrange that every request carries everything needed to serve it, so which unit takes it genuinely cannot matter. Or give the units distinct identities, which restores the ability to address them and immediately forfeits the pooling — a named unit can be busy while its neighbour idles, and callers now have to know something they were previously spared. The trap is wanting both: an arrangement that is interchangeable except for the requests that stick to one unit carries the reasoning weakness of the pool together with the routing obligations of named units, and pays twice. Note also that this is the same decision seen at a different scale as whether two things share a name at all, since interchangeability is manufactured by shared naming and destroyed by distinct naming.
