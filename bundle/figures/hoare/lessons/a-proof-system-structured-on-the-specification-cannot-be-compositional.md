# A proof system whose rules follow the shape of the specification cannot be compositional, and negation is where it gives itself away

**Lesson:** Any system of rules for establishing that an artifact meets a requirement has to be organized around the structure of one of the two things, and the choice determines what kind of work the system can support. Organize the rules around the structure of the artifact and each rule says: to establish this of a compound, establish these of its parts. That shape lets a claim about a component be discharged while the rest of the system is still a sketch, and lets the finished component be handed on with its guarantee attached. Organize the rules around the structure of the requirement instead — a rule for conjunction, a rule for implication, a rule for each modality — and every rule takes the artifact as an indivisible object it merely tests. Nothing about a part can be established before the whole exists.

Negation is where this becomes undeniable rather than merely inconvenient. The natural rule is that the artifact satisfies a negated requirement exactly when it fails to satisfy the requirement itself, which is not an inference from smaller facts but a verdict on an exhaustive search: you must be able to conclude that no proof exists. That obliges you to have the entire artifact in hand, since a missing part might have supplied the very behaviour whose absence you are asserting. Two costs follow immediately and both are practical rather than aesthetic. Verification becomes a phase that starts only when construction has ended, so it can no longer inform the design it is supposed to be checking. And you acquire a new and thankless obligation — proving that something does *not* satisfy a description — which a component-structured system never asks of anyone, because its rules only ever build positive facts out of positive facts.

The transferable rule is to look at what a verification approach demands as its unit of input before adopting it, and to be suspicious of expressive power in the requirement language that has no corresponding rule on the artifact side. Richness in the specification logic is genuinely attractive; it lets you say things you could not otherwise say. But each such construct has to be paid for by a rule, and a construct whose rule is a whole-artifact test converts the entire method from incremental to monolithic — the weakest link determines the granularity of the whole thing. It is better to accept a specification language that says slightly less and whose every construct decomposes, because the property you actually need from a verification method is that it survives contact with a system being built in pieces, by different people, over time.

**Source:** [Communicating Sequential Processes](../works/communicating-sequential-processes-book.md) — the discussion, in the CCS section of the mathematical-models chapter, of Milner's modal logic for specifying observable behaviour: the note that its calculus of correctness is based on the structure of the specification rather than the structure of the program, illustrated by the rule for negation under which a process satisfies a negated formula when it fails to satisfy the formula, with the consequence that the whole process must be written before its correctness proof can start — contrasted with the book's own satisfaction notation, under which the correctness proof of a compound process is assembled from the proofs of its parts and no proof that a process fails its specification is ever required.
