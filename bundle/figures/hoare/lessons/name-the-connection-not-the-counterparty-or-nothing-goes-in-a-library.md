# Name the connection, not the counterparty: addressing your partner by name buys a free safety property and costs you the library

**Lesson:** When two components must exchange values, there are two things you can give a name to: the other component, or the link between them. Naming the other component is the tempting choice because it eliminates a whole concept from the design. There is no notion of a connection to declare, no separate namespace to manage, and one important safety property comes for free rather than being checked — a link is between exactly two parties, with one sending and one receiving, because there is nothing else it could possibly be. Fewer primitives, an invariant enforced by construction, and no extra syntax. It looks like a clear win.

The cost lands somewhere that is not visible while you are designing the notation, and it is fatal to reuse. A component that names its partner has that partner's identity baked into its text, which means the generic version — a buffer, a doubler, a filter, the sort of thing you would expect to write once — cannot exist, because it would have to be rewritten for every user. You have made the connection an attribute of the participants rather than an object in its own right, so the participants are no longer separable from the arrangement they happen to be in. Parameterizing over the link restores this immediately: the same component text is instantiated at different links, and a library becomes possible. The general form of the mistake is worth carrying around, because it recurs far from concurrency — any time an entity refers to a specific peer instead of to an indirection that a caller supplies, it stops being a component and becomes a fragment of one particular system.

There is a second cost, less obvious and more structural. If parties are named inside the composition, then composition is inherently an operation over a whole group at once, with a variable number of arguments, rather than a binary operator you can apply twice. That difference is not cosmetic. A binary, associative operator is what lets you build a system in whatever order and grouping suits you, prove things about a two-part composite and then use it as a part, and reason about assembly independently of assembly order. A variadic construct offers none of this, and the algebra you would want to reason with does not exist. So the two costs are the same cost seen from two directions: naming the counterparty welds each component to its context, and it welds each composition to its arity. When choosing what gets a name in a design, ask which choice leaves the pieces separable — that usually matters more than which one removes a concept from the manual.

**Source:** [Communicating Sequential Processes](../works/communicating-sequential-processes-book.md) — the subsection of the discussion chapter comparing the book's notation with the earlier CSP proposal, where component processes carry unique names and communication commands address the peer process by name: the noted advantages that no concept of channel or channel declaration is needed and that it becomes logically impossible to violate the two-party, one-sender-one-receiver restriction, set against the two stated disadvantages — that a subordinate process must know the name of its using process, which complicates building libraries of subordinate processes, and that parallel composition becomes an operation with a variable number of parameters that cannot be reduced to a binary associative operator.
