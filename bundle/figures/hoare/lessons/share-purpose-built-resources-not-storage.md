# Share purpose-built resources whose operations are already atomic, never general storage guarded by a convention

**Lesson:** Sharing a general-purpose storage location among concurrent participants fails in a characteristic way. An update that reads, computes and writes back is three separate acts; two participants doing it at once produce an interleaving in which one contribution is silently lost. What makes this uniquely nasty is not the loss but its statistics: the corruption depends on timing, so it is rare, unreproducible and invisible to ordinary testing, and a system can run for years producing quietly wrong totals with nothing ever failing. Any technique whose correctness rests on every participant remembering to do something — enter a region, take a lock, signal on the way out — inherits the same property, because omitting the step produces no error, just a slightly increased probability of a wrong answer nobody will trace.

The robust alternative is to stop sharing storage and share a purpose-built thing instead, designed around what the sharers actually need to do with it. If the intent is counting, do not expose a location plus a discipline; expose a counter whose increment is one indivisible occurrence. The possibility of interference is then not prevented by anyone's care, it is absent from the vocabulary — there is no way to express the half-completed update because there is no half. The design question at each shared thing becomes: what are the operations its users genuinely perform, and can each be made a single act? Where the answer is yes, the protection is structural. Where the answer is no, you have learned something important about that resource before shipping it rather than afterwards.

Two further arguments, one about mechanism and one about reach. The single-processor trick of suppressing interruption for the duration of a delicate sequence delays response to everything else and, more decisively, stops working the day a second processor is added — a good example of a solution whose validity depends on an assumption nobody wrote down. And the purpose-built resource has a portability property the shared location cannot have: a design whose sharing happens through defined operations rather than through common memory runs unchanged on distributed hardware, on multiprocessors, and on one processor timesharing, whereas anything resting on physically shared store is confined to the last two. Choosing the operation-shaped design is therefore not only safer, it is the one that survives the machine changing underneath it.

**Source:** [Communicating Sequential Processes](../works/communicating-sequential-processes-book.md) — the shared storage section of the shared resources chapter, which argues against shared storage: the interference example where two interleaved read-modify-write pairs on a shared count increment it once instead of twice, with the observation that the fault is highly nondeterministic, not reliably reproducible and almost impossible to diagnose by conventional testing, and the author's suspicion that operating systems in current use regularly produce slightly inaccurate summaries and accounts; the criticism of inhibiting interrupts for a critical region as delaying response and failing entirely once a second processor is added; the note that a binary exclusion semaphore works only if every process observes the discipline, an omitted or misordered signal producing chaotic or subtly wrong behaviour; and the recommendation that protection be built into the design of each shared resource — an increment being a single atomic operation on a purpose-built counter — with pure storage never shared, which additionally yields designs implementable on networks of distributed processing elements as well as on shared-store machines.
