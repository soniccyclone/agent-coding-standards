# If the replacement formalism turns out just as complicated, the complication belonged to the problem and not to the notation you rejected

**Lesson:** A common origin story for a new abstraction is that someone found the existing one unbearable to explain. The explanation required textual substitution, renaming, and a mental expansion nobody would want to look at; the rules for which names were visible where had accumulated into a system of decorations; and the accumulated awkwardness was reason enough to go build something cleaner. That is a legitimate motivation and it often produces good work. What it does not produce, by itself, is evidence that the awkwardness was the notation's fault.

The test comes later, and it is worth actually running. Build the alternative, solve the same problems in it, and then compare the *finished solutions* rather than the initial impressions. If the clean formalism also needs several interlocking layers to control sharing safely, and the resulting programs are of comparable intricacy, then the intricacy was in the problem all along, and the choice between the two systems drops to a matter of taste and implementation efficiency rather than one of correctness or clarity. That is a genuinely useful conclusion, not a defeat: it tells you where further effort will and will not pay, and it stops you from repeating the migration a third time under the same misapprehension. It also lets you say something honest about the older system — that on a machine whose shape suits it, it may well be the better choice — instead of the usual retrospective story in which the thing you left behind was simply bad.

The habit generalizes well past language design. Whenever a rewrite is justified by "the old system was too complicated," ask which complication is being eliminated and which is being relocated. Accidental complication — ceremony, redundant declarations, explanations that require expanding a program in your head — genuinely disappears under a better notation. Essential complication is conserved: mutual exclusion, lifetime management, failure handling, the coordination of things that really do interact. A rewrite that reduces the first without touching the second is a real gain and should be described that way. A rewrite advertised as a simplification that in fact only moves the second into a different vocabulary has bought you a preference and charged you a migration. The way to tell which you have is to look at the complete new solution to the hardest old problem, and be willing to conclude that the parts you disliked were load-bearing.

**Source:** [Communicating Sequential Processes](../works/communicating-sequential-processes-book.md) — the closing paragraphs of the nested-monitors subsection of the discussion chapter, where Hoare records that Dijkstra's criticisms of the clumsiness of the PASCAL- and SIMULA-based notations, and of explanations given in terms of substitution and renaming, were what first impelled him toward designing communicating sequential processes, and then observes that the constructions of the shared-resources chapter make clear that control of sharing requires complication whichever framework it is expressed in, leaving the choice between the two languages partly a matter of taste or efficiency, with the shared-store implementation arguably favouring the older notation.
