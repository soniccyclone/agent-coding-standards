# Before betting on exhaustive search, work out whether you would be enumerating configurations or histories

**Lesson:** Replacing an argument with a machine search is attractive, and the arithmetic that decides whether it is viable can be done on the back of an envelope before any code is written. Two quantities are in play and they are separated by an exponential. Putting independent parts together multiplies their configuration counts, so configurations grow exponentially in the number of parts: five parties with six positions apiece, plus five contended items with three apiece, is already past a million. The sequences of events leading to those configurations grow exponentially in the *number of configurations*, because in most configurations several things could happen next and each choice forks the history. A search that walks configurations faces the first number; a search that walks histories faces two raised to the first number. Choosing to search without first establishing which of the two your procedure actually enumerates is how a plan that looked ambitious turns out to be flatly impossible.

The practical consequence is that the entire art of making exhaustive checking feasible consists of refusing to enumerate histories. Anything that lets you recognize that two different pasts have arrived at the same situation, and abandon the second, converts the hopeless quantity into the merely large one. That recognition is not free and it is not a property of the search program: it requires that whatever you carry as the current situation be a genuine summary of the past, such that two histories agreeing on it really do have identical futures. Establishing that is modelling work, done before any tool is chosen. Where it cannot be established — because some relevant fact about the past is not captured by the summary — you are stuck enumerating histories, and no amount of engineering on the checker will rescue you. So the first question about any exhaustive check is not how fast it runs but whether the model admits a finite summary of what has already happened.

When neither quantity is tractable the fallback is not a less thorough search; it is an argument, and the argument that replaces such a search has a characteristic shape worth learning because it looks nothing like a proof of a functional property. Take an arbitrary reachable situation. Invent a quantity that classifies it into a handful of cases. Show that in each case somebody can still move. It is case analysis over a partition you had to think up, and thinking up the right partition — how many parties are currently in a given phase — is the whole of the content; the rest is bookkeeping. Two things are worth noticing about the trade. The argument holds for any size of system, whereas a search only ever answers for the size it searched. And the partition, once found, is usually the clearest explanation available of why the design works at all, which the search would never have produced no matter how much of it completed.

**Source:** [Communicating Sequential Processes](../works/communicating-sequential-processes-book.md) — the deadlock-proof section of the dining philosophers example, which first gives an informal case analysis on the number of seated philosophers, the number eating and the number of raised forks, then considers programming a machine to explore all behaviours instead: noting that for a finite-state system it suffices to bound trace length by the number of states, that the states of a parallel combination are bounded by the product of the operands' states, computing the college's bound from six states per philosopher and three per fork, and observing that the number of traces to examine exceeds two raised to that power.
