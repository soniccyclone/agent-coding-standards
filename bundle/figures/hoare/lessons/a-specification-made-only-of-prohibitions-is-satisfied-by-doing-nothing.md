# A specification made only of prohibitions is satisfied by doing nothing, and no amount of tightening fixes that

**Lesson:** Define meeting a specification as: every observation you could make of the thing is described by the predicate. This is the natural definition, it supports a clean set of rules — conjoin two specifications and the same thing meets both, weaken a specification and everything that met the strong one still qualifies — and it has a defect that no particular predicate can repair. A thing that produces no observations meets every specification that anything could meet. The inert product is a universal solution. Writing a stricter predicate makes this worse rather than better, since strictness only removes candidates and the inert one is never among those removed. The defect is a property of the satisfaction relation, not of any specification written in it, which is why it survives arbitrarily careful requirements work.

This is not a curiosity of formal notation; it is the commonest failure of ordinary requirements documents, which are almost entirely prohibitions — never dispense goods that were not paid for, never accept a request while the previous one is outstanding, never exceed the limit — and are therefore met in full by a component that refuses everything, or by one that has crashed. So the first practical consequence is bookkeeping: sort your requirements into the ones saying what must not happen and the ones saying something must eventually happen, and notice how thin the second pile is. The second consequence is that the second pile needs an obligation of a different logical shape, quantified the other way — in every reachable situation, some continuation is available — and it cannot be derived from any quantity of the first kind. Evidence differs too. Prohibitions are refuted by exhibiting one bad run; progress claims are refuted by exhibiting a situation with no good run, which is a search over states rather than over histories.

There is a cheap way to discharge the progress obligation, and its expiry date is the part worth internalizing. Within a restricted fragment you can often make it structural: if the only way to build something that gets stuck is to write the stuck primitive explicitly, or to offer a choice among no alternatives, then any text containing neither cannot get stuck, and the check is a scan rather than a proof. That is a legitimate and very cheap method. But it is a theorem about a closed set of constructs, and the honest form of the claim names that set — because the construct that breaks it is composition. The moment two of these things are joined so that each constrains the other, both can be individually capable of continuing while sharing no continuation, and a guarantee that was free becomes the hardest obligation in the system. Any argument of the form "this cannot happen by construction" should therefore carry the list of constructions it ranges over, since every new combining form reopens it from scratch.

**Source:** [Communicating Sequential Processes](../works/communicating-sequential-processes-book.md) — the specifications section of the chapter on processes: the definition of a specification as a predicate over variables standing for observable aspects of behaviour, the satisfaction relation as all traces of the process satisfying the predicate, the laws for the weakest specification and for conjunction and weakening, the demonstration that the stopped process satisfies the vending machine's owner-and-customer specification by the expedient of doing nothing and hence satisfies every specification any process satisfies, and the closing remark that processes built only from prefixing, choice and guarded recursion never stop while that precaution ceases to be adequate once concurrency is introduced.
