# Let the rate budget fix how many components there are, and the formula's own shape fix how they are wired

**Lesson:** Two decisions normally made by taste can be made by arithmetic instead. How many concurrent components should there be? Divide the required output rate by what one component can accomplish in the available interval: if a result is due every interval and a single unit manages one operation per interval, then you need at least as many units as the result contains operations. That is a derived lower bound rather than a preference, and it turns "how parallel should this be" from a debate into a calculation. What structure should the components have? Take the formula the system is supposed to compute and split it algebraically into that many pieces, each within one unit's budget — most often by replacing a summation with its inductive definition, so that one closed expression becomes a chain of equations carrying one operation apiece.

The decomposition is then the architecture, not an input to designing one. Write a component per equation, connect components wherever their equations share a variable, and the resulting connection diagram is a drawing of the formula. Nothing about the wiring had to be invented: an intermediate quantity in the algebra is a channel, an inductive index is a position in the array, the base case is the component at one end, and the answer is whatever leaves the other. This matters because the alternative — sketch a topology, then work out what each node should compute — supplies no criterion for when to stop subdividing and no argument that the pieces reassemble into the intended answer. Here the correctness argument is the algebraic derivation you already performed to get the pieces, so it costs nothing extra and cannot drift from the design.

Two qualifications keep this from being oversold. The rate arithmetic is valid for a particular shape of problem: the same computation applied to every item of a stream, with results emerging at the input rate after some fixed initial delay. That shape is what lets a whole pipeline's throughput equal one stage's throughput, and it is the shape the calculation assumes, not parallelism in general. And the splitting should be pushed until each piece fills a unit's budget and then stopped. Pieces smaller than a unit's capacity buy nothing and cost a component and its connections, and every connection is another place where two parties must agree and therefore another opportunity for the assembly to seize up. The count you derived from dividing the rates is a target to hit, not a floor to leap past.

**Source:** [Communicating Sequential Processes](../works/communicating-sequential-processes-book.md) — the communications section of the communication chapter: the remark that the shape of a data flow network corresponds closely to the structure of the operands and operators in the expressions being computed, and the scalar-product example, which states a throughput requirement of one point per microsecond against a processor that needs nearly a microsecond for one input, multiplication, addition and output, concludes that at least as many processors as dimensions are required and that the design should therefore be an iterative array, then replaces the summation in the specification by its inductive definition to split it into a conjunction of component equations each containing at most one multiplication, and writes one process per equation with the connection diagram following directly.
