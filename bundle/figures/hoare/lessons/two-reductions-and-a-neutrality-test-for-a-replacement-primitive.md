# To replace an established primitive, owe two reductions in opposite directions — then admit only refinements that leave the reasoning rules untouched

**Lesson:** Proposing that a new construct should displace an entrenched one incurs two separate debts, and they point in opposite directions. First, encode the old primitive using the new construct: this settles that nothing expressible before has become inexpressible, which is the question users of the old thing actually have. Second, implement the new construct using the old primitive: this settles that the proposal is realizable on machinery that already exists, and yields the lowering path an implementation will follow. Neither proof substitutes for the other — the first is about adequacy, the second about feasibility — and a proposal missing either one is asking to be adopted on taste. The second direction has a further payoff: once the general encoding exists, the cases where a cheaper encoding suffices can be identified as recognizable syntactic situations, so the abstraction's cost becomes a schedule of known reductions rather than an unexamined overhead.

The trap adjacent to the second reduction is mistaking it for advice. An encoding written to demonstrate realizability is deliberately general and will be uglier than anything a person should write; it is machine output, and treating it as exemplary usage inverts the entire point. A construct introduced to replace a primitive comes with its own idiom, in the same way that a structured loop is not judged by the tangle of jumps it compiles into. Keep the reduction and the recommended style in separate compartments and say so, or readers will evaluate the proposal by the appearance of its implementation.

For the construct itself, start deliberately impoverished and treat every subsequent enrichment as needing an argument. The admissible argument is neutrality with respect to reasoning: an addition qualifies cheaply if every program that worked without it still works with it, no proof rule changes, and its only effect is on timing or resource use — an ordering hint among waiting parties, or a query that reveals information the caller could otherwise obtain only by maintaining its own bookkeeping. Additions of that kind can be judged purely on implementation cost, and the cost should be bounded a priori rather than estimated. Additions that change what must be proved are a different category entirely and must be argued on semantic grounds, however convenient they are. Keeping the two categories apart is what lets you concede to convenience without eroding the reasoning system, and it also lets you be honest about the concessions you have made: the temptation to enrich a synchronization primitive is permanent, resisting it is a matter of degree, and naming which resistances you abandoned and why is more useful than claiming you never did.

**Source:** [Monitors: An Operating System Structuring Concept](../works/monitors-an-operating-system-structuring-concept.md) — the single-resource example noted as a simulation of a Boolean semaphore and therefore a proof that monitors and conditions are not in principle less powerful, followed by the interpretation section's implementation of monitors and conditions in terms of semaphores together with its enumerated special cases where cheaper exits are valid, and its warning that the semaphore code is not intended to correspond to recommended style since the condition variable is a substitute for semaphores with its own style of use, as while loops are for jumps; also the deliberate primitiveness of the condition variable, the stated intention to resist more complex synchronization primitives, and the two concessions later admitted — the priority-parameterized wait and the queue-occupancy test — each justified by having no effect on program logic or proof rules and by bounded implementation cost.
