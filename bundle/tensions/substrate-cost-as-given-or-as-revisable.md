# Whether the substrate's cost is a specification or a temporary obstacle

## The decision
The design you want costs a large constant factor on the machine you have, in time and in space, on every operation. Not on an exceptional path that a cheap guard could route around, but flatly, everywhere, because the mechanism is the point. You can build it and accept that the numbers will be bad until the layer underneath changes, or you can conclude that the numbers are the design's actual specification and pick a different design. The question is what today's measured price is evidence of: a constraint the design must satisfy, or the shape of the previous generation's assumptions, which the design exists to break.

Note the scope. Where the expensive case is rare and a cheap check can send the common case down a full-speed path, this argument does not arise, and the separate question of whether a two-priced facility is legitimate is worked out in [cost models against uniform generality](cost-model-vs-uniform-generality.md). What is left here is the residue: no fast path is available, and everyone pays.

## Kay: the machine is frozen software, and the historically effective move is to build anyway
[Progress is the history of moving decisions later](../figures/kay/lessons/progress-is-moving-decisions-later.md) refuses to treat hardware as a boundary condition. The instruction set is a set of choices somebody made earlier and then froze, and they were usually frozen to make the programs of the previous era run well. Accepting them as fixed therefore means letting the assumptions behind last decade's software decide what this decade's programs are permitted to be, which is how careful local optimization ends up substituting for progress. Kay's reading of the accepted advances in computing supports him: each one moved a binding later, each one was resisted on efficiency grounds at the time, and each one was eventually absorbed into the machine. His posture is to build the deferred mechanism, carry the inefficiency in the open, show what it buys, and go get the substrate changed. He is explicit that this has repeatedly taken years, and equally explicit that it has repeatedly worked.

## Wirth: the promised improvement is the mechanism that makes the waste permanent and unmeasurable
[Every facility owes its user a cost model](../figures/wirth/lessons/every-facility-owes-the-user-a-cost-model.md) closes by aiming directly at that argument. Wirth's claim is not that the substrate will fail to improve. It is that substrates which promise to erase cost differences do it by absorbing enormous complexity of their own, down to needing an interpretive layer beneath the instruction set, and that the outcome of the absorption is not a cheap general mechanism but a mandatory one. The complicated behavior becomes the default, the simpler means stop being reachable, and the built-in overhead can no longer be measured against any baseline, because no baseline is left to measure it against. On that reading the improved substrate is not the debt being repaid. It is the debt being refinanced into a form nobody can audit. He also refuses the intermediate escape by name: a facility whose cost swings by large factors between situations that look nearly identical has no cost model at all, so the fallback of getting the good numbers when circumstances permit is not a partial answer, it is the failure mode.

## Lampson: the number on the machine in front of you is part of the specification
[The measured price of a primitive decides which program structures are available to you](../figures/lampson/lessons/the-price-of-a-primitive-decides-which-structures-you-can-think-in.md) supplies the reason today's cost cannot be waved off as an implementation detail. A price is not a footnote about how a construct was built, it is a determination of which designs its users are allowed to have. Expensive creation does not produce slow programs, it produces different programs, written around the expense, carrying their own bugs. Lampson's instruction is to ask what an operation costs on this system, in this runtime, today, and to let that set the grain, rather than importing a granularity from a paper written where the numbers were different. A design that reports nothing invites its users to guess, and they will guess conservatively.

## Hoare: past a modest multiple the cost is not yours to commit
[Don't pre-spend your users' efficiency budget](../figures/hoare/lessons/dont-pre-spend-your-users-efficiency-budget.md) prices the slack. Ten or twenty percent traded for clarity or safety is ordinary engineering judgment. A factor of two or ten in both time and space is a commitment the builder made with somebody else's resources, and the argument that hardware improvement will make it free is only honest at the small end. Hoare's deeper objection is about ownership: when performance is somebody else's magic arriving later, nobody in the room is responsible for it. The full allocation question is worked out in [who owns the efficiency budget](who-owns-the-efficiency-budget.md).

## Resolution
**LLM DECISION — Nathan may overturn.**

The line is whether you can name the party who would revise the substrate and the evidence that would move them. Kay's posture is not a bet that the hardware will improve on its own schedule; read the lesson again and the sequence he describes ends with persuading manufacturers, which is work, done by named people, over years. Where you can identify that party, whether it is your own microcode group, a runtime team in the same company, a standards process you sit on, or a compiler vendor who reads your benchmarks, then eating the factor is a financeable debt and Kay governs. Where you cannot, the sentence "the substrate will catch up" is a claim about a roadmap nobody in the room controls, and it is the same object as Hoare's promise of a sufficiently clever optimizer arriving later. Then Wirth governs, today's number is the specification, and the design that cannot afford it is the design that has been refuted.

Two things follow that are worth stating separately because they cut across the line rather than along it.

The duty to publish is not in dispute and applies to both sides. Lampson is a weaker ally to Wirth here than he first appears, because he does not forbid an expensive construct. He names activity creation as the one place his own implementation fell short of its goal, prints the number, and tells readers which usage pattern that pushes them toward. That is Kay's posture executed with an invoice attached. What Lampson forbids is an unpublished price, and a designer wearing a factor of ten in the expectation of future relief satisfies him completely by measuring it, printing it, and saying which program structures it currently rules out. This is also the discipline that keeps Kay's side honest over time, since a published number is the thing that later shows whether the substrate revision ever actually arrived, and Wirth's real fear is a world where that comparison has become impossible to make.

Wirth's veto is stronger than the fast-path answer can absorb. It is tempting to say the disagreement mostly evaporates because Kay's cheap-guard technique satisfies everyone, but Wirth explicitly rejects that construction: he treats a facility whose cost varies by large factors under nearly identical circumstances as the definition of a useless cost model, and a trap boundary is exactly such a variation. So the collision is wider than the flat-cost residue, and the conditions under which a two-priced facility is nonetheless legitimate are the subject of the separate resolution linked above. What is settled here is only the flat case.

**Strongest counter-argument:** the ownership test may have the history exactly backwards. The substrate revisions that actually rescued expensive abstractions came overwhelmingly from parties the designers did not control and could not have named in advance. Nobody at Sun owned a processor, and nobody writing early JavaScript owned anything at all, yet both got their factor of ten back from third parties who wrote optimizing runtimes years later because the usage was already there. Meanwhile the projects that did own their whole stack, the Lisp machines most of all, owned it right up until commodity hardware went past them. If that is the pattern, then what finances the debt is not control of the layer below but installed base large enough to make somebody else's optimization work worth doing, and the correct test is whether the design can attract enough usage while slow to be worth rescuing. That test would license Kay's posture for exactly the people my rule denies it to, and would deny it to integrated shops whose control of the substrate lets them stay slow unchallenged. Nathan should overturn this if he thinks adoption, not ownership, is what pays the interest.
