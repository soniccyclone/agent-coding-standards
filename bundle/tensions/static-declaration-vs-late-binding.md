# When a program commits to what a thing is: enforced declaration against deferred binding

## The decision
At what moment does a program commit to what a thing is, and what holds it to the commitment. Both sides want the same payoff and say so plainly: a large system you can restructure without fear, where a bad decision can be collapsed and rebuilt rather than lived with. Wirth gets there by moving the commitment as early as possible and making it uncircumventable. Kay gets there by pushing the commitment as late as it can go and keeping every part answerable only through behaviour. One says the machine should be able to enumerate, before the program runs, every place a change breaks. The other says the value of the design is measured by how few places have to be told anything at all.

## Wirth: an abstraction nobody can enforce is a convention, and conventions freeze architecture
[Unbreakable static typing is what makes abstraction real](../figures/wirth/lessons/unbreakable-static-typing-is-what-makes-abstraction-real.md) starts from a definition rather than a preference. An abstraction is a promise that certain internal facts cannot be observed from outside. If nothing in the language can enforce that promise, what you have is an agreement that holds until the first person in a hurry, and a type system with a sanctioned escape hatch delivers the vocabulary without the guarantee. This is why he insists a language's claim to support a discipline be judged by what it forbids rather than what it offers.

The part worth stealing is that his argument is not about catching mistakes. It is about the economics of changing your mind. A compiler that checks consistency across every boundary, particularly boundaries between separately compiled parts, converts restructuring from a gamble into a mechanical procedure: split the type, move the definition, and the machine hands you the exhaustive list of everything that no longer fits. Redesigns you would never risk otherwise become routine. Weak checking is therefore not merely a source of bugs, it is what makes architecture permanent, because every structural improvement carries risk nobody can bound. He also notes the sealed abstract data type as the thing that keeps being rediscovered under new names, which matters here: representation hiding is his idea too, not the opposition's.

## Kay: the whole history of the field is decisions moving later, and deferral is what makes redesign cheap
[Progress is the history of moving decisions later](../figures/kay/lessons/progress-is-moving-decisions-later.md) reads the accepted advances as a single process in which something fixed at construction time got moved to a later moment, and concludes that the question in front of a designer is rarely which mechanism but how late a decision can be bound before it becomes unaffordable. The family of designs that historically won pushed it latest.

[Never divide a system into things weaker than itself](../figures/kay/lessons/never-divide-a-system-into-things-weaker-than-itself.md) supplies the redesign argument that meets Wirth head on. If the only thing anyone outside a part can do is ask it for something, nobody outside depends on how it holds what it holds, and concrete representation can be postponed through design, through early implementation, sometimes through several releases, until the answers start arriving wrong or too slowly. You then choose on evidence. Under the divided scheme the representation sits in the interface from the first line, which means it is chosen at the moment of least information and revised at the moment of most cost. Kay's answer to Wirth's exhaustive-list machinery is that a design which needs the list has already lost: the sites should not have known enough to break.

## Resolution
**LLM DECISION — Nathan may overturn.**

Most of this conflict dissolves once you separate two things that both sides call binding, and the residue that survives has a sharp condition attached to it.

The two mechanisms defend against different kinds of change. Static checking governs what may be *said* across a boundary and gives you an exhaustive account of who said it. Deferred binding governs what may be *seen* through a boundary and reduces how many parties said anything in the first place. Those compose. A declared, statically enforced interface whose type is opaque, with the representation invisible and revisable, satisfies Wirth's requirement exactly and delivers Kay's postponement exactly, and it is not a compromise invented here: Wirth's own sealed abstract data type is that arrangement, and his module boundaries are where he wants the checking to bite hardest. Kay's central redesign claim, that concrete representation should be chosen late and on evidence, costs Wirth nothing at all. If a reader takes only one thing from this file it should be that representation-deferral is not the disputed territory.

What does not compose is conformance. Kay wants the set of things a component will answer to be settled when the message arrives, by the receiver, possibly for a receiver that did not exist when the sender was compiled, and with the receiver free to decline based on who is asking. There is no way to check that exhaustively before running, and Wirth's rule specifically forbids the escape hatch that would let it through. This is a real disagreement about the same decision at the same layer, and the condition that decides it is whether the population of participants is closed at the moment of checking.

Closed means every part that will ever send or receive is present in one build, from source, compiled together. Then Wirth is straightforwardly right, and the argument is not about safety, it is about the enumeration. The checker can produce a complete list of affected sites, so the collapse-and-rebuild move is cheap, and deferral buys you nothing you could not get from an opaque type. Open means participants arrive after the sender shipped, users extend the running system, or the system is never off long enough to have a build moment. Then exhaustive static checking is not inconvenient, it is undefined, because there is no instant at which all the sites exist to be listed. Kay is right there, and Wirth's mechanism does not so much lose the argument as fail to have a place to stand. Notice that this line runs through projects, not through languages: the same statically typed program is closed at its module graph and open at its plugin boundary and at every place it reads data somebody else wrote, and the honest design puts the enforced declarations on the first and behavioural negotiation on the second.

This is the same axis as [cost model against uniform generality](cost-model-vs-uniform-generality.md), which asks what a facility may conceal about price rather than about identity, and the two resolutions agree on their underlying test: deferral is legitimate when the thing being deferred is invisible to the caller's correctness, and illegitimate when the caller is obliged to reason about it and has been given no way to.

**Strongest counter-argument:** the closed world is a fiction with a shelf life. Every system that lives long enough acquires plugins, hot patches, serialized data written by an older version of itself, and a network peer running last quarter's build, and at that point the exhaustive check covers a shrinking fraction of the real boundaries while the parts of the program shaped around it are the parts that cannot bend. If the closed case is only ever the first few years, then designing for it buys checking you will later be forced to escape from, and Wirth's own no-escape-hatch rule turns into precisely the frozen architecture he wrote the lesson to prevent. The counter would say the correct default is Kay's, with static checking applied as a local optimization inside components rather than as the thing holding the system together, and that the burden is on the closed-world claim to prove it stays true.
