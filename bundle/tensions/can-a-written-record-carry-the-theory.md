# Handing off a system: fund the written design or fund the overlap with the people who hold it

## The decision
The builders of a working system are dispersing, or a newcomer has to take over a branch of it, and there is a fixed budget for making that survivable. It can be spent on artifacts, by writing down the design precisely enough that someone else can continue from it, or on people, by overlapping the departing and arriving staff so the understanding transfers by apprenticeship. Naur and Parnas both address exactly this and point the money in opposite directions.

## Naur: the transferable state lives in people and does not survive extraction
Naur's claim in [A system is alive only while someone still holds its theory](../figures/naur/lessons/a-system-is-alive-only-while-someone-holds-its-theory.md) is not that documents are lazy or inaccurate. It is that the thing you need is a grasp of how the program's structure corresponds to the world it acts on and where the correspondence was never meant to reach, and that this grasp is only exercised when someone is asked a question nobody anticipated. Someone rebuilding that grasp from surviving artifacts is stuck between fidelity to the confusions actually present in the text and the coherent account they are forming, and what they end up with is a different understanding wearing the old program's clothes. The mismatch is invisible until a change that looks right behaves badly. From that he draws two hard conclusions: overlap between leaving and arriving people is load-bearing schedule rather than a courtesy, and once the builders are genuinely gone, re-solving the problem from the requirement can cost no more than excavating the old solution and leaves you with something somebody understands.

## Parnas: the durable asset is the design stopped short on purpose
Parnas in [The artifact worth maintaining is the unfinished design](../figures/parnas/lessons/the-deliverable-is-the-unfinished-design-not-the-working-program.md) is arguing against finishing one variant and cutting the rest from it, because completion settles questions that only the first variant's circumstances posed and every descendant inherits settlements tuned to a load it never carries. The alternative requires that the common ancestor be a real written artifact rather than a remembered stage. Once it exists it does more than prevent inheritance: two live variants that diverge below it can share it as valid documentation for both, a proposed change can be evaluated against it once rather than twice, and branches that do not feed each other information can proceed in parallel. His handover claim is narrower than it first looks. He says completing a documented partial design is bounded in a way that modifying somebody else's finished program is not, which concedes Naur's point about finished programs and stakes the claim only on the deliberately unfinished one.

## Resolution
**LLM DECISION — Nathan may overturn.**

They are not funding the same thing, and the line between them is whether the incoming change lies on an axis of variation the design anticipated. Parnas's partial design does not transmit Naur's theory. It shrinks the set of questions that need the theory, by stating which decisions are fixed for the whole family and which remain open. A newcomer completing a branch does not need to know why the family was carved this way; they need to know what is settled and what is theirs to settle, and the document answers exactly that. Naur's impossibility claim is stated about unanticipated questions, and his own lesson is explicit that the loss shows up when somebody asks for a change and nobody can say intelligently how to accommodate it. A change on a stated axis of variation is by construction anticipated. A change off it is the case Naur is describing, and there the document goes silent, because it never claimed to fix anything in that direction.

So the mapping is: the written partial design covers movement inside the anticipated space cheaply and without people, and overlap with the builders buys the ability to move outside it at all. Neither substitutes for the other, and the diagnostic question at handover is not "how good is our documentation" but "which of the changes we expect over the next two years fall on axes the design named." If most of them do, fund the record. If the roadmap is mostly off-axis, the record will not save you and the overlap is the only thing that will.

Two details are worth keeping straight. First, Naur's rewrite recommendation composes with Parnas rather than contradicting him. Rebuilding from a surviving partial design is cheaper than rebuilding from the requirement, precisely because there is less settled matter to excavate and the settled matter that remains was written to be read rather than inferred from code. Parnas's record is the better thing to rewrite from, which is a stronger claim for the record than the one Parnas makes and one Naur has no reason to reject. Second, the anecdote in [A design record is worth what its timing allows](../figures/parnas/lessons/a-design-record-is-worth-what-its-timing-allows.md), where a documentation exercise got a dead program running, is often read as revival from documentation and is not. It is revival by the act of documenting, performed by people making a systematic pass over the system, which is Naur's own account of how understanding is acquired: by working the material rather than by reading a description of it. The artifact was the residue, not the agent.

**Strongest counter-argument:** the layer split holds only if axes of variation can be identified in advance, and Parnas's own program-families argument is that the hard part is choosing them well. If a team names its axes badly, the partial design is a document about a variation space nobody wants, and the changes that arrive are all off-axis. That failure mode is common enough that "fund the record" may be advice that rarely pays out in practice even though it is correct in principle, which would push the honest recommendation back toward Naur's unconditional version. There is also a sharper attack: even inside the anticipated space, a newcomer completing a branch has to judge whether their completion is in the spirit of the design, and Parnas's document states constraints rather than intent. Two completions can both satisfy the written common core and one of them can be wrong in the way Naur means, with the mismatch surfacing years later. If that is right, the bounded task is bounded in scope but not in judgment, and the theory is still doing load-bearing work on axes the document appeared to cover.
