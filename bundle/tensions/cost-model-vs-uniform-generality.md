# Whether one facility may have two costs: cost models against uniform generality

## The decision
You are designing a facility that everyone will use constantly — how a variable holds a value, how an operation reaches its operands, how a call finds its target. You can specialize it, giving each realization its own name and its own price, or you can offer one general form whose realization varies underneath: cheap when the operands are ordinary, expensive when they are not. The question is whether the second arrangement is a legitimate service to the programmer or a withdrawal of information they need.

## Wirth: a facility that cannot quote its own price has not earned the right to hide its mechanism
[Every facility owes its user a cost model it can explain by itself](../figures/wirth/lessons/every-facility-owes-the-user-a-cost-model.md) sets a test that is harder than it first sounds. It is not enough that a mechanism be documented; each one must be describable without reference to any of the others, closely enough that someone reading a program can estimate the work it does. The independence clause is the sharp part. A construct whose implementation you can only explain by enumerating what else happens to appear nearby has no cost model at all, and the programmer is guessing. Wirth's worked case is the pair of individually reasonable features that destroy each other in combination: dense static layout is sound only because the compiler knows every offset, so admitting sizes fixed at run time forces indirection and takes down the whole storage scheme that justified the first feature.

He then names the obvious escape and refuses it. Having the compiler emit good code where it can and fall back otherwise buys you a bulky and unreliable optimizer, and it leaves the user with a facility whose cost swings by large factors between situations that look nearly the same. That swing is precisely what makes a cost model useless. His last move is aimed at the future: distinctions the substrate promises to erase are usually erased by absorbing enormous complexity underneath, until the complicated path is the default, the simple path is no longer reachable, and the built-in waste cannot even be measured against an alternative.

## Kay: buy the deferral only in the case that needs it
[Progress is the history of moving decisions later](../figures/kay/lessons/progress-is-moving-decisions-later.md) reads the accepted advances in computing as one process in which something fixed at construction time got moved to a later moment, and treats the efficiency objection as a solved recurring problem rather than a fresh cost each time. The technique: arrange the ordinary case to run at exactly the speed it would have had with no generality present, guarded by a check cheap enough to be concurrent with the work, so the general machinery is entered only when the operands are not ordinary. The generality is then free in the case that dominates, and the cases where it costs something are the ones the specialized design could not have handled at all. Better yet, fold the discrimination into a lookup the machine already performs and pay nothing extra even for the check.

Behind that is a claim about the substrate that Wirth's closing paragraph denies directly. Hardware is not a fixed constraint that software accommodates; it is a set of earlier decisions frozen to make the previous generation of programs run well, and treating it as given is how local optimization comes to stand in for progress. Build the deferred mechanism, wear the inefficiency, show what it buys, and expect the substrate to follow.

## Ingalls: make the price constant so no storage site has to plan
[Make reference uniform](../figures/ingalls/lessons/make-reference-uniform-so-generality-costs-nothing-per-use.md) carries the argument down to representation. If every value is designated the same way and at the same width, a variable is a plain memory cell that can hold anything, and generality at each storage site is free rather than something the site's author arranges in advance. Under a scheme where different kinds of value are referred to differently, every container, parameter and field must commit to a kind, and that commitment then propagates outward into every procedure that touches it.

## Resolution
**LLM DECISION — Nathan may overturn.**

Both are right, and the line between them is whether the discriminator that selects the cheap or the expensive realization can be written down and stays written down. Wirth's requirement is not that a facility have one cost; it is that a reader of the program can estimate the cost. A two-branch facility satisfies that test whenever the predicate selecting the branch is stated in the manual, is a property of the data or of a declaration the programmer wrote themselves, and does not move between releases. It fails the test whenever the predicate is the optimizer's private notion of what it managed to prove.

That reading is not a stretch, because it is exactly the distinction Wirth's own text draws. The escape he rejects by name is a compile-time best effort, where the boundary is undocumented, non-local, and revisable by whoever ships the next compiler. His failure case is a feature whose cost depends on an unrelated declaration somewhere else in the program. Kay's arrangement has neither property. The trap boundary sits on a stable, architectural fact about the operands, and a programmer who knows that small integers add in one instruction and large ones allocate has a cost model with two entries and a stated predicate between them. Two branches with published prices is not the absence of a cost model. One branch with a price nobody can quote is.

Ingalls is on the safe side of the same line for the opposite reason. Uniform reference does not vary at all; it pays a fixed price everywhere to buy the elimination of variance. Wirth may object to that price on hardware-affinity grounds, and often should, but he cannot object to it on cost-model grounds, and his own lesson does not.

What survives the reconciliation is the substrate argument, and there the two are in flat disagreement about the same thing. Kay says build the mechanism and expect the machine to be revised beneath it. Wirth says the revision arrives as an interpretive layer that makes the complicated behavior mandatory and the simple path unreachable. History gave each of them their case: dynamic dispatch, automatic reclamation and just-in-time specialization did become universal, and it also became true that nobody can predict what a line of managed code costs, and that no measurement of the built-in overhead against a simpler baseline is available to anyone. The condition that decides which of them you should follow is not about the mechanism. It is about whether your project ever needs to make a local performance argument that someone else must accept — a deadline that must be certified, a budget that must be defended, a path that must not allocate. If it does, Wirth's rule governs and the two costs get two names. If the dominant expense is the restructuring you would otherwise not attempt, Kay's rule governs and the trap is worth its unpredictability.

This connects directly to [one interface over two cost profiles](one-interface-over-two-cost-profiles.md), where the same question is asked about failure modes rather than speed, and the answer turns on a related property: how many realizations are live inside a single running program.

**Strongest counter-argument:** the stability of the discriminator is a property of the design as documented, not of the design as it ages, and Wirth's real point may be that no such boundary stays stable. Smalltalk's small-integer trap was clean; its descendants accumulated inline caches, escape analysis, tiered compilation and deoptimization, and the predicate that once read "operands fit in a tagged word" is now a hundred heuristics that nobody can state and that change with the runtime version. If the fast-path-plus-trap pattern reliably decays into the optimize-when-possible compiler Wirth named, then the distinction proposed here is a distinction about the first release only, and Wirth is simply right that the arrangement should be refused at the start because there is no stopping point after it.
