# Should the notation you design in be made to run

## The decision
You have a notation precise enough to state what a system means while the design is still open. It is expressive, it says what happens without committing to storage, and it is exact enough that someone could in principle build a translator for it. Do you build the translator? Hoare says deliberately not, and keep two languages. Kay says the two-artifact arrangement is the thing to eliminate.

## Hoare: an unimplemented design language keeps the expensive choices visible
[Keep the notation you design in deliberately unimplemented](../figures/hoare/lessons/keep-the-design-notation-deliberately-unimplemented.md) makes two arguments and they are separable. The first is about process. The operations that make a design notation pleasant are the ones that treat a large aggregate as a single value: whole-structure copy, concatenation, set-valued expressions. Removing them, and choosing the representation that replaces them, is the actual work of refinement, and it routinely forces the algorithm and the data layout to change together. A compiler cannot perform that work. It can only pick a representation and conceal that a decision was ever available, so making the notation executable deletes the obligation without improving the outcome. The second argument is about the designer. Anything that runs has a cost model, competent people learn which constructs are slow, and they stop reaching for them while thinking rather than merely while coding. The vocabulary for forming ideas contracts to whatever compiles well on current machines, and a fixed implemented notation additionally teaches people to stop inventing notation fitted to the problem in front of them, because only what the implementation already understands feels legitimate. Both losses are invisible from the inside, since you never see the design you failed to consider.

## Kay: an unexecuted description is an untested one, and it will be wrong
[Anything precise enough to be worth writing will contain mistakes](../figures/kay/lessons/anything-worth-stating-precisely-must-be-runnable.md) attacks the assumption underneath the two-language arrangement, that a description is more trustworthy than an implementation because it is shorter and more abstract. Kay's point is that a statement precise enough to constrain an implementation is precise enough to be wrong in the same ways an implementation is wrong: a case not considered, a relation stated backwards. The only difference is that nothing exercises the errors. Large proofs need debugging like large programs. Once you grant that the description will need debugging, the question becomes what tool does the debugging, and the only general-purpose answer anyone has is running it. He is careful that this is a demand on the languages you build rather than permission to skip the description, and he pre-empts the obvious defence by noting that specification notations usually fail to run because of a decision about their purpose rather than a limit on what they can express.

## Resolution
**LLM DECISION — Nathan may overturn.**

Run the design notation, keep the executor deliberately naive, and refuse to let anything it produces be the deliverable. What Hoare is actually protecting against is not execution but promotion, and the two can be separated.

Take his arguments in order. The refinement argument objects to collapsing two stages into one, so that the representation choice is made silently by a compiler instead of loudly by a person. That harm arrives when the executed design becomes the shipped artifact. It does not arrive when execution is used only to interrogate the description, because refinement remains a mandatory downstream step performed by a human and the run tells you nothing about how to perform it. The cost-model argument is sharper but it depends on a gradient. What people internalize is a ranking, that concatenation costs a hundred times a pointer swap, and a ranking requires the implementation to be differentially fast. An interpreter that is uniformly slow, where set-valued expressions and scalar assignment sit in the same order of magnitude because nothing has been optimized, presents no gradient to learn. Kay's standing disposition of wearing the inefficiency and expecting the substrate to be revised later is exactly what keeps the cost model flat, so the arrangement Kay would build is not the one Hoare is warning about.

The switching condition, stated so a reader can locate themselves: does the executor create a cost gradient across the notation's constructs, and does its output reach the deliverable? Two noes and you get Kay's benefit, a debugger for your description, without Hoare's harm. Either yes and Hoare is right, because you have either taught your designers an expense ranking or you have merged the stages he wants kept apart.

Parnas sits usefully between them on the narrow question of how errors in a description get found. [A design record is worth what its timing allows](../figures/parnas/lessons/a-design-record-is-worth-what-its-timing-allows.md) claims the rigour of writing precisely is itself an analysis that surfaces duplicates, near-duplicates and outright defects, which is a third answer to Kay's question about what debugs a description. It is a real answer and it is not sufficient: it finds the errors that show up as structural inconsistency and misses the ones that show up only as behaviour.

**Strongest counter-argument:** Hoare's second loss may not depend on a cost gradient at all. His point about users ceasing to invent notation of their own is about a notation being *fixed and implemented*, not about it being fast. Any implementation, however slow, closes the expressible set to what its implementer anticipated, and the designer who wants a new construct for the structure of the problem in front of them now faces the cost of extending an interpreter rather than the cost of writing a definition. Kay's answer is that the language must be trivially extensible by its users, which is the whole of his program, but that answer asks every designer to be a language implementer and quietly prices that at zero. There is also a durability objection: an interpreter deliberately kept slow is institutionally unstable. Somebody will optimize it, and the moment they do the gradient appears and the flat-cost discipline that made the resolution work is gone, with no mechanism to notice. If both of those land, the honest reading is that Hoare's abstinence is the stable arrangement and Kay's is the one that only survives under a discipline nobody sustains.
