# When a partition makes an invariant uncheckable, does the node withhold the answer or emit it and repair afterwards

## The decision
The network severs, a node holds a request whose correctness depends on state it can no longer see, and it has exactly two moves. It can refuse to answer until contact returns, preserving the invariant at the cost of being unavailable for however long the partition lasts. Or it can answer on local information, accepting that the invariant may be broken during the window and that the breakage will have to be found and mended afterwards. Both sides of this tension address that instant and prescribe opposite defaults.

## Lamport and Lynch: stalling is free, corrupting is not
Lamport's split in [Split every correctness claim into safety and liveness, and never let one pay for the other](../figures/lamport/lessons/split-correctness-into-safety-and-liveness.md) is not a taxonomy, it is an asymmetric budget. Safety claims are refuted by a finite prefix of an execution, which means a violation is a fact about the run that no later behavior can retract. Liveness claims are refuted only by an infinite execution, which means a delay is never a violation, only a postponement. Since the impossibility results forbid holding both unconditionally in an asynchronous world, you get to choose which one absorbs the loss, and the shapes decide for you: the half that can be repaired by waiting is the half you weaken. Lynch makes the same assignment mechanically in [Let timing assumptions buy you progress and nothing else](../figures/lynch/lessons/keep-timing-assumptions-out-of-safety.md), and adds the argument that the split is forced rather than tidy. State your timing assumption in the honest form, that the network calms down at some unknown future point, and any protocol correct under it is automatically safe against a network that never calms down at all, because the finite prefix ending in a violation extends into a run where timing does eventually stabilize. A timeout, on this reading, is not evidence about the world. It is permission to try something else.

## Brewer: the invariant was never the machine's to keep
[Treat the rare failure as a mode with an entry, a discipline, and an exit](../figures/brewer/lessons/manage-the-partition-as-an-event.md) starts from the observation that a strongly consistent system is silently protecting invariants its designer never wrote down, and that the protection is a subsidy the designer stops paying the moment availability is chosen. The work that replaces the subsidy is an inventory: name every invariant, decide per operation whether it may be risked during the window, and attach a restoration or a compensation to each one that can break. Some breakages merge back mechanically. Some escaped, because the machine dispensed cash or sent a message, and those can only be compensated. The closing claim is the sharp one and it is not a plea for looser engineering: commerce ran for centuries on ledgers, audits and reversing entries, and in that tradition correctness has never meant that no error occurs, only that every error is detected and settled. A system that stalls a withdrawal for the duration of a partition has not avoided an error. It has chosen a different error, one with no compensating entry and no audit trail, and it has chosen it silently.

## Resolution
**LLM DECISION — Nathan may overturn.**

The two rules govern different sides of a boundary, and the boundary is repairability without a second party's consent. Inside it, Lamport and Lynch hold without exception. Outside it, Lamport's rule has nothing to enforce and Brewer's regime is the only discipline available.

Trace what each side's invariant is actually a property of. A Lamport safety property constrains the traces of the machine: which values a replica may store, which log entry may occupy slot seventeen, which of two conflicting writes wins. The machine owns every bit that property talks about, so it can always hold the property by declining to move, and declining to move costs latency and nothing else. Brewer's inventory contains properties of a different kind. An account balance, an inventory count, a seat allocation, a delivered message are facts about a world the machine models but does not own. When the machine emits an irreversible effect into that world it leaves its own trace algebra, and no amount of local restraint keeps the world-level invariant, because the world-level invariant was never in the machine's gift. Worse, refusal is itself an emission at that boundary: a withdrawal not dispensed is a customer standing at a broken machine, which is a fact with its own cost and no undo either. The stall-versus-corrupt asymmetry that makes Lamport's rule free stops being free the instant both options write to the world.

So the test at the moment of decision is whether the system can restore the invariant later using only state it owns. Merging concurrent edits, reconciling a counter that only climbs, picking a winner between conflicting writes: all unilateral, so stall or reconcile as you like, but never let a replica commit a value the reconciliation cannot undo. Reversing an overdraft, refunding an oversold seat, retracting a shipped package: repair requires cooperation from someone outside the system, and once the repair is out of band the machine's guarantee has to be restated. That restatement is where the two sides rejoin. Brewer's regime does not abandon Lamport's frame, it re-enters it one level up. The compensable operation still owes safety properties, just about the record rather than about the balance: every risked operation is logged with the invariant it risked, the exposure during the window is bounded, and the reconciliation obligation is discharged within a stated interval. Those properties are machine-owned, so they hold unconditionally in exactly Lamport's sense, and Lynch's degraded-mode discipline is the instruction to write them down with the same rigor as the strong ones instead of leaving the fallback path to be whatever the code happens to do. Brewer's own lesson concedes the boundary from the other direction when it says to delay externally visible actions while the truth is unknowable. That is a stall, and he is prescribing it precisely where the emission would be irreversible and the exposure unbounded.

The practical shape: run Brewer's inventory before writing replication code, sort each invariant by whether its violation is unilaterally repairable, hand the repairable ones to Lamport and Lynch with no negotiation, and for the rest spend the design effort on bounding and recording the exposure rather than on pretending the machine can prevent it. This connects directly to [one-agreed-log-vs-per-operation-ordering](one-agreed-log-vs-per-operation-ordering.md): the inventory Brewer demands and the per-operation declaration Liskov demands are the same act of forcing a designer to name what each operation actually protects, and the same discipline that shrinks the set of operations needing agreement shrinks the set needing compensation.

**Strongest counter-argument:** the boundary is drawn in the wrong place, and drawing it there quietly restores the advice Brewer wrote his correction to attack. Almost every availability decision that matters commercially concerns state entirely inside the machine. Inventory counts, seat reservations, unique username claims, rate limits: nothing physical escapes, every one of them is unilaterally repairable in principle, and my rule therefore says stall. But nobody stalls those, and they are right not to, because overselling three seats out of four hundred and refunding them is cheaper than being unavailable for the duration of a transatlantic partition. If that is the common case, the real line is not who can repair the violation but whether the violation is detectable and its cost boundable, which cuts straight across the machine boundary and leaves my resolution describing a rare edge rather than the decision people actually face. Nathan could overturn this by replacing the repairability test with a cost test and keeping Lamport's rule only for invariants whose violation is undetectable, which is a much smaller set and a genuinely different design posture.
