# Whether to buy a construct by weakening a reasoning principle or to decline the construct

## The decision
A construct you want breaks a rule that currently holds uniformly everywhere in your logic. You can admit the construct and weaken the rule to accommodate it, or you can keep the rule intact and do without the construct. Reynolds gives both answers as general rules, and both of them name recursively defined functions as the case in point.

## Reynolds paying: run the accounting, then take the smallest restriction that restores the theorem
[Every expressive power you add is paid for out of a reasoning principle](../figures/reynolds/lessons/every-added-power-is-paid-for-out-of-a-reasoning-principle.md) treats the interesting question about a new construct as which existing guarantee stops holding, not whether the construct can be given a meaning. General recursion and the weakened conditional it drags along do not merely dent the theorem that implementations agreeing on their abstract types agree everywhere; they destroy it. The construct is kept anyway, and the bill is paid on the reasoning side by narrowing which correspondences are permitted to ones closed under the limits recursion introduces. Some previously legitimate correspondences are lost. The lesson's discipline is to find that restriction deliberately and record it as standing rather than discover it when a proof fails.

[If your specifications grow quadratically as you mention more things, the fix is a new connective](../figures/reynolds/lessons/build-the-invariant-into-the-connective.md) makes the same trade with the price tag fully visible. Separating conjunction cannot be layered over the ordinary connectives, and the logic that results is missing structural rules everyone has internalised: a description no longer implies two copies of itself, and a fact joined onto a description cannot be dropped again. The instruction is to expect familiar steps to stop working and to treat that as what the scaling cost.

## Reynolds refusing: uniformity of the assertion language is not for sale
[Every expression you can write in the program must be legal in an assertion about it](../figures/reynolds/lessons/keep-the-expression-language-usable-inside-assertions.md) identifies one assumption as load-bearing for the entire proof method and defends it against two attractive features. Block expressions let a statement hide inside an expression, and an expression that changes state has no meaning inside a claim about a single state. Recursively defined function procedures are conceded to be clear and elegant, and are declined anyway, because such a function applied to an out-of-range argument denotes a nonterminating computation and an assertion has no room for a subterm that denotes nothing. Both refusals rest on the same observation about what the repair would look like. Once such expressions exist you can no longer say that an assertion may contain any expression; you have to say any expression except these, and every rule already written needs that exception grafted on. The closing rule generalises: when a feature forces expression to split into the kind you can reason with and the kind you cannot, give up the feature rather than the uniformity.

## Resolution
**LLM DECISION — Nathan may overturn.**

The line is the shape of the repair, not the size of the loss. Pay when the repair is a uniform narrowing, where afterwards you still have one notion of the thing and every rule reads exactly as it did before, only over a smaller range. Refuse when the repair partitions a syntactic category, so that every rule mentioning that category grows a side condition selecting which kind it means.

Reynolds' own three cases sort themselves on that line without strain. The completeness restriction on admissible relations leaves the abstraction theorem stated in the same words; there is still one notion of admissible relation, it simply has fewer inhabitants, and no rule anywhere acquires a case. Separation logic is a larger loss and still uniform: contraction and weakening are gone everywhere, for every assertion, and what you are left with is a weaker logic rather than a logic with exceptions in it. Block expressions and recursive function procedures do something structurally different. They do not shrink what you may conclude, they fork what the word expression denotes, and the fork propagates into every rule that ever mentions an expression.

The reason to trust that line rather than treat it as convenient bookkeeping is that Reynolds already prices its two sides differently elsewhere. [A case split in a basic law is a prediction of where the bugs will be](../figures/reynolds/lessons/a-case-split-in-a-basic-law-predicts-where-the-bugs-will-be.md) says a conditional appearing in the most elementary statement about a representation forecasts a class of defects, because some code will handle one branch and not the other. A uniform narrowing costs you proofs you can no longer construct, and you find that out the moment you try. A category split costs a side condition on every rule from now on, and you find that out when somebody omits one.

There is also a genuine layer difference underneath, worth naming even though it does not by itself settle the case: one lesson is doing metatheory of a calculus and the other is fixing which programs a Hoare logic will accept assertions about. The two recursions are not the same object either. A fixed-point operator on domains is not a partial function procedure appearing inside an assertion. But neither lesson scopes itself that way and both close with a rule stated for features in general, so the layer observation explains why Reynolds never noticed the collision without telling a reader facing a new construct what to do.

**Strongest counter-argument:** the distinction may not survive contact with how the costs actually land. every-added-power says outright that the repair it prescribes narrows what may be assumed in ways that only bite much later, in a proof nobody has written yet, which is why it insists the restriction be recorded as a standing constraint. That is precisely the deferred, unbounded, discovered-by-the-next-person cost I attributed only to the category split. If a uniform narrowing can silently disqualify a proof anyone might attempt, then it is not observable at the point of trade and my line has no load-bearing difference in it. The better line under that reading is who pays: a category split taxes everyone who writes or reads a rule, forever, while a narrowing taxes only whoever turns out to need an excluded case. That is a defensible alternative, it sorts the same three cases the same way, and it makes the shape of the repair a symptom rather than the criterion. If Nathan prefers it, the practical guidance barely changes but the justification does, and the incidence-of-cost version is the one that would extend to a case where the two criteria disagree.
