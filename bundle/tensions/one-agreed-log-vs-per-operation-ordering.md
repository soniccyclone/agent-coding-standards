# Does every operation in a replicated service pass through one agreed order, or does each operation declare the order it needs

## The decision
You are designing a replicated service and you have to fix, before anything else, how ordering is decided. One answer makes ordering a property of the service: there is a single numbered sequence of commands, every operation enters it, and a replica's state is whatever you get by applying that sequence deterministically. The other makes ordering a property of each request: the service publishes a small ladder of ordering strengths, every operation names the rung it needs, and the machinery charges it only for that. Both sides call this the first design question, and they answer it opposite ways.

## Lamport: one agreement mechanism, and anything ordering itself outside it is an unproved second one
[Reduce every distributed coordination problem to agreeing on one sequence of commands](../figures/lamport/lessons/reduce-coordination-to-an-agreed-command-sequence.md) is an argument about where proof effort goes, not about throughput. The zoo of coordination problems collapses into a single primitive because determinism plus identical input order forces identical replicas, which means the application layer's correctness argument becomes trivial for free. You pay for that once, in the agreement protocol, and the payment is nonrecurring. The red-flag rule follows directly: if some part of the design decides ordering by its own means, you now have two mechanisms that can settle the same question, one of them proved and one of them invented on the spot by somebody who was optimizing. Reconfiguration folding into the machine state is the demonstration that the reduction is not a simplification of convenience but genuinely total. Fewer distinct agreement mechanisms is the whole measure of whether a human or a checker can verify the thing.

## Liskov: the strictest operation should not set the price for the rest
[Consistency strength belongs to the operation, not to the system](../figures/liskov/lessons/consistency-strength-is-a-per-operation-choice.md) starts from an accounting observation that is hard to argue with once stated. A guarantee owned by the infrastructure is necessarily set by whichever operation has the strictest requirement, so one rare administrative action drags the entire ordinary traffic up to its cost, permanently, to protect an invariant that concerns almost none of it. Making the strength a declared property of the request charges each one for what it actually needs. The part that matters more than the latency saving is what the declaration forces out of the designer: to name a rung you have to say what invariant the operation protects, and that is a question most systems never ask, because the infrastructure answered it silently. The cheap rung is also a superset rather than a weakening, since declaring everything strong reproduces the conventional design while still letting a read state exactly how stale it will tolerate being.

## Resolution
**LLM DECISION — Nathan may overturn.**

These are consecutive steps in one design, not rival architectures, and the seam between them is the question of whether an operation's postcondition mentions anybody else. Liskov's ladder is the procedure for deciding which operations must be ordered against each other. Lamport's rule governs how the ordering is implemented for every operation that comes back yes. Run in that order they do not compete, and each one repairs the other's characteristic failure.

The test that sorts operations is whether the operation's correctness can be stated entirely against what its own caller previously saw. Read your writes, see at least what you saw before, do not go backwards relative to your own history: these quantify over one client's causal past and nothing else, and they can be enforced by each replica unilaterally from data it already holds. Nothing converges, because nothing had to. Compare an invariant that quantifies over operations issued by other people, at other sites, that this node has not heard of: at most one holder of the lock, exactly one leader, the balance never negative, this username claimed once. There is no local information sufficient to establish those, and the only thing that establishes them is the participants settling on a single answer. That set is what Lamport's noun means. Mutual exclusion, reconfiguration and leader handoff are all in it; the read that only has to not regress is not, and never was, whatever the infrastructure charged it.

So the red flag survives intact once you read it as a claim about agreement rather than about ordering. Dependency tracking in the cheap rung is not a second consensus protocol, because at no point do two nodes have to come out the same way, and a mechanism where no node ever has to match another cannot be an informal agreement mechanism hiding in the design. That gives a sharp boundary a reader can check on their own system rather than a disposition: the moment the cheap path needs a tiebreak whose result must be identical everywhere, it has stopped being dependency tracking and has become exactly the unproved second protocol Lamport is pointing at, and it belongs in the log. Liskov's middle rung, agreement on a common order within a designated subset, is on the wrong side of that boundary and is where the two lessons actually collide. The resolution there is to keep the subset and drop the second mechanism: the subset enters the one log and the operations outside it simply do not, which costs the subset exactly what Lamport says it costs and leaves the ordinary traffic where Liskov wants it.

Each side is then guarding against the other's failure. Lamport's failure without the classification step is the one Liskov names in her first paragraph: never asking the question, letting the strictest operation price everything forever, and getting a system whose ordinary requests pay coordination cost to protect invariants that do not concern them. Liskov's failure without the red-flag rule is a system with three ordering mechanisms of increasing informality, where the interesting invariants are protected by the least-proved one because that is the one somebody extended under deadline. Note also that her own concession is a load-bearing part of this. The classification is a bet that the strong rung stays rare, and if the audit comes back saying most operations coordinate, you have paid for the vocabulary and inherited Lamport's architecture anyway, with per-operation bookkeeping on top. That is a real losing outcome, and it is knowable early: count the invariants that quantify over other callers before you build the ladder.

The classification act here is the same one demanded in [stall-or-compensate-under-partition](stall-or-compensate-under-partition.md), where the inventory of invariants is what tells you which operations can be answered during a partition. In both cases the design work is the same enumeration, done once, and the payoff is that it feeds two decisions instead of one.

**Strongest counter-argument:** the classification does not stay put, and Lamport's uniform order is the only thing that is safe against that. An operation is honestly declared causal in the first year because at that moment nothing depends on it globally. Three years later a feature ships that reads it and makes a decision the rest of the system must agree with, and the declaration is now wrong, silently, with no failure at the point of the mistake and no mechanism that flags it. Nobody re-audits a ladder of two hundred operations when adding the two-hundred-and-first. The uniform order is expensive precisely because it never asked, which is also why it cannot be invalidated by a requirement nobody thought to check. If Nathan holds that requirement drift is the dominant cost in long-lived services rather than coordination latency, the right default flips: put everything in the log, and take an operation out only with the same ceremony you would spend on relaxing a memory model, which in practice means almost never.
