# Whether the first artifact of a project is allowed to run

## The decision
On the first day of a project, somebody chooses what gets written and what kind of thing it is. Brooks says the answer is a system: an end-to-end assembly with all its seams present, doing nothing at all but doing it correctly, which then acquires function while never stopping. Abrial says the answer is a description no machine could evaluate, and that its inability to run is the property being purchased. These are instructions about the same first move, and only one of them can have the schedule.

## Brooks: the requirement does not exist until something exists to react to
[Nobody can state what they want before using something](../figures/brooks/lessons/keep-the-system-running-from-the-first-day.md) rests on a claim about people rather than about method. Someone who needs a system has never been asked the questions at the resolution a working artifact forces, and the thing they are being asked to imagine acts over time, which is the case imagination handles worst. So a complete statement of requirements produced in advance is not hard to get, it is unavailable, and any process that defers the first assembly until most of the construction is paid for discovers the unusable interaction, the unacceptable response time and the hostility to error after the money is gone. Growing function into a live skeleton buys three things at once: the design under discussion is a real object rather than a projection, the project can stop at any moment and still have shipped, and the people building it can see the thing exist. Chaitin says the same from the builder's side in [confidence in a large system's correctness is an empirical result](../figures/chaitin/lessons/treat-correctness-of-a-large-system-as-an-empirical-claim.md): the understanding required to write the plan is manufactured by writing the code, so a plan settled beforehand is a plan written by someone who did not yet know the problem.

## Abrial: executability is the thing that lets you stop reasoning
[Begin at a level of description you cannot run](../figures/abrial/lessons/start-above-executability-and-descend-along-two-axes.md) is not a preference for austerity. Quantified invariants over sets and relations, with nondeterministic transitions, are chosen because they are strictly more expressive than anything a runtime evaluates, and because they close off an escape route. As long as a description can be executed, a team under pressure will settle its doubts by executing it, and the early representation decisions will get made on behalf of a machine that has no business being in the conversation yet. Remove the option and the only remaining way to gain confidence is to understand the description, which is the discipline the whole descent then depends on. Nothing here says the system never runs; it says the layer where all the thinking about what the system must do happens is a layer where implementability is forbidden as a consideration, and the mechanical layer below it is where a processor first appears.

## Resolution
**LLM DECISION — Nathan may overturn.**

Run both, because they answer different questions, and when only one can be funded the deciding question is where the properties that would kill you come from.

The convergence is larger than the titles suggest and it is worth naming first, because it locates the residue. Look at what Brooks says the staged process finds too late: interaction nobody can use, performance nobody will accept, behaviour that punishes a user for a slip. Not one of those is a conformance question. Every one of them is a question about whether the thing being built is the thing anybody wanted, which is the class Abrial says no proof can reach and which he handles by animating the abstract model, deliberately outside the proof loop, at the earliest and cheapest moment, so that people can watch behaviour and recognise it ([a proof establishes internal consistency and nothing about whether you wanted this system](../figures/abrial/lessons/proof-cannot-tell-you-that-you-wanted-this.md)). Brooks's skeleton and Abrial's animation are the same instrument aimed at the same gap. Neither man offers the running thing as evidence that an invariant holds.

So the real disagreement is narrower: it is about whether the artifact that carries intent-checking is permitted to be the artifact that carries the design. Brooks fuses them, and the fusion is what buys him the option to ship at any moment. Abrial splits them, and the split is what protects the reasoning from the debugger. Holding both means paying for two artifacts, an abstract model and a live skeleton, which most projects will not do, so the choice is usually forced.

Force it on this: whether the invariants that matter originate outside the software. Rail interlocking has a rulebook older than the computer, written by people who can sign it, describing a world that will not negotiate; no amount of user trial produces the property that two trains must not occupy one block, and the one experiment that would test it is not available. There the understanding needed to write the plan already exists in the domain, Chaitin's objection does not bite, and starting above executability costs nothing you could have had. A system whose requirement is a preference nobody has formed yet sits on the other side of the line. There the specification genuinely has the status of a hypothesis, the artifact is the instrument that generates the requirement, and a non-executable model written first is a precise description of a guess.

The line also predicts the failure mode of each side used wrongly. Brooks's method applied to a safety kernel produces a system that has been alive at every stage and that nobody can argue about, with the invariants discovered by the incident. Abrial's method applied to an interactive product produces a beautifully coherent model of a system the customer will not use, at which point the front-loaded cost was spent on the wrong target. Jones's [spend the dividend on safety, not ambition](../figures/jones/lessons/spend-the-dividend-of-rigour-on-safety-not-ambition.md) is the same allocation rule stated from the other direction, and Brooks's own conclusion elsewhere in the corpus, that rigour pays best on sharply bounded kernels inside a system whose requirements are still moving, is close to an endorsement of exactly this split.

**Strongest counter-argument:** the line I drew may be an artifact of which industries have bothered. Abrial's answer would be that rail has a signed rulebook because somebody sat down and wrote one, that the doubly-classified requirements document is a producible thing in far more domains than the field assumes, and that Brooks's premise is a description of an industry that has never seriously tried to state intent before building. Worse, the two-artifact arrangement I recommend is unstable in exactly the direction Abrial warns about. Once a skeleton runs, the abstract model stops being consulted, because answering a question by poking the running thing is faster than answering it by understanding, and the discipline degrades within weeks. If that is right, then the option to run is not neutral, funding both is not actually available, and Abrial's instruction is unconditional for the reason he gives rather than conditional on where the invariants come from.
