# What establishes that a system too large to hold in one head is correct

## The decision
You have built something whose behaviour carries more content than any statement a person can hold at once. Somebody has to decide what would count as grounds for believing it correct, and the answer determines where the project spends its assurance budget. Dijkstra and Chaitin both answer with an absolute, and the absolutes point in opposite directions: one says confidence can only come from structure and never from observed runs, the other says knowledge about a large system's behaviour comes from watching it behave.

## Dijkstra: structure carries the argument, runs carry nothing
[Confidence in a program can only come from its structure, never from sampling its behavior](../figures/dijkstra/lessons/correctness-comes-from-structure-not-testing.md) rests on an argument that is hard to dent. In a discrete artifact there is no metric under which nearby inputs behave alike, so a passing run licenses a claim about that run and about nothing adjacent to it. Interpolating between passing tests is not weak evidence, it is not evidence. If the black box cannot be validated by probing, the only remaining move is to stop treating it as a black box: arrange the thing so its internal structure carries the argument, keep state spaces small enough that legal states can be written down, and grow the argument with the code rather than reconstructing it afterward. Dijkstra pairs this with [design down to the limits of your head](../figures/dijkstra/lessons/program-within-the-limits-of-your-head.md), which is what makes the position an engineering programme rather than a counsel of despair: when the content exceeds what you can hold, decompose further, and if you cannot decompose further, you have learned something about what you should be building.

## Chaitin: past a certain size the alternative to assuming is not proving, it is not knowing
[Confidence in a large system's correctness is an empirical result](../figures/chaitin/lessons/treat-correctness-of-a-large-system-as-an-empirical-claim.md) is not an anti-formalist reflex from someone who never did the work. It is a theorem-shaped argument from a man who spent a career proving things about programs. Establishing a property requires assumptions carrying at least as much content as the property. A specification a person can hold does not carry enough content to determine a system whose behaviour carries far more, so the specification cannot be the thing that settles the question. What remains is observation: run the new system beside the old one until the people who depend on it believe it, compile the compiler with itself, use the thing all day so that feedback about it arrives continuously. And Chaitin refuses the lazy version of his own position. The other half of his practice is driving out ad hoc code and forcing everything back onto clean systematic algorithms, precisely so the artifact's content shrinks toward what continuous use can actually cover. He is running Dijkstra's restriction discipline; he simply denies that it ever reaches the point where structure alone certifies.

## Resolution
**LLM DECISION — Nathan may overturn.**

The two absolutes are about different propositions, and once the propositions are separated both survive intact. Dijkstra's claim is about conformance: given a stated property, sampling cannot establish that the mechanism has it. Chaitin's claim is about content: no property small enough to state pins down a large system's behaviour. Dijkstra's argument presupposes the specification as given and asks how you discharge it. Chaitin's argument attacks the presupposition. They are stacked, not opposed.

The conservation law does not defeat structural proof, and this is the load-bearing step. Content conservation prices the total content of an argument, not the content any one person holds at one moment. A layered verification pays the full price in installments: each level is proved against the level below, each installment is small enough to hold, and the sum is as large as the system requires. That is exactly what Dijkstra means by abstraction as the only mechanism that keeps effort near linear in program length. Chaitin's inference from "no holdable spec determines the system" to "proof is hopeless" quietly identifies holdable-at-once with carried-by-the-argument, and those are not the same quantity.

What the conservation law does defeat is the pretence that discharging the stack tells you the top of the stack was worth discharging. The statement that the system does what the people who need it wanted is not reducible by decomposition, because it is a claim about the fit between an artifact and a world, and nothing internal to the artifact bears on it. Abrial concedes exactly this from inside the formal camp in [a proof establishes internal consistency and nothing about whether you wanted this system](../figures/abrial/lessons/proof-cannot-tell-you-that-you-wanted-this.md), and Jones concedes it in [spend the dividend of rigour on safety, not ambition](../figures/jones/lessons/spend-the-dividend-of-rigour-on-safety-not-ambition.md). At that layer Chaitin's instruction is the only one anybody has: put the thing in front of people and in front of the load, and watch. Running the new system beside the old is not a sample of the input space, it is the only available measurement of whether the specification stack was aimed at the right target.

So the rule a practitioner should carry: behavioural evidence is worthless as conformance evidence and is the sole evidence at the intent layer, and confusing the two is how projects end up with a heavily exercised system nobody can argue about and a heavily argued system nobody wanted. Chaitin's [buy assumptions deliberately and stay willing to retract](../figures/chaitin/lessons/buy-assumptions-deliberately-and-be-willing-to-retract.md) is what to do about the residue where you genuinely cannot decompose further and are building it anyway: write down what you assumed, what it buys, and what observation would withdraw it. That is a better answer than pretending the structure covers it, and it is not in conflict with Dijkstra, who never claimed you should ship what you cannot argue about.

**Strongest counter-argument:** the layer split may be drawn in the wrong place, because Chaitin's central example is conformance evidence and not intent evidence at all. A compiler compiling itself is not a sample drawn from an input space. It is one input carrying more content than any test suite a team would write, exercising nearly every path in the artifact under an oracle that is the artifact itself. Dijkstra's no-continuity argument is an argument against generalizing from sparse low-content probes, and it does not reach a single input whose content is comparable to the program's own. If behavioural evidence can be high-content rather than sampled, then the honest reading is that Chaitin found a third thing, neither proof nor sampling, that certifies conformance in exactly the regime where Dijkstra says nothing can. Bootstrapping, dogfooding, and running production traffic through a shadow deployment would then belong on the conformance side of the line, Dijkstra's "only" would be false rather than merely scoped, and this resolution would be preserving his absolute by narrowing it until it stops covering the interesting case.
