# When a subsystem runs to tens of thousands of lines, is that a measurement of the problem or of the attempt

## The decision
A subsystem you depend on takes forty thousand lines in every implementation anyone has shipped. You have to decide, before spending anything, what that number is evidence of, because the two available readings fund opposite work. If the number measures the problem, the money goes into organizing and confining what is there and into finding the people who can hold it. If the number measures the attempt, the money goes into building a small model to find out how much of it survives a change of formulation. You cannot fund both on the same subsystem, and the reading you pick determines which one you never try.

## Brooks: size counts distinct constructs, and much of the count was fixed by other people
[When the complexity is the subject, simplifying models lose the subject](../figures/brooks/lessons/complexity-that-is-the-subject-cannot-be-abstracted-away.md) makes two claims that have to be taken separately. The first is about counting: above the statement level a program has essentially no repeated parts, because anything genuinely repeated gets named and factored on sight, so growth in size is growth in the number of mutually different elements interacting non-linearly. A description that abstracts the differences away has thrown out the artifact. The second claim is harsher and does not depend on the first. A physicist digging into apparent mess proceeds on the confidence that some consistent order underlies it, and that confidence has been repeatedly rewarded. Software gets no such warrant, because much of what a system must accommodate was settled independently by separate groups of people for their own reasons at different times, and is mutually inconsistent for no deeper cause than that. Redesigning your own code does not remove that, since it never originated there. The prescription follows: stop waiting for the collapsing insight, separate the intricacy the world imposed from the intricacy you added, organize the first and delete the second. And read anyone who claims a new formalism dissolves the difficulty as making an empirical claim about the problem, not a claim about the formalism.

## Kay: nobody has ever measured the fraction, and the measurement is cheap
[Treat the size of an existing solution as an unanswered empirical question](../figures/kay/lessons/treat-the-size-of-the-current-solution-as-an-open-question.md) refuses the reading at the root. The line count records how the problem was attacked, by whom, with which tools, on what schedule, compounded over decades, and none of those terms is the problem. The tell that the real question has never been asked seriously is that plausible answers to it span several orders of magnitude, a spread nobody tolerates about a quantity they have actually investigated. What makes this more than a complaint is that Kay converts it into something checkable and then does the check: name a target size, build a small model, and find out whether the functionality fits. Failing to shrink is a result too, and load-bearing for the design. The mechanism underneath is the part that answers Brooks most directly. A whole-system budget cannot be met by local improvement, so no component is permitted its customary cost, and the only move left is to change the component's formulation rather than tighten its code. Absent a total, every component is negotiated alone, each one is individually defensible, and the system arrives at a size that nobody chose and nobody can be blamed for.

## Resolution
**LLM DECISION — Nathan may overturn.**

The disagreement dissolves once you notice that Brooks's inherent category is holding two different things, and that only one of them is a property of the problem. Split the bulk of a subsystem three ways rather than two. There is intricacy you added, which both figures say to delete. There is construct count demanded by the function itself, which is what Kay's small models actually measure and what Brooks's counting argument is about. And there is conformity: code that exists because some format, protocol, wire representation or API was fixed by people who are not in the room. Brooks folds the third into the first and calls the sum inherent. It is not. Conformity cost is inherent relative to a boundary, and the boundary is a choice made by whoever scoped the system, which makes it the same kind of thing as the intricacy you added, just at a larger radius. Brooks already licenses deleting what you added yourself. Kay draws the circle around yourself wide enough to include the boundary.

That gives a number you can estimate before funding anything: what fraction of this subsystem exists to satisfy something you did not choose and cannot change. Where the fraction is low, the size is genuinely an open question and Kay's method is fundable. A language runtime, a graphics pipeline, a compiler for a notation you control, a UI framework, a document model: these are large mostly because of accumulated formulation decisions, every one of which is yours to revisit, and the historical record of order-of-magnitude collapses in exactly these areas is what makes the bet reasonable rather than romantic. Where the fraction is high, Brooks is right for his own stated reason and the reason is not pessimism. A tax engine, a standards-conformant browser, an EDI integration, a device driver against silicon errata: the intricacy is other people's decisions, it is mutually inconsistent by construction, and worse, it changes under you on their schedule, so even a successful collapse decays. Organizing, layering and confining is not a consolation prize there, it is the correct response to complexity that has an external owner and a revision cycle.

Kay's budget mechanism also escapes the objection Brooks would otherwise raise from his own [method raises the floor of design and never the ceiling](../figures/brooks/lessons/method-raises-the-floor-of-design-not-the-ceiling.md). That lesson concedes the ceiling exists, that the strongest designers do deliver the same function from a smaller basis, and denies only that a method can transfer the capability. A size budget is not a method in that sense. It transfers no knowledge and teaches nobody anything. It removes an option, namely the customary formulation, and leaves whoever is present to find another one or fail visibly. Constraints of that shape are not subject to Brooks's floor-and-ceiling argument at all, which is the sharpest thing Kay has to say back, and it is why the budget has to be fixed for the whole system before any component is negotiated. Fix it afterward and it becomes a target that each component argues its way out of.

The failure mode on each side is worth naming because it is what the fraction test protects against. Kay's method aimed at a high-conformity subsystem produces a small, beautiful implementation that cannot talk to anything anyone else built, and the shrinkage turns out to have been bought by dropping the cases that made it large. Brooks's advice aimed at a low-conformity subsystem funds the permanent organization of an accident, and the tell is that the layering keeps needing revision without the total ever moving.

**Strongest counter-argument:** the conformity fraction is not a property of the problem either, and treating it as one is the same mistake I just accused Brooks of, one level down. What is fixed by outsiders is the external format's intricacy. What is not fixed is the size of the code that absorbs it, and that size is a formulation decision like any other. Kay's own strongest results are precisely here: a network stack derived from the diagrams in the specification, parsing machinery generated from a grammar rather than hand-written, adapters produced by a meta-tool instead of typed out. If code written to conform to arbitrary external rules need not scale with the intricacy of those rules given the right meta-notation, then high-conformity subsystems are the best target for a size budget rather than the worst, and my test has the sign backwards on exactly the cases where the money is. Overturning this means holding that conformity is a compression problem rather than a construct-count problem, which would put Brooks's second claim in serious trouble and make the small-model bet fundable almost everywhere.
