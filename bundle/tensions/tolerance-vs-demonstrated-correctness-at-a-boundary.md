# What standard of acceptance a component boundary is entitled to demand

## The decision
A part arrives at an interface and you must decide what would license accepting it, which also decides where the sampling budget goes. Cox says the standard should be compliance to a stated specification within a stated tolerance, checked by an instrument you can run against the finished part, and that the field's refusal to settle for this is why it cannot buy anything from anybody. Abrial reports a project that deleted its module-level testing outright and moved the money upward to interrogate the requirements, on the grounds that sampling belongs only where no decision procedure exists. One boundary, two opposite verdicts about whether to spend a cent checking what crosses it.

## Cox: the grip on demonstrated correctness costs the field its instrumentation
[When a field stops moving, look for the good it refuses to trade away](../figures/cox/lessons/name-the-good-you-refuse-to-let-go-of.md) is not an argument that correctness is overrated. It is an argument about price. Wanting demonstrated correctness commits you to assembling systems only out of parts small enough and closed enough to be reasoned about completely, and that commitment is a ceiling on what can ever be built from work you did not do yourself. Cox's companion move is to supply the missing tool rather than merely complain: [a specification is an instrument you can run against a candidate](../figures/cox/lessons/a-specification-is-an-instrument-you-can-run.md) makes the acceptance predicate a first-class deliverable that ships with the interface, applies to a part made by any method in any language, survives the part being rewritten, and gives both sides of a trade a shared procedure for deciding what was delivered. The concession is deliberate and Cox states it plainly: the instrument reports conformance inside a tolerance and cannot report absence of defects, and treating that as disqualifying is the preference he is charging the field with.

## Abrial: do not sample where something stronger already applies
[Properties chosen before the artifact exists are evidence; properties chosen afterward are opinion](../figures/abrial/lessons/fix-the-properties-before-the-artifact-exists.md) attacks a step in testing that usually goes unbilled. Somebody decides which property to check and what the right answer is, after the program exists, usually having read it, frequently sourcing the expected answer from their own head. A pass then licenses one small claim and a failure is ambiguous, since the expectation is as likely to be the wrong thing as the code. Properties that constitute a model have no such step, because they were written as a statement of what the system is for before there was any artifact to be influenced by, and the obligations are generated from the model's structure rather than nominated by the person doing the checking. Once that machinery is in place, module-level sampling measures something already decided, so the honest allocation moves the budget to the one question the machinery cannot touch, which is whether the stated properties were the right ones. Dijkstra's [design down to the limits of your head](../figures/dijkstra/lessons/program-within-the-limits-of-your-head.md) is the same commitment as an engineering datum rather than a project report: the ceiling Cox calls a price is where Dijkstra says the discipline lives.

## Resolution
**LLM DECISION — Nathan may overturn.**

Apply Abrial's own rule and it produces Cox's answer at Cox's boundary, because the rule is conditional on something Cox's situation lacks. Sample where sampling is the only tool available, says Abrial, not where a decision procedure exists. At a boundary a part crosses because somebody else built it, in a language you did not choose, by a method you cannot inspect, no decision procedure exists on your side of the interface. The proof did not travel with the part. So the runnable gauge is not a weaker substitute for something better that was on offer; it is the only instrument in the room, and refusing it because it cannot certify absence leaves the consumer with nothing at all, which is the outcome Cox is describing.

The condition to state precisely is whether a discharged correctness argument crosses the boundary along with the artifact. Inside a refinement stack it does: the module exists because a linking predicate ties it to a model whose obligations were closed, and testing it re-asks a settled question. Across a supplier relationship it does not, and cannot be made to without the supplier adopting your method, which is exactly the demand Cox says has never once been met at scale. Notice that both figures move the budget the same direction, away from module internals and toward the interface. Abrial moves it up to the requirements document; Cox moves it out to the contract between producer and consumer. Neither one spends it where most organisations spend it.

What does not dissolve is which artifact is authoritative. Cox's gauge is the definition of the part, so tolerance-compliance is acceptance, full stop. Abrial's checking is never authoritative; the model's invariants are, and any running check is a channel to intent kept deliberately outside the proof loop. That difference is decided by consequence class rather than by taste. Where a failed part produces a bounded, repairable loss, Cox is right and the ceiling is the trap he names, because the systems you decline to build out of other people's work are a real cost paid every year in exchange for an assurance you were not going to get anyway. Where a failure is unrecoverable, the ceiling is not a trap, it is the boundary of what should be attempted, and the correct response to a part whose argument does not travel is to decline the part rather than to gauge it. That is Jones's allocation in [spend the dividend on safety, not ambition](../figures/jones/lessons/spend-the-dividend-of-rigour-on-safety-not-ambition.md), read as a rule about which parts you are allowed to buy.

This also settles the question the corpus deferred here from the executable-specification pairing. A finite, decisive-within-tolerance procedure can be the authoritative interface contract, on the far side of that line and only there.

**Strongest counter-argument:** tolerance may not be a coherent notion for a discrete artifact, in which case Cox's central metaphor imports a property of metal that software does not have. Blanchard's gauges work because the failure surface is continuous: a part a thousandth out of true is a thousandth worse, and passing the gauge really does bound how wrong the part can be. Dijkstra's no-continuity argument says a digital mechanism has no metric under which nearby inputs behave alike, so a part that passes every check in the instrument can be arbitrarily wrong on the next input, and "within tolerance" is a phrase with no referent. If that holds, the field's grip on demonstrated correctness is not value rigidity at all but accurate knowledge of a disanalogy Cox did not price, the gauge is a marketing device rather than a measurement, and the consequence-class line I drew is doing all the work while the instrument does none of it.
