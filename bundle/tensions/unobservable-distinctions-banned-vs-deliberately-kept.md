# Whether to delete a distinction today's operations cannot observe

## The decision
You are writing a model-oriented specification and there is no implementation yet. Its state space distinguishes two configurations that no sequence of the operations on the interface can tell apart. You can strip the structure until the distinction disappears, or you can leave it there knowing the current interface cannot see it. Jones gives both instructions, about the same artifact, at the same moment in its life.

## Jones on the ban side: the surplus is a defect, remove it
Three lessons treat the indistinguishability check as the thing that keeps model-oriented specification honest. [Model a type with exactly the distinctions its operations can observe, and no more](../figures/jones/lessons/build-the-model-your-operations-can-distinguish.md) states it as a mechanical procedure with a mechanical consequence: if the operations cannot separate two values, the structure carrying the difference has to go, because otherwise you have specified a representation while believing you specified behaviour. [Run the over-specification check before there is an implementation to hide it](../figures/jones/lessons/run-the-over-specification-check-before-there-is-an-implementation-to-hide-it.md) adds the economics. The defect is invisible to the first builder, who shapes his implementation like the description, and lands entirely on the second, who did nothing wrong; and the check is one of the few correctness checks that needs no consumers, so it is cheap exactly while nothing yet forces you to run it. In that lesson the many-to-many correspondence between description and implementation is listed among the repairs for having missed the window, alongside inserting an intermediate description or bolting reconstruction machinery onto the implementation and arguing it away later.

[Choose between describing a thing by a model and by its visible behaviour according to who is reading](../figures/jones/lessons/hidden-state-is-not-a-vice-in-a-description.md) leans on the ban hardest. Its whole defence of giving a component an inside is that the inside has been reduced to precisely what the outside requires. The ban is what makes having structure innocent. Remove the ban and the objection people raise against model-oriented description, that it commits to things nobody asked for, is simply correct.

## Jones on the keep side: a tight fit is a model that cannot grow
[Fit your model to the subject matter when the operation list is still open](../figures/jones/lessons/fit-the-model-to-the-subject-not-to-todays-operations.md) points at what the test is relative to and refuses to look away. Observability is defined by the operation set, so the test measures the model against a snapshot of an interface rather than against the thing being modelled. Follow it strictly and the demonstration is brutal: a queue whose dequeue operation does not hand back the removed value collapses to a counter, and dropping dequeue collapses it to a single Boolean. Both are correct answers to the question the test asks. Both are useless as a foundation for anything that acquires a second operation later. So when the operation list is open, which is the normal condition of anything with a future, fit the model to the essence of the subject and treat the resulting excess as a price you agreed to pay. The same relation-instead-of-function refinement that the other lesson files under damage control appears here as the escape route whose existence is what makes the trade legitimate rather than reckless.

## Resolution
**LLM DECISION — Nathan may overturn.**

The ban stands, but the three lessons that state it quantify it over the wrong set: run the test against the operations the subject matter admits, not against the operations the interface currently lists.

The two worked examples separate cleanly under that reading. A set modelled as a sequence carries an ordering, and there is no operation you could ever add to a set that observes it, because order is not a fact about sets at all. It is residue from the notation reached for. That distinction goes, open list or closed, and no appeal to future extensibility can rescue it. A queue modelled as a sequence but exposed through operations that never reveal the removed element carries the removed element, and that is a fact about queues. A peek, an audit trail, a replay, an undo: any of these separates the two configurations, and each is an operation a queue plausibly grows. The current interface's silence about the removed element is information about the interface, not about queues. Stripping the queue to a counter is not removing residue, it is deciding that queues are counters.

So the question that decides which side you are on is whether some operation belonging to the subject could separate the two configurations, not whether some operation presently on the interface does. If none could, delete it, and this is the unconditional ban that build-the-model and run-the-over-specification-check are right to enforce without exception. If one could but none currently does, you are on fit-the-model's ground and the decision turns on whether the operation list is contractually closed.

One consequence needs saying because hidden-state gets it wrong as written. Having an inside costs nothing only under the closed reading. Once you knowingly keep subject-matter structure the current operations cannot see, the inside costs a real obligation: the retrieve function becomes a relation, adequacy is surrendered, and every argument that used to be a substitution becomes an argument about a relation. The mechanics are identical whether you arrive there by choice or by having missed the window, which is why the same device shows up as a price in one lesson and as a repair in the other. The difference is whether you took the trade or the trade took you.

**Strongest counter-argument:** the whole value of the check, as run-the-over-specification-check argues explicitly, is that it can be run against the description alone with no judgment call and no consumers in existence. "Operations the subject matter admits" reintroduces exactly the judgment the check was invented to eliminate, and it is unfalsifiable in the direction that matters: any specifier attached to his structure can name a hypothetical future operation that would observe it, and nothing in the rule can contradict him. If that abuse is common, the better answer is to keep the test literal and unconditional and move the exception into the open. Run the mechanical check, list every distinction it flags, and for each one either delete it or write down in the specification which anticipated operation justifies keeping it and that the refinement will therefore be relational. That preserves a test with no discretion in it and puts the discretion somewhere a reviewer can attack. The cost is that the specification now carries a paragraph of anticipated-operations prose, which is itself a commitment nobody validated.
